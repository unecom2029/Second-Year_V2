LO 1

Clinical Reasoning Lens

Apply the 5 Pathophysiological Models to a Case

The lecture case is a 67-year-old man with an acute right ankle sprain, COPD, worsening dyspnea/orthopnea/edema, diastolic heart failure, smoking, alcohol use, and caregiver stress. The point is not "OMM for ankle pain only." The point is to ask how somatic dysfunction may be blocking respiration, circulation, neurologic regulation, energy efficiency, and behavior change.

Core principle

OMT is framed as helping the patient express self-healing and self-regulation. In this case, the ankle sprain matters because it changes gait, raises energy cost, increases nociceptive drive, and may worsen a cardiopulmonary patient who has little reserve.

Respiratory/Circulatory

Goal: optimize arterial, venous, and lymphatic exchange. Think diaphragms, pressure gradients, thoracic inlet, rib motion, lymph return, and tissue congestion.

  • COPD flattens the diaphragm -> less excursion -> weaker low-pressure circulatory pump.
  • JVD, hepatojugular reflux, edema, orthopnea, and crackles make fluid return a major target.
  • Treat junctions/diaphragms: OA, thoracic inlet, TLJ/respiratory diaphragm, pelvis/sacrum, ribs.

Biomechanical

Goal: restore useful motion, posture, and anatomic reliability so tissues and organs can work with less mechanical drag.

  • Right posterior fibular head + internally rotated navicular/cuboid -> altered gait.
  • Limping can meaningfully increase metabolic demand, which is not trivial in COPD/HF.
  • Mechanotransduction: mechanical stress can alter cellular behavior, not just joint position.

Neurologic

Goal: reduce facilitation/nociceptive drive and improve autonomic balance.

  • Heart/lung visceral input can drive viscerosomatic findings around upper thoracic segments.
  • OA matters because vagal/parasympathetic influence passes through the upper cervical region.
  • ATFL pain adds nociceptive input -> more tone, worse gait, more reflex load.

Metabolic/Energetic

Goal: conserve energy, decrease allostatic load, and remove somatic dysfunctions that force the body to spend extra effort.

  • A limp, tachypnea, tripoding, and poor diaphragm motion all cost energy.
  • OMT target choice should consider the patient's reserve, not just the painful body part.
  • For acute sprain: reduce swelling, improve motion, and decrease the energy cost of walking.

Behavioral

Goal: support coping, lifestyle change, function, and the social context that determines whether a plan is realistic.

  • Smoking and alcohol use are clinically relevant, not moral footnotes.
  • Caregiver role matters: if he decompensates, his wife loses support.
  • Home exercise, hydration, smoking cessation, safe mobility, and follow-up belong in the plan.

Q: In the COPD/HF ankle-sprain case, why might treating the ankle and pelvis be justified even if the most dangerous active problem is cardiopulmonary?

Answer

Because the ankle sprain and altered pelvic mechanics can create an antalgic gait, increase energy expenditure, raise nociceptive input, and worsen compensation in a patient with limited respiratory/circulatory reserve. That is biomechanical + metabolic + neurologic reasoning, not just local pain care.

LO 2

Practical Exam Flow

Perform Axial Spine, Ribs, Pelvis, and Sacrum Screening Exams

The rubric rewards complete regional screening plus accurate segmental diagnosis. Build one smooth patient-flow: communicate, consent, wash hands, screen the whole region, pick the most restricted finding, do a 3-position diagnosis when required, treat, then recheck.

Rubric language to internalize

Communication, consent, and hand hygiene are not filler. The screening rubrics explicitly score lay explanation, consent before the exam and sensitive parts, and hand washing before technique. A cue for hand washing can remove credit, so make it automatic.

  1. Set the frame. "I am going to examine how your spine/ribs/pelvis move, looking for areas that do not move as well. Nothing should be painful. You can ask me to stop at any time. Is that okay?"
  2. Supine head/cervical. Diagnose OA, AA, lower cervical C2-C7, then rib 1 and thoracic inlet while the patient is still supine.
  3. Seated thoracic/lumbar. Segmentally screen the entire thoracic spine T1-T12 and lumbar spine L1-L5 with localized contact and force vectors.
  4. Ribs. Screen respiratory ribs by groups and mechanical ribs posteriorly, confirming anteriorly when appropriate and consented.
  5. Pelvis/sacrum efficiency. Do standing flexion, seated flexion, supine ASIS/pubic/ASIS compression, then prone PSIS/sacral base/ILA/spring/sphinx.
  6. Document and recheck. Objective gets OSE details; assessment gets "somatic dysfunction of [region]." After treatment, recheck tissue texture/ROM/pain.

OA Screening / Diagnosis

  • Contact occiput/mastoid region; stay above C1.
  • Use tiny translatory motion to induce OA sidebending; do not sidebend the whole neck.
  • Flexion: gently tuck/draw chin. Extension: lift finger pads toward the head.
  • OA sidebending and rotation are opposite: OA ESrRl.

AA Screening / Diagnosis

  • Contact posterior transverse processes of C1.
  • Flex head to about 45 degrees to lock out lower cervical motion.
  • AA only rotates; no flexion, extension, or sidebending diagnosis.
  • Name freedom: if it rotates better right -> AA Rr.

Lower Cervical C2-C7

  • Contact articular pillars, not sharp lateral transverse processes.
  • Screen all the way to C7 even if C3/C4 already feels restricted.
  • Translation left = right sidebending. Translation right = left sidebending.
  • Lower cervical sidebending and rotation are same side, but not true Freyette mechanics because of uncovertebral joints.

Thoracic / Lumbar

  • Static screen: posterior transverse process/asymmetry.
  • Dynamic screen: thumb just lateral to spinous process/facet; translate and sidebend segmentally.
  • Use only enough flexion/extension to move the target segment. Too much motion leaks into the pelvis/SI.
  • Pick the most restricted segment/group for 3-position diagnosis.

LO 3

Naming Logic

Use Freyette Type I and Type II Somatic Dysfunction

For thoracic and lumbar spine, Freyette mechanics help you convert palpation findings into a named diagnosis. The diagnosis names the position of ease/freedom, not the direction that is restricted.

FeatureType IType II
PositionNeutralFlexed or extended
SegmentsUsually a group curve, often multiple segmentsUsually single segment
Sidebending/rotationOpposite directionsSame direction
ExampleT5-T8 NSrRlT4 ESRr or L1 FSRl
How to confirmAsymmetry persists similarly in flexion and extensionAsymmetry improves in either flexion or extension and worsens in the opposite position
Three-position diagnosis

Neutral: find the posterior transverse process or sidebending restriction. Flexion: see whether asymmetry improves. Extension: see whether asymmetry improves. If it improves in flexion, the segment is flexed; if it improves in extension, the segment is extended; if no meaningful change, consider neutral Type I.

Example 1

Right TP of L4 is posterior. It becomes more symmetric in flexion and worse in extension.

Name: L4 FSRr. It is flexed, sidebent right, rotated right.

Example 2

T5-T8 group has left TPs posterior with no change in flexion or extension.

Name: T5-T8 NSrRl. Type I: neutral, sidebent right, rotated left.

Example 3

C3 resists rotation left; it moves more freely right.

Name: C3 Rr and, for lower cervical coupling, usually sidebent right too.

Q: A single thoracic segment has a posterior right transverse process. The finding improves in extension and worsens in flexion. What is the diagnosis?

Answer

T# ESRr. Improved in extension means the segment is extended. For Type II thoracic/lumbar dysfunction, sidebending and rotation are to the same side as the posterior TP/freedom: right.

Lecture pearl: when seated flexion conflicts with spring/sphinx, spring/sphinx are more specific for forward vs backward torsion.

Mechanical rib vs thoracic segment

A rib that feels posterior may simply be following a rotated thoracic vertebra through the costotransverse ligament. Check the connected vertebral segment first. True mechanical rib dysfunction is more likely with trauma and sharp, discrete "ice-pick" pain.

LO 5

Treatment Recipe

Explain Muscle Energy Technique Across Regions

Muscle energy is an active, direct technique: the physician positions the dysfunctional tissue at the restrictive barrier, the patient gently contracts against resistance, then the physician follows the new barrier and rechecks. The treatment rubric cares about hand placement, principle sequence, force magnitude, force direction, timing, recheck, and actual tissue change.

Universal MET sequence
  1. Confirm no contraindication and obtain consent.
  2. Localize the dysfunctional segment/joint with precise contacts.
  3. Move the region to the restrictive barrier in all planes you diagnosed.
  4. Ask for a gentle contraction, usually 10-20% effort for 3-5 seconds, while you resist isometrically.
  5. Patient fully relaxes. Wait for relaxation, then take up slack to the new barrier.
  6. Repeat 3-5 cycles, return to neutral, and recheck TART/ROM/pain.

Do not muscle-energy everything

Use caution or choose another modality when the patient cannot follow commands, has acute fracture/instability, severe pain, surgical precautions, infection, malignancy in the area, vascular compromise, or an acute tear where contraction would worsen injury.

Treatment rubric survival

Before finishing, say what changed and recheck it. The treatment rubric gives explicit weight to recheck and efficacy: tissue texture change, ROM improvement, and pain reduction matter more than performing a memorized choreography.

  • Translate OSE into regional somatic dysfunction assessment.
  • Plan diagnostics, therapeutics, OMT, education, and follow-up.
  • Delete unused template comments before submission.
Objective vs assessment wording

Objective/OSE: "L4 posterior TP on the right, better in flexion; L4 FSRr." Assessment: "Somatic dysfunction of the lumbar spine." Specific mechanics live in OSE; the assessment names the body region diagnosis.

LO 7

Clinical Writing

Write a SOAP Note with an OMT Procedure Note

A good OMT note proves medical necessity, records informed consent, identifies the treated regions and modalities, documents response, and gives aftercare. The procedure note should be specific enough to support the encounter but not cluttered with every normal finding.

SOAP partWhat to include for OMMExample language
SComplaint, function, pain behavior, goals, relevant ROS"Right ankle pain x3 days after inversion injury; worse with walking; associated swelling; denies fever/rest pain."
OVitals, focused MSK, relevant cardiopulmonary, and OSE by region"OSE: R posterior fibular head; R navicular/cuboid internally rotated; L rib 1 elevated; R rib 12 depressed."
AClinical diagnoses + somatic dysfunction regions"Right ankle sprain; COPD with worsening DOE; somatic dysfunction of head, cervical, thoracic, ribs, lumbar, pelvis, sacrum, LE."
PDiagnostics, therapeutics, OMT, education, follow-up"OMT today; RICE/ROM/HEP; smoking cessation counseling; continue current medications; follow-up 5-7 days or sooner if worsening."
Procedure note skeleton

Procedure: Osteopathic manipulative treatment. Indication: somatic dysfunction with TART findings in named regions. Consent: verbal consent after risks/benefits. Description: list only treated regions and only modalities used. Complications: none or describe. Disposition: tolerated well with increased ROM/decreased pain. Instructions: hydration/rest/HEP/return precautions.

Example OMT Procedure Note

Procedure: Osteopathic manipulative treatment (OMT).

Physician/Student: OMT performed by [Name], OMS2, supervised by Dr. [Name], DO.

Indication: Somatic dysfunction of head, cervical, thoracic, ribs, lumbar, pelvis, sacrum, and right lower extremity with restricted ROM, tissue texture change, asymmetry, and tenderness. No contraindications to OMT today.

Consent: Verbal consent obtained after discussion of risks, benefits, and alternatives. Patient agreed and could stop the treatment at any time.

Description: Head, cervical, thoracic, ribs, lumbar, pelvis, sacrum, and right lower extremity treated with muscle energy, soft tissue, articulatory, and lymphatic techniques as clinically indicated.

Complications/management: No complications.

Disposition: Patient tolerated the procedure well with improved rib excursion, improved ankle/foot motion, and decreased pain after treatment.

Post-procedure instructions: Encouraged hydration, rest, gentle ROM, and return precautions for worsening dyspnea, chest pain, fever, or worsening edema.

Q: Where do you document "L4 FSRr" versus "Somatic dysfunction of the lumbar spine"?

Answer

L4 FSRr belongs in the Objective/OSE section as the specific structural exam finding. "Somatic dysfunction of the lumbar spine" belongs in the Assessment as the diagnosable region.

Active Recall

Case-Based Practice Checks

Use these as rapid oral-practical prompts. Say the diagnosis out loud, then say how you would document it and what you would recheck after treatment.

Q: OA sidebends more freely right, improves in extension, and worsens in flexion. Name it.

Answer

OA ESrRl. OA sidebending and rotation are opposite.

Q: Right rib 11-12 move up on inhalation but do not return down fully on exhalation.

Answer

Right ribs 11-12 inhaled. They are stuck in inhalation/exhalation is restricted.

Q: Standing flexion positive right; right ASIS low, right pubic tubercle low, right PSIS high.

Answer

Anterior right innominate. Same-side ASIS/pubic tubercle inferior and PSIS superior fit anterior rotation.

Q: Left sacral sulcus deep, right ILA posterior/inferior, seated flexion positive right, spring negative and sphinx improves.

Answer

Most consistent with left on left sacral torsion: forward torsion pattern with negative spring/sphinx improvement and a left oblique axis.

High-Yield One-Pager

        

          
          
        

      

      

        
Screen -> Diagnose -> Treat -> Recheck -> Document. Organized by the seven learning objectives.

        

          

            
LO1 · 5 Pathophysiological Models

            
Lecture case: 67-year-old man, acute R ankle sprain + COPD/diastolic HF, dyspnea/orthopnea/edema, smoking, alcohol, caregiver stress. Ask how somatic dysfunction blocks respiration, circulation, neuro regulation, energy, and behavior — not just "OMM for the ankle."

            
    
              
  • Resp/Circ: optimize arterial/venous/lymphatic exchange — diaphragms, thoracic inlet, rib motion, pressure gradients.
    • 
              
  • Biomechanical: restore motion/posture so tissues work with less mechanical drag; mechanotransduction alters cell behavior, not just joint position.
    • 
              
  • Neurologic: reduce facilitation/nociceptive drive, improve autonomic balance (viscerosomatic reflexes, vagal input via OA).
    • 
              
  • Metabolic/Energetic: conserve energy, decrease allostatic load; target choice considers the patient's reserve, not just the painful part.
    • 
              
  • Behavioral: support coping/lifestyle/social context — smoking, alcohol, caregiver role, HEP, follow-up.
    • 
            

          

          

            
LO3 · Fryette Type I vs Type II

            
    
              
  • Diagnosis names the position of ease/freedom, not the restricted direction.
    • 
              
  • Type I: neutral, usually a group curve (multiple segments), sidebending/rotation opposite.
    • 
              
  • Type II: flexed or extended, usually a single segment, sidebending/rotation same direction.
    • 
              
  • 3-position dx: Neutral (find posterior TP/SB restriction) → Flexion (improves?) → Extension (improves?). Improves in flexion = flexed; improves in extension = extended; no change = neutral Type I.
    • 
            

          

          

            
LO4 · Naming Convention

            
    
              
  • Name so another clinician can reconstruct it: region, segment/side, positional diagnosis, relevant tissue findings.
    • 
              
  • Translate specific OSE into an assessment line, e.g. "Somatic dysfunction of the thoracic spine."
    • 
            

          

          

            
LO5 · Muscle Energy Technique

            
    
              
  • Active, direct technique: position the dysfunctional tissue at the restrictive barrier.
    • 
              
  • Patient gives a gentle contraction (~10-20% effort, 3-5 sec) against physician resistance.
    • 
              
  • Patient fully relaxes → physician takes up slack to the new barrier; repeat 3-5 cycles.
    • 
              
  • Return to neutral and recheck TART/ROM/pain.
    • 
              
  • Avoid/modify if: patient can't follow commands, acute fracture/instability, severe pain, surgical precautions, infection, malignancy in area, vascular compromise, or an acute tear contraction would worsen.
    • 
              
  • Rubric weighs hand placement, principle sequence, force magnitude/direction, timing, recheck, and actual tissue change — not memorized choreography.
    • 
            

          

          

            
LO6 · Student OMM Clinic Documentation Form

            
    
              
  • Header: student name(s), attending physician, clinic date, time of encounter, patient #.
    • 
              
  • Subjective: CC; narrative HPI (LOPQRST + associated symptoms) + ICEE (ideas/concerns/effects/expectations); PMH; surgical Hx; allergies; meds; family Hx; social Hx; ROS.
    • 
              
  • Objective: vitals + focused exam; MSK (AROM/PROM, strength, special tests); OSE by region (Head, Cervical, Thoracic, Lumbar, Sacrum, Pelvis, Ribs, UE, LE, Abdomen) — only regions with diagnosed SD; labs/X-rays if applicable.
    • 
              
  • Assessment: primary dx + DDx (secondary/tertiary as needed); "Somatic Dysfunction of: [regions]" line — only regions documented in OSE.
    • 
              
  • Plan: diagnostics, therapeutics, OMT procedure note, patient education, follow-up timeframe (or PRN).
    • 
              
  • Use it as a clinical document, not a worksheet: delete italicized instructional comments/unused prompts; customize ROS/exam systems to the actual patient for full credit.
    • 
            

          

          

            
LO7 · SOAP Note Elements

            
    
              
  • A good OMT note proves medical necessity, documents informed consent, identifies treated regions/modalities, records response, and gives aftercare.
    • 
              
  • Objective/OSE holds the mechanics (e.g. "L4 posterior TP right, better in flexion; L4 FSRr"); Assessment names the body-region diagnosis ("Somatic dysfunction of the lumbar spine").
    •

LO2 · Axial Spine & Rib Screening Detail

            

              
RegionContact / procedureDiagnosis logic

              
OAContact occiput/mastoid, stay above C1; tiny translatory motion for sidebending; flexion = tuck chin, extension = lift finger pads toward head.SB and rotation are opposite: e.g. OA ESrRl.

              
AAContact posterior TPs of C1; flex head ~45° to lock out lower cervical motion.AA only rotates (no F/E/SB dx); name the freer side, e.g. AA Rr.

              
Lower cervical C2-C7Contact articular pillars (not sharp lateral TPs); screen all the way to C7.Translation left = right SB (and vice versa); SB/rotation same side, but not true Fryette (uncovertebral joints).

              
Thoracic / lumbarStatic: posterior TP/asymmetry. Dynamic: thumb lateral to spinous process/facet, translate + sidebend segmentally; use only enough F/E to move the target segment.Pick the most restricted segment/group for 3-position diagnosis (see LO3).

              
Rib 1Posterior first rib near costotransverse area + anterior rib under clavicle.Pump-handle + bucket-handle motion; poor rise on inhalation but full exhale = exhaled.

              
Ribs 2-4Upper anterior ribs/pump-handle contact with consent.Assess inhalation/exhalation lag; respect breast tissue/comfort.

              
Ribs 5-10Lateral contacts toward axilla for bucket-handle motion.Compare sides during breathing.

              
Ribs 11-12Posterolateral caliper contact.Side that does not fully exhale = inhaled on that side.

              
Mechanical ribsPosterior screen; confirm anteriorly if appropriate.Check related thoracic vertebra first — a posterior TP can drag the rib posteriorly via costotransverse mechanics.
