Lecture framing: SBS mechanics act as a schematic language for documenting cranial somatic dysfunction. They simplify dynamic three-dimensional events into predictable axes, directions, and naming rules.

Review

Primary respiratory mechanism

Five anatomic and physiologic components function as one unit. The lecture separates motility, inherent self-propelled motion, from mobility, the capacity to be moved.

Brain and spinal cord motilityInherent motion of central nervous system tissues.
CSF fluctuationMovement of cerebrospinal fluid within the system.
Intracranial and intraspinal membrane mobilityThe reciprocal tension membrane transmits and accommodates motion.
Cranial bone articular mobilityMidline and paired bones participate in the three-dimensional shape change.
Involuntary sacral mobilityThe sacrum moves between the ilia through dural continuity.
Reciprocal tension membrane: falx and tentorium. The lecture links cranial mechanics to the sacrum through dural attachments.

Treatment principles

Balanced membranous tension

Treatment aim: address cranial somatic dysfunction, normalize physiologic function such as CSF and circulatory motion, and release membranous or articular strain.

BMT seeks the most neutral position available: the point where the membranes remain poised between normal free-range tension and the increased tension that precedes strain or fixation.

1

Exaggeration / indirect

Move toward the abnormal relationship to permit tissue recoil and release.

Lecture caution: avoid in acute trauma, headache, inflammation, or before mature sutural “gearing” around age 5–6.

2

Direct action

Retrace the dysfunctional position toward normal anatomy through the free range of motion.

The lecture favors this approach for acute trauma, infants, young children, and headaches.

3

Disengagement

Decompress opposing articular surfaces within physiologic limits before finding balance.

Useful near bevel changes or sutural interdigitations.

4

Opposing physiologic motion

Combine exaggeration in one component with direct action in another when permitted motion allows.

The lecture gives the occipitomastoid articulation as a classic “unlocking” example.

5

Molding

Use gradual direct action to normalize intraosseous warping, especially in infants.

This is a slow, progressive process.

Conceptual target

Neutral buoyancy

The operator unloads the strained system and finds the point with the greatest permitted freedom, then allows self-correction and self-maintenance.

Course notes, not an independent treatment protocol. Cranial technique selection, contraindications, and application require faculty-supervised training and patient-specific clinical judgment.

Engineering model

SBS mechanics

One axis

AP axis

Nasion → SBS → opisthion. Torsion uses this axis. Sidebending/rotation adds it for the rotational component.

Two axes

Vertical axes

One through the sphenoid body and one through the occipital foramen magnum.

Two axes

Transverse axes

Sphenoid through the body; occiput just above the jugular processes at SBS level.

AP axis from nasion through the SBS to opisthion.
Fast solver

Axis + direction = pattern

  • 2 transverse + opposite: flexion or extension.
  • 1 AP + opposite: torsion.
  • 2 vertical + opposite, plus AP same: sidebending/rotation.
  • 2 transverse + same: vertical strain.
  • 2 vertical + same: lateral strain.
  • No axis or excursion: compression.

Physiologic · 2 transverse axes

Flexion and extension

These patterns exaggerate normal PRM mechanics. They remain physiologic when the cranium can continue cycling, but treatment may be appropriate when the pattern restricts motion or accompanies symptoms.

FlexionSBS rises; short, wide head
ExtensionSBS falls; long, narrow head
Flexion vaultGreater wings move outward/anterior
Extension vaultGreater wings narrow/posterior
Transcript image: think “round head” for flexion and “tower head” for extension.
Motion rule

Opposite rotation

The sphenoid and occiput rotate in opposite directions around their separate transverse axes, which permits the normal opening/closing shape change.

Physiologic · 1 AP axis

Torsion

The sphenoid and occiput rotate in opposite directions. Name the pattern for the higher greater wing of the sphenoid.

Named sideHigh sphenoid wing
Same side occiputRelatively low
Opposite sideLow wing, high occiput
Common forcesBirth or asymmetric vertical force
Right torsion: right greater wing high and right occiput low. The left side mirrors this relationship.
Torsion: opposite rotation around a single AP axis.
Physiologic · 2 vertical + 1 AP

Sidebending/rotation

Opposite rotation around the vertical axes creates sidebending and a cranial convexity. Same-direction rotation around the AP axis accommodates the intact three-dimensional head.

NameSide of convexity
Convex sideFuller, wider, and relatively lower
Concave sideNarrower and relatively higher
Typical forceBirth force or blow at SBS level
Crack the egg and drop it: the side that opens is the convexity, then the whole pattern drops toward that side.
Sidebending/rotation combines vertical-axis sidebending with AP-axis rotation.
Nonphysiologic · 2 transverse axes

Vertical strain

Same-direction rotation produces a superior/inferior glide at the SBS. Name the dysfunction for the basisphenoid position.

Superior strainSphenoid relative flexion; basisphenoid superior
Superior feelGreater wings down; occiput high
Inferior strainSphenoid relative extension; basisphenoid inferior
Inferior feelGreater wings up; occiput low
Typical mechanism: midline trauma in the vertical plane to the front or back of the head, including birth forces or a fall on a hard surface.
Vertical strain: same rotation around parallel transverse axes creates a vertical glide.
Nonphysiologic · 2 vertical axes

Lateral strain

Same-direction rotation around the paired vertical axes produces a side-to-side shear. Name the dysfunction for the direction of the basisphenoid.

Left lateralBasisphenoid glides left
Right lateralBasisphenoid glides right
Greater wingOften anterior on named side

Palpation: feel the greater wings translate together toward the side named by the basisphenoid.
Lateral strain: parallel vertical axes with same-direction rotation.
A parallelogram-like contour can accompany lateral shear. Appearance remains suggestive, not definitive.
Nonphysiologic · no axis

Compression

Global external force compresses the cranial base and dampens normal flexion/extension. The lecturer described these as “tight,” “hard,” or “bowling-ball” heads.

MotionLittle to none
CRIDecreased amplitude / expression
RangeMild to severe
Typical forceBirth trauma or blow to front/back
Diagnostic contrast

Direction disappears

Other SBS patterns have a directional preference. Compression presents chiefly as loss of motion and increased density across the whole system.

Palpatory logic

Diagnosis as a sequence

1 · Quality

Is motion present?

Assess global compliance, amplitude, symmetry, and whether both sides participate in the three-dimensional cycle.

2 · Axis

What motion family?

Sort the preference into AP, transverse, vertical, a combination, or no meaningful axis.

3 · Relationship

Same or opposite?

Opposite rotations usually identify physiologic patterns. Same rotations identify vertical or lateral shears.

4 · Naming

Use the right landmark

Higher wing for torsion, convexity for SBR, and basisphenoid position for vertical or lateral strain.

5 · Context

Match the force

Birth, blunt trauma, falls, or global compression can support the mechanical diagnosis.

6 · Reassess

Confirm change

After treatment, reassess symmetry, quality, and range rather than relying on visual shape alone.

Physiologic does not mean asymptomatic. A physiologic pattern can still require treatment when it meaningfully restricts the PRM or accompanies clinical complaints.

Week 11 lab

Treatment techniques

Hands-on safety: use these notes as a lab memory aid under faculty supervision. Do not treat acute neurologic emergencies or skull trauma based on a study guide.

Venous sinus drainage

A gentle respiratory-circulatory technique intended to improve intracranial venous drainage through dural sinus and jugular pathways.

Prepare the outlet first: address thoracic outlet, cervical, and OA dysfunction before attempting cranial drainage.

Lecture sequence

External occipital protuberance

Begin at the confluence region.

Occipital sinus to condylar parts

Progress inferiorly along the posterior drainage route.

Condylar decompression

Address the jugular-foramen outlet.

Return to the EOP

Re-establish the central starting point.

Transverse and straight sinuses

Follow the next drainage segments.

Return to the EOP

Prepare for the final midline segment.

Sagittal sinus

Move from the sagittal suture toward the metopic suture.

Major intracranial venous sinuses and their drainage toward the internal jugular vein.
Course indications
  • Headache and congestive cranial patterns
  • “Tight” heads
  • Post-concussion or TBI care after appropriate evaluation
  • Selected sinus-related complaints
Contraindications
  • Patient refusal
  • Acute intracranial bleeding or neurologic instability
  • Acute skull fracture
  • Acute cerebrovascular accident

The transcript mentions modified use after stabilization in specialized neurologic cases. That does not override the acute contraindications above.

Frontal lift

The lecture highlights frontal headache, sinus complaints, eye or nasal symptoms, and prior frontal/supraorbital trauma as possible indications.

Position

Patient supine; physician seated at the head.

Contact

Interlace fingers. Thenar eminences contact the lateral frontal bone anterior to the coronal suture; hypothenar regions contact the lateral angles superior to the maxilla.

Disengage

Gently spring the lateral angles to disengage the articulation.

Lift and balance

Guide anterior-superiorly while maintaining balanced membranous tension.

Release and reassess

Wait for softening or improved symmetry, return gently to neutral, and reassess motion.

Frontal lift contact from the course deck.

Parietal lift

The lecture presents parietal lift for cranial congestion, headache, insomnia, hypertension, and “keyed-up” or overstimulated states. It also cites neonatal irritability as a teaching example.

Position

Patient supine; physician at the head.

Modified vault contact

Cross the thumbs over the vertex. Place index fingers anteriorly and the fourth/fifth fingers on the posterior parietals.

Midline disengagement

Use a gentle medial, resilient force at the thumbs.

Lateral lift

Lift the lateral parietal aspects superiorly within tissue tolerance.

Release and reassess

Wait for softening, return to neutral, and reassess motion.

Parietal lift contact and lateral lift direction.

OA rebalancing with BLT

This review applies balanced ligamentous principles to the occipitoatlantal articulation.

Chin tuck

Positioning reduces anterior convergence at the atlas facets.

C1 contact

Contact the posterior tubercle of C1 with a finger pad.

Balance side-slip

Find the position of permitted motion and balanced tension.

Respiratory cooperation

Use inhalation to support release and tissue relaxation.

Reassess

Return and check OA motion.

Posterior atlas and suboccipital anatomy relevant to OA contact.

Rapid review

Board pearls

Higher wing names torsion. The occiput sits relatively low on that same side.
Convexity names SBR. The convex side feels full, wide, and lower.
Basisphenoid names the shears. Superior/inferior for vertical strain, left/right for lateral strain.
Opposite rotations preserve physiologic motion. Same rotations around paired axes create nonphysiologic shear.
Compression has no axis. Expect reduced amplitude and a dense global feel.
Shape is a clue. Palpatory motion and the full clinical context carry more diagnostic weight.
Outlet before drainage. The lecture places thoracic outlet, cervical, and OA mobility before venous sinus work.
Direct action returns toward normal. The lecture distinguishes that from simply “pushing through a barrier.”

Self-test

Active recall

1List the five PRM components.
Brain/spinal cord motility, CSF fluctuation, intracranial/intraspinal membrane mobility, cranial bone articular mobility, and involuntary sacral mobility between the ilia.
2What is the BMT target?
The most neutral point where membranes are poised between normal free-range tension and the increased tension preceding strain or fixation.
3Which BMT method first decompresses opposing surfaces?
Disengagement.
4Which axis runs nasion → SBS → opisthion?
The single AP axis.
5Which patterns use two transverse axes?
Flexion/extension and superior/inferior vertical strains.
6How do you distinguish them mechanically?
Flexion/extension uses opposite-direction rotation. Vertical strain uses same-direction rotation.
7What names a torsion?
The side of the higher sphenoid greater wing.
8What names sidebending/rotation?
The side of cranial convexity.
9What names a vertical strain?
The superior or inferior position of the basisphenoid.
10What names a lateral strain?
The left or right direction of the basisphenoid glide.
11What does right torsion feel like?
Right sphenoid greater wing high with the right occiput relatively low; the left side has the opposite relationship.
12What does superior vertical strain feel like?
The greater wings feel down/caudad in relative sphenoid flexion while both sides of the occiput feel high.
13Which pattern suggests a parallelogram head shape?
Lateral strain.
14What is the defining palpatory feature of compression?
Little or no motion with a dense, globally tight cranial feel.
15What must be addressed before venous sinus drainage?
Thoracic outlet, cervical, and OA dysfunction that could impede drainage.
16Name four acute contraindications from the venous sinus slide.
Refusal, acute intracranial bleed or neurologic instability, acute skull fracture, and acute CVA.
17What is the key parietal-lift hand setup?
Modified vault contact with thumbs crossed over the vertex, index fingers anterior, and fourth/fifth fingers posterior.
18What respiratory phase assists the OA BLT release in this lecture?
Inhalation.
