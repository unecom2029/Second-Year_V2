SBS strain patterns

The sphenobasilar synchondrosis (SBS) is the cartilage joint between the body of the sphenoid and the basilar occiput — the reference point for naming every cranial strain pattern. A visual, exam-focused guide to its axes, naming rules, sphenoid–occiput motion, vault-hold findings, and observational clues.

Foundation

Primary respiratory mechanism

The primary respiratory mechanism (PRM) links central nervous system motility, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fluctuation, reciprocal tension membranes (the dural falx and tentorium), cranial bone mobility, and involuntary sacral motion.

Inhalation phase

Flexion pattern

  • SBS rises cephalad (toward the head).
  • Midline bones flex.
  • Paired bones externally rotate.
  • Biparietal width (side-to-side) increases as the anterior–posterior diameter decreases.
  • Sacrum counternutates (sacral base tips posteriorly).
Exhalation phase

Extension pattern

  • SBS falls caudad (toward the feet).
  • Midline bones extend.
  • Paired bones internally rotate.
  • Biparietal width decreases as the anterior–posterior diameter increases.
  • Sacrum nutates (sacral base tips anteriorly).
Core sorting rule: midline structures flex and extend around transverse axes. Bilaterally paired bones externally and internally rotate.

Orientation

Three axis families

One axis

Anterior–posterior (AP) axis

A front-to-back axis running nasion (root of the nose) → SBS → opisthion (back rim of the foramen magnum). Torsion and the rotational component of sidebending/rotation use this axis.

Two axes

Vertical axes

Top-to-bottom axes: one through the sphenoid body and one through the occipital foramen magnum. Used in lateral strain and in the sidebending component of sidebending/rotation.

Two axes

Transverse axes

Left-to-right (ear-to-ear) axes. Sphenoid: through the body at the base of the sella turcica (the saddle-shaped hollow holding the pituitary). Occiput: just above the jugular process, at SBS level.

Palpation setup

The vault hold

Every vault finding in this guide assumes the standard five-finger vault hold, with the fingers resting on fixed landmarks so the two ends of the SBS can be compared.

Finger placement
  • Index fingers — greater wings of the sphenoid (at the temples).
  • Middle fingers — just in front of the ears, on the zygomatic processes of the temporal bones.
  • Ring fingers — mastoid processes of the temporal bones.
  • Little (fifth) fingers — squamous portion of the occiput.
  • Thumbs — crossed, resting lightly above the head without pressing on it.
Why it matters

The index fingers read the sphenoid base and the little fingers read the occiput, so descriptions below such as “named-side fingers rise” or “wrists up, fingers down” only make sense against this hand position.

Used to diagnose cranial dysfunction, especially SBS base patterns (UNE COM OPP Manual, Vol. 2, p. 25).

Patterns 1–3

Physiologic strains

Physiologic strains of the sphenobasilar synchondrosis (SBS) are exaggerations of motions the cranium can normally make: the sphenoid and occiput still move in opposite directions around their shared axis (in sidebending/rotation, opposite around the vertical axes, with an added same-direction roll around the AP axis).

Exaggerated PRM phase

Flexion

Both bases move superiorly while the sphenoid and occiput rotate in opposite directions around parallel transverse axes. The vault becomes short and broad.

SBSRises cephalad
Paired bonesRelative external rotation
VaultAll fingers caudad and wider
Head shapeShort AP, wide transverse
Shadow hands: index and fifth fingers spread farther apart and move forward/caudad.

Extension

Patterns 4–6

Nonphysiologic strains

Nonphysiologic strains of the sphenobasilar synchondrosis (SBS) follow trauma. The sphenoid and occiput move in the same direction (vertical and lateral strain), or barely move at all (compression).

Two transverse axes

Vertical strain · superior / inferior

The sphenoid and occiput rotate in the same direction around their separate transverse axes. Name the strain for the position of the sphenoid base.

SuperiorSphenoid base superior; occipital base inferior
InferiorSphenoid base inferior; occipital base superior
Superior vaultWrists up, fingers point down
Inferior vaultWrists down, fingers point up
Mechanism: impact above or below the head, anterior or posterior to the SBS. A superior strain can follow a force on the posterior vertex or an upward force under the chin.

Bone motion · Conte deck

Vault-hold feel

Superior vertical strain: wrists rise, fingers drop
Inferior vertical strain: wrists drop, fingers rise
Two vertical axes

Lateral strain · left / right

The sphenoid and occiput rotate in the same direction around separate vertical axes. Name the strain for the more anterior sphenoid base.

Right lateralRight sphenoid base is more anterior
Left lateralLeft sphenoid base is more anterior
VaultHands roll along forearm long axes
Head shapeParallelogram appearance
Mechanism: a shear just anterior or posterior to the SBS. These forces can occur during birth. In right lateral strain, the fifth fingers move right, thumbs move left, and the right hand sits slightly anterior.

Right lateral: right hand slightly anterior
No axis

Compression

The sphenoid and occiput are jammed together. Expect markedly reduced or absent motion rather than a directional preference.

MotionVery little to none
Vault feelRigid “block”
Typical forceAnterior, posterior, or circumferential compression
BirthMay follow significant compressive forces
Exam distinction: compression has no axis. Every other listed dysfunction has one or more axes.
Begin in neutral. Compression feels like loss of the expected excursion.

Clinical pattern recognition

Observational diagnosis

Visual observation is suggestive. Palpation is diagnostic. Head and facial contours also reflect heredity, labor and delivery, and later forces or trauma.

Torsion

Named side

The side of the superior sphenoid wing shows relative external rotation. The orbit sits higher, the ear protrudes, and the mandible may deviate toward that side.

Sidebending/rotation

Full vs flat

One side of the face appears full or convex while the opposite side appears flatter. Confirm the side with vault motion.

Lateral strain

Parallelogram

The face and cranial base may have a sheared, parallelogram-like contour rather than a simple wide/narrow pattern.

Structural influence: the sphenoid strongly influences the anterior cranium and most facial bones. The occiput influences the parietals, temporals, mandible, and sacrum through their articulations and dural relationships.

Exam shortcuts

Memory map

Transverse = up/down

Flexion/extension and vertical strains use two transverse axes. Opposite rotation gives flexion/extension; same rotation gives a vertical strain.

Vertical = front/back

Lateral strain uses two vertical axes. Sidebending/rotation adds an AP axis to its two vertical axes.

AP = twist

Torsion uses one AP axis. Sidebending/rotation also uses that AP axis for its rotational component.

Name the sphenoid clue

Torsion: higher wing. Vertical: superior or inferior sphenoid base. Lateral: more anterior sphenoid base. Sidebending/rotation: convex side.

Same = nonphysiologic

Same-direction motion around paired axes points to vertical or lateral strain. Flexion/extension and torsion use opposite directions.

No axis = compression

A rigid, low-amplitude vault with no clear directional pattern supports compression.

Self-test

Active recall

1Which axis runs from nasion through SBS to opisthion?
The single anterior-posterior axis.
2What names a torsion?
The side of the higher sphenoid greater wing, which is also usually the more superior sphenoid base.
3In right torsion, what happens to the right bases?
The right sphenoid base moves superiorly while the right occipital base moves inferiorly.
4How does flexion feel in the vault hold?
All fingers move caudad and farther apart, with widening of the vault.
5Which pattern uses two vertical axes plus one AP axis?
Sidebending/rotation.
6What names a sidebending/rotation dysfunction?
The side of apparent convexity, where the fingers spread farther apart and move more caudad.
7What distinguishes vertical strain from flexion/extension?
Both use two transverse axes. In vertical strain the sphenoid and occiput rotate in the same direction; in flexion/extension they rotate in opposite directions.
8What do the hands do in a superior vertical strain?
The wrists move superiorly while all fingers point or move inferiorly.
9How are lateral strains named?
For the more anterior sphenoid base.
10What head-shape clue suggests lateral strain?
A parallelogram-like contour.
11Which strain has no axis of motion?
SBS compression.
12Which carries more diagnostic weight: appearance or palpation?
Palpation. Visual observation is suggestive.

Reference

Glossary

TermMeaning
SBS — sphenobasilar synchondrosisThe cartilage joint (synchondrosis) between the body of the sphenoid and the basilar part of the occiput. Every cranial strain pattern is named from the position of the sphenoid base here.
PRM — primary respiratory mechanismThe five-part unit — central nervous system motility, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fluctuation, reciprocal tension membranes, cranial bone mobility, involuntary sacral motion — that produces the slow inherent motion of the cranium.
RTM — reciprocal tension membraneThe dural membrane system (falx cerebri, tentorium cerebelli) whose balanced tension guides and links cranial bone motion.
AP axis — anterior–posterior axisFront-to-back axis running nasion → SBS → opisthion. Used by torsion and by the rotational component of sidebending/rotation.
Transverse axisLeft-to-right (ear-to-ear) axis. Two parallel transverse axes are used by flexion/extension and by vertical strain.
Vertical axisTop-to-bottom axis. Two parallel vertical axes are used by lateral strain and by the sidebending component of sidebending/rotation.
Sidebending/rotation (SBR)The physiologic strain combining sidebending around two vertical axes with rotation around the AP axis; named for the side of convexity.
Flexion / extensionHere, exaggerated versions of the two normal PRM phases — not the neck movements of the same name.
NasionMidline bony point at the root of the nose, where the frontal and nasal bones meet.
OpisthionMidline point on the posterior rim of the foramen magnum.
Sella turcica“Turkish saddle” — the saddle-shaped hollow on the sphenoid body that holds the pituitary gland; the sphenoid transverse axis passes through here.
Jugular processLateral projection of the occiput; the occipital transverse axis runs just above it, at SBS level.
Greater wing of the sphenoidLateral projection of the sphenoid felt at the temple; the index fingers rest here in the vault hold.
Cephalad / caudadToward the head / toward the feet.
Nutation / counternutationSacral base tipping anteriorly (nutation, pairs with cranial extension) or posteriorly (counternutation, pairs with cranial flexion).
Biparietal diameterSide-to-side width of the skull between the parietal bones; widens in flexion / external rotation, narrows in extension / internal rotation.
