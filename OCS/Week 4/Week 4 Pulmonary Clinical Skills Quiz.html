Week 4 Pulmonary - High-Yield One-Pager

1. Normal breath sounds by location

  • Vesicular: soft, low-pitched, inspiration longer than expiration, no gap - expected over most peripheral lung fields; this is the default sound.
  • Bronchovesicular: intermediate pitch/intensity, inspiration about equal to expiration - expected at the 1st-2nd interspaces anteriorly and between the scapulae posteriorly, over the mainstem bronchi.
  • Bronchial (tubular): loud, high-pitched, expiration longer than inspiration with a brief pause - expected only over the manubrium/trachea and large central airways.
  • Tracheal: loudest and harshest of all - normal only directly over the trachea.
  • Rule of thumb: sound should soften as you move from central airways toward the periphery; anywhere that sounds "too loud/tubular" for its location is a clue, not a variant.

2. Sounds in the wrong place

  • Bronchial or bronchovesicular quality heard over peripheral lung, where vesicular is expected, means abnormal sound transmission through solidified lung - think pneumonia, lobar consolidation, or tumor-infiltrated tissue.
  • Mechanism: air-filled alveoli normally filter out higher frequencies; fluid- or cell-filled (consolidated) alveoli conduct central-airway sound more efficiently out to the chest wall.
  • Vesicular sounds that are diminished, distant, or absent where they should be present mean something is blocking transmission - pleural air (pneumothorax), pleural fluid (effusion), or airway obstruction (mucus plug, mass, foreign body).
  • Always compare the same level side-to-side; asymmetry, not the sound in isolation, is what flags pathology.
  • Absent breath sounds plus unilateral hyperresonance plus trachea deviated away from that side = tension pneumothorax until proven otherwise.

3. Crackles (rales): fine vs coarse

  • Discontinuous popping sounds from collapsed airways/alveoli snapping open, or fluid/secretions bubbling.
  • Fine crackles: high-pitched, brief, late-inspiratory, "Velcro"-like - classic for pulmonary fibrosis/interstitial lung disease and early or mild CHF pulmonary edema.
  • Coarse crackles: lower-pitched, longer, louder, early inspiratory or biphasic - classic for pneumonia, bronchiectasis, and heavy secretions.
  • Crackles that clear with cough usually reflect secretions/atelectasis; crackles that persist after cough favor fibrosis, consolidation, or true pulmonary edema.
  • CHF crackles are classically bilateral, symmetric, and dependent (posterior/basilar bases).

4. Wheezes, rhonchi & pleural rub

  • Wheezes: continuous, musical, high-pitched - turbulent airflow through narrowed airways; hallmark of asthma and COPD/bronchospasm.
  • Expiratory-only wheeze suggests mild-moderate obstruction; wheeze on both inspiration and expiration suggests more severe narrowing.
  • A "silent chest" in an asthma exacerbation (no wheeze, minimal air movement) is a red flag for impending respiratory failure, not reassurance.
  • Rhonchi: low-pitched, snoring/gurgling - from secretions or partial obstruction in larger airways (bronchitis, mucus plugging); often clears or shifts after coughing.
  • Focal rhonchi that persist after cough should raise concern for an obstructing lesion or mass, not simple secretions.
  • Pleural rub: coarse, grating, leathery, heard on both inspiration and expiration - inflamed pleura (pleuritis, pneumonia near the pleura, PE); usually localized and painful with breathing.

5. Inspection

  • Look before you touch: respiratory rate/rhythm/depth/effort, symmetry of chest wall movement, accessory muscle use (SCM, scalenes), nasal flaring, retractions (supraclavicular, intercostal, subcostal).
  • Chest shape: barrel chest/increased AP diameter signals chronic air trapping (COPD/emphysema).
  • Color: central cyanosis (lips, tongue, mucosa) means significant hypoxemia; peripheral cyanosis can just reflect poor circulation.
  • Clubbing of the digits: chronic hypoxemia from lung cancer, bronchiectasis, pulmonary fibrosis, or cystic fibrosis.
  • Tripod positioning, inability to complete full sentences, or stridor signals impending respiratory failure - act before finishing the formal exam.

6. Palpation & percussion

  • Palpation: tracheal position at the sternal notch (should be midline), symmetric chest expansion (thumbs at the costal margin), tactile fremitus, focal tenderness.
  • Trachea deviates away from a tension pneumothorax (pushed by pressure); deviates toward volume loss such as atelectasis or lobar collapse.
  • Percussion: side-to-side ladder pattern comparing matched levels - never march diagonally down the back.
  • Resonant = normal aerated lung. Dull/flat = fluid or solid tissue replacing air (pneumonia, effusion, hemothorax, empyema, fibrosis, tumor).
  • Hyperresonant = excess air - diffuse in COPD/asthma, unilateral in pneumothorax or a large bulla. Tympanic is expected only over the gastric bubble.

7. Auscultation technique & tactile fremitus

  • Listen on bare skin, diaphragm of the stethoscope, patient breathing deeply through an open mouth, full respiratory cycle at each point, compare side-to-side.
  • Characterize every sound by pitch, intensity, timing (inspiratory/expiratory/both), location, and whether it changes with cough or position.
  • Tactile fremitus: palpate symmetric points with the ulnar edges/palms while the patient says "99" or "1-1-1" in a normal voice.
  • Increased fremitus = consolidation/pneumonia or tumor-infiltrated lung transmitting vibration well.
  • Decreased/absent fremitus = pleural effusion, pneumothorax, obstruction, or COPD hyperinflation - anything separating lung from chest wall or reducing airflow.

8. Voice sounds & excursion

  • Egophony: patient says "E"; normal is a muffled "E." Over consolidation the sound changes to a nasal "A" (E-to-A change) - same mechanism as increased fremitus.
  • Bronchophony: spoken "99" becomes louder and clearer over consolidation (normally muffled).
  • Whispered pectoriloquy: whispered "1-2-3" becomes loud and distinct over consolidation (normally inaudible/indistinct) - the most specific voice test for consolidation.
  • All three voice tests are decreased or absent over effusion or pneumothorax, since there is no aerated or solid lung to transmit sound.
  • Diaphragmatic excursion: percuss to find the diaphragm level at full exhale vs full inhale; normal is about 3-5 cm and should be symmetric. Decreased/asymmetric excursion occurs with COPD hyperinflation, large effusion, phrenic nerve paralysis, or pulmonary fibrosis.
  • Respiratory (chest wall) excursion: hands on the lower posterior ribs with thumbs meeting at midline - asymmetric separation localizes the side of disease (pneumonia, effusion, pneumothorax, fibrosis, bronchial obstruction).

9. Consolidation vs effusion vs pneumothorax

  • Pneumonia/consolidation, "solid lung transmits sound": dull to percussion, increased fremitus, bronchial breath sounds where vesicular is expected, crackles, positive egophony/bronchophony/whispered pectoriloquy.
  • Pleural effusion, "fluid blocks sound": dull-to-flat percussion, decreased fremitus, decreased/absent breath and voice sounds, decreased chest expansion on that side.
  • Pneumothorax, "air blocks sound and there is no lung to hear": hyperresonant percussion, decreased fremitus, decreased/absent breath and voice sounds; the tension variant shifts the trachea away from the affected side.
  • Quick discriminator: effusion and pneumothorax both look "quiet" (everything decreased) - tell them apart with percussion: dull/flat means fluid, hyperresonant means air.
  • Pneumonia is the "loud" pattern (increased transmission); effusion/pneumothorax are the "quiet" patterns (decreased transmission) - this pairing is the single most tested concept in this unit.

10. COPD, asthma & CHF patterns

  • COPD/emphysema: barrel chest, decreased expansion, diffuse hyperresonance, distant/decreased breath sounds, prolonged expiratory phase, decreased fremitus, reduced diaphragmatic excursion - chronic smoking history.
  • Asthma/obstruction: accessory muscle use, possible hyperresonance from air trapping, diffuse wheezing with prolonged expiration; a silent chest in a severe attack is a crisis, not improvement.
  • CHF/pulmonary edema: bilateral dependent (basilar) inspiratory crackles, percussion usually resonant unless a co-existing effusion is present, no egophony and no increased fremitus - this is not a consolidation pattern.
  • CHF vs pneumonia: CHF crackles are bilateral, symmetric, and basilar with a normal voice-sound exam; pneumonia crackles are typically more focal/unilateral with positive egophony and increased fremitus.

11. USPSTF: lung cancer screening & tobacco cessation

  • Lung cancer screening, Grade B: annual low-dose CT for adults age 50-80 with at least a 20 pack-year smoking history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years - the "50-80-20-15" rule.
  • Stop screening once the patient has not smoked for more than 15 years, or if a health problem substantially limits life expectancy or the ability/willingness to undergo curative lung surgery.
  • LDCT is a screening test for asymptomatic high-risk patients; a patient with hemoptysis, unexplained weight loss, or a worrisome focal finding needs diagnostic workup, not a screening order.
  • Tobacco use and cessation counseling, Grade A: ask every adult, including pregnant patients, about tobacco use at every visit.
  • For non-pregnant adults who use tobacco, offer both behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy; for pregnant patients, offer behavioral counseling (pharmacotherapy evidence is insufficient in pregnancy, as is evidence for e-cigarettes as a cessation tool).

12. USPSTF: COPD screening & vignette traps

  • COPD screening, Grade D: USPSTF recommends against screening asymptomatic adults for COPD - no routine spirometry just because someone has a smoking history but no symptoms.
  • This Grade D recommendation does not apply to patients who already have respiratory symptoms (cough, sputum, dyspnea, wheeze) - evaluate those patients rather than withholding testing.
  • It also does not apply to special high-risk groups, such as known alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency or significant occupational/environmental lung exposure.
  • Classic exam trap: an asymptomatic ex-smoker asking for a "lung function check" - correct answer is no spirometry screening, but do assess tobacco use and lung-cancer-screening eligibility.
  • Grade pattern to memorize: lung cancer screening is FOR (Grade B), tobacco cessation counseling is FOR everyone (Grade A), COPD screening in asymptomatic adults is AGAINST (Grade D).
