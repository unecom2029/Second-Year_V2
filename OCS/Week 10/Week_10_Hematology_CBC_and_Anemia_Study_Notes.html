Interpreting the CBC & Working Up Anemia

Lecture and lab combined. A complete blood count is one of the most commonly ordered tests in primary care and one of the least straightforward to read. These notes work through what each number means, how to sort anemia by red cell size, which markers confirm which deficiency, and how the reticulocyte production index tells you whether the bone marrow is actually responding.

Vocabulary, and the One Habit That Matters Most

Abbreviations used in this section — CBC complete blood count · Hgb hemoglobin · Hct hematocrit · PLT platelets · MCV mean corpuscular volume · RBC red blood cell · WBC white blood cell. Every section repeats its own abbreviation list, and the full glossary is at the bottom of the page.

The naming system: root + suffix

Most hematology vocabulary is one cell type plus one of three endings. -penia means too few, -osis or -philia means too many, and -cytosis/-cythemia means too many cells. Learn the pattern once and the words stop needing memorization.

🔑 The habit: read the result against the patient, not on its own

Dr. Brandt's framing, and the single most testable idea in this lecture: a laboratory value is interpreted in light of the presentation. The same number means two different things in two different patients, and it drives two different next steps.

  • Result does not match the clinical picture → consider that it may be spurious. Repeat the test. A 54-year-old with no complaints, here for a routine hypertension follow-up, whose white blood cell count comes back at 24,000 — repeat it before you do anything else.
  • Result matches the clinical picture, or the situation is acute → do not delay for a repeat. Act. That same 54-year-old with warmth, redness, and swelling of the right leg after an insect bite, with a white count of 24,000, has cellulitis. Treat.
🧠 Two lenses on anemia — kinetic and morphologic

You will hear both, and they answer different questions.

  • Kinetic — why is the count low? Three possibilities only: loss, destruction, or a production problem. This is the "where is the blood?" question.
  • Morphologic — what do the cells look like? Size (MCV), color (hemoglobin concentration), shape (peripheral smear). This is what the classification by MCV is built on.

The two lenses are complementary, and the reticulocyte count is what connects them: it converts "what do the cells look like" into "is the marrow producing or not."

🩺 "Blood does not just disappear"

When a hemoglobin is low, force yourself through the three-way split before ordering anything:

  • Loss — menses, gastrointestinal tract, trauma, surgery. Ask: acute or chronic? Characterize it.
  • Destruction — hemolysis. Check lactate dehydrogenase, bilirubin, and haptoglobin.
  • Production — a marrow problem. Check the reticulocyte count. Consider iron, vitamin B12, and folate deficiency as things that slow production down.

Q: An asymptomatic 54-year-old man has a white blood cell count of 24,000/mm³ on a routine screening panel. His examination is entirely normal. What is the next step, and what single feature of the case determines it?

Answer

Repeat the complete blood count with differential. The determining feature is that the result does not match the clinical picture — he has no symptoms, no fever, and a normal examination. When result and presentation disagree, confirm the result before acting on it. Had he presented with an inflamed leg after a bite, the same value would match the picture and you would treat rather than repeat.

LO 1 — CBC & Differential

Learning Objective 1

Components of the CBC and the White Cell Differential

Abbreviations used in this section — CBC complete blood count · WBC white blood cell · RBC red blood cell · Hgb hemoglobin · Hct hematocrit · RDW red cell distribution width · MCV mean corpuscular volume · MCH mean corpuscular hemoglobin · MCHC mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration · MPV mean platelet volume · PLT platelet.

A complete blood count reports indices for the three cell lines: white cells, red cells, and platelets. A CBC with differential adds the breakdown of the white cell count into its five subtypes. It is one of the most frequently ordered tests in medicine, and — as the lecture puts it — not always straightforward to interpret.

What is actually in the panel

01

White cell count

Number of white blood cells per microliter of blood, with or without the differential breakdown.

02

Red cell count

Number of red cells per unit volume. Note carefully: this does not accurately measure the body's ability to carry oxygen.

03

Hemoglobin

The amount of hemoglobin in the blood. This is the measure of oxygen-carrying ability.

04

Hematocrit

The percentage of blood volume occupied by red cells.

05

Calculated red cell indices

RDW, MCV, MCH, MCHC — derived, not directly measured. Detailed below.

06

Platelets

Platelet count, plus mean platelet volume (average platelet size).

⚠️ Red cell count ≠ oxygen-carrying capacity

This distinction is explicitly flagged in the lecture and is easy to gloss over. The red blood cell count tells you how many cells are present per unit volume. The hemoglobin tells you how much oxygen those cells can actually carry. A patient with thalassemia trait can have a red cell count above the reference range while being anemic, because each of those many cells carries too little hemoglobin.

🔽

Low MCHC — hypochromia

Two mechanisms produce pale cells:

  • Decreased red cell production — the classic example is iron deficiency anemia, where there is not enough iron to fill each cell with hemoglobin.
  • Reticulocytosis — reticulocytes are immature and carry a lower hemoglobin concentration than mature red cells, so a blood full of them reads as hypochromic.
🔼

High MCHC — artifact or real morphology

Often an artifact. Lipemia, hemolysis of the specimen, red cell agglutination, leukocytosis, and hyperbilirubinemia all falsely raise it.

But it can be real, reflecting a genuine red cell shape abnormality:

  • Spherocytosis — the cell loses membrane surface area, so the same hemoglobin is packed into a smaller sphere.
  • Hemoglobinopathies

The white cell differential, subtype by subtype

The differential splits the white count into neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils. 🩺 Iron deficiency raises the platelet count

Reactive thrombocytosis is one of the quiet confirmatory findings in iron deficiency anemia, and it shows up in two of the cases below. When you see microcytic anemia with a platelet count above the reference range, iron deficiency should move to the front of your differential. Mean platelet volume (average platelet size) is reported alongside the count.

Q: A patient's complete blood count reports a mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration of 38 g/dL (normal 32–36). Name the two broad explanations, and give one specific example of each.

Answer

(1) Artifact — lipemia, in-tube hemolysis, red cell agglutination, leukocytosis, or hyperbilirubinemia can all falsely elevate it. (2) Genuine red cell morphology abnormality — spherocytosis (membrane surface area is lost, so hemoglobin is packed into a smaller sphere) or a hemoglobinopathy. Because artifact is common, the practical move is to look at the specimen and the peripheral smear before chasing a diagnosis.

LO 2 — Anemia by MCV

Learning Objective 2

Classifying Anemia by Mean Corpuscular Volume

Abbreviations used in this section — MCV mean corpuscular volume · RDW red cell distribution width · ACD anemia of chronic disease (also written anemia of inflammation) · TIBC total iron-binding capacity · Tsat transferrin saturation · LDH lactate dehydrogenase · LFT liver function test · B12 vitamin B12 (cobalamin) · EtOH ethanol (alcohol) · GI gastrointestinal.

🔑 The three buckets

Mean corpuscular volume is the average size of the red cells. Normal is 80–100 fL (femtoliters).

  • Microcytic — MCV below 80
  • Normocytic — MCV 80 to 100
  • Macrocytic — MCV above 100

The lecture's caution: the MCV is a useful sorting tool but should not be the only tool. It is a mean, so a population with large variation in size can average into the normal range. When the numbers do not add up, order a peripheral smear and look at the other red cell indices.

Microcytic anemia — MCV below 80

Start the differential with three entities: iron deficiency anemia, thalassemia, and anemia of chronic disease (which can equally be normocytic). Less common causes include sideroblastic anemias, lead poisoning, zinc and copper deficiency, and certain drugs.

🧠 Workup of microcytic anemia — the branching logic

Assuming the patient is not acutely unwell and needing urgent care:

  1. Check ferritin firstAlso reasonable at the same visit: a reticulocyte count and a peripheral smear.
  2. Low ferritin → iron deficiency anemiaThe diagnosis is made. The work is now to find the cause of the iron loss.
  3. Normal or elevated ferritin → iron deficiency is unlikelyMove to the next question rather than treating with iron.
  4. Ferritin normal: is the microcytosis new or old?Dig for prior complete blood counts. Chronic microcytosis → think thalassemia → order a hemoglobin electrophoresis and get a hematology consult.
  5. Acquired microcytosis that is not iron deficiency → think anemia of chronic diseaseThen go looking for the underlying systemic disease driving it.

Source: Tefferi, Hanson & Inwards, Mayo Clin Proc 2005.

⚠️ Iron deficiency vs. anemia of chronic disease — the pattern to memorize

From Bakerman's. Both drop the serum iron and both drop the transferrin saturation, so those two do not separate them. The discriminators are the transferrin/TIBC and the ferritin, and they move in opposite directions.

MarkerIron deficiency anemiaAnemia of chronic disease
Serum ironDecreasedDecreased
Transferrin saturation (Tsat)DecreasedDecreased
Transferrin / TIBCIncreased — the liver makes more transferrin to scavenge ironDecreased or normal — inflammation suppresses transferrin synthesis
FerritinDecreased — stores are genuinely emptyNormal or increased — it is an acute phase reactant

Normocytic anemia — MCV 80 to 100

The broadest bucket, and often a mixed anemia — two processes at once whose opposite effects on cell size cancel out. The workup is therefore a systematic exclusion rather than a single branching tree.

  • Exclude the treatable causes first — bleeding, nutritional deficiency, anemia associated with renal insufficiency, and hemolysis.
  • Fecal occult blood testing to rule out occult gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Ferritin, iron studies, vitamin B12, and folate — because a mixed anemia is common and a normal MCV does not exclude a deficiency.
  • Creatinine to rule out renal disease. Dr. Brandt also checks liver function tests.
  • If hemolysis is suspected — haptoglobin (low), lactate dehydrogenase (increased), indirect bilirubin, reticulocyte count, and a blood smear.
  • Then consider anemia of chronic disease or a bone marrow disorder, using the peripheral smear and further testing to sort between them.
🩺 Scope of practice

Primary care manages a great deal of anemia of chronic disease and iron deficiency anemia. These are not automatic referrals — knowing the workup is the point of this lecture.

Macrocytic anemia — MCV above 100

  1. Start with the historyMedications — hydroxyurea is the named example — and alcohol use. These are common and often the whole answer.
  2. Rule out nutritional deficiencyVitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, or both.
  3. Order vitamin B12 and homocysteine togetherIf both are abnormal in the expected direction, that is suggestive of B12 deficiency. If only one of the two is off, check methylmalonic acid. A high methylmalonic acid is suggestive of B12 deficiency.
  4. Clinical caveatMany clinicians simply order methylmalonic acid and homocysteine at the same time when B12 deficiency is suspected, rather than sequencing them.
  5. If B12 deficiency is confirmed, ask whyIs there evidence of pernicious anemia? Consider intrinsic factor antibodies.
🧬 Other causes of a high MCV worth knowing

Beyond B12, folate, alcohol, and drugs: reticulocytosis (reticulocytes are larger than mature red cells, so a brisk marrow response raises the MCV), myelodysplastic syndromes, liver disease, and hypothyroidism. This is why a macrocytosis with normal B12 and folate is not a dead end.

Sorting by size alone gets you partway. Crossing size against the reticulocyte count — is the marrow responding? — narrows the differential considerably, and it is how the lecture connects Learning Objective 2 to Learning Objective 4.

Q: A 24-year-old man has a mean corpuscular volume of 68 fL, a hemoglobin of 11.9 g/dL, a red cell distribution width of 13%, a red cell count above the reference range, and a ferritin of 145 ng/mL. A complete blood count from five years ago showed an MCV of 70. What is the diagnosis, and what is the next test?

Answer

Thalassemia trait, and the next test is a hemoglobin electrophoresis (plus a hematology consult). Every element points away from iron deficiency: the ferritin is normal, the red cell distribution width is normal (thalassemia makes cells that are uniformly small, so there is no anisocytosis), the red cell count is high (many small cells rather than few), and the microcytosis is documented as chronic. The lecture's algorithm is explicit — normal ferritin plus chronic microcytosis equals hemoglobin electrophoresis.

LO 3 — Anemia Markers

Learning Objective 3

Iron Studies, B12, Folate, MMA and Homocysteine

Abbreviations used in this section — TIBC total iron-binding capacity · Tsat transferrin saturation · MMA methylmalonic acid · B12 vitamin B12 (cobalamin) · CoA coenzyme A · MI myocardial infarction · DNA deoxyribonucleic acid · OCP oral contraceptive pill.

Iron studies

Ferritin

The protein that stores iron

High in hemochromatosis, in chronic infection where ferritin is released from cells, and with increased synthesis. Also high simply as a marker of inflammation.
Low in iron deficiency anemia, hypothyroidism, and vitamin C deficiency.

Serum iron

The amount of iron circulating in blood

High in iron overload.
Low in iron deficiency anemia and in chronic disease — which is exactly why it cannot distinguish the two.

Iron-binding capacity (TIBC)

Transferrin and other iron-binding proteins available

High when the liver ramps up transferrin production to scavenge iron: iron deficiency anemia, pregnancy, oral contraceptive use (an estrogen effect), viral hepatitis.
Low when transferrin synthesis is suppressed or iron stores are already high: anemia of chronic disease, hemochromatosis, liver disease, malnutrition or nephrotic syndrome (protein loss).

Transferrin & Tsat

The carrier, and how full it is

Transferrin is the protein that carries iron. Transferrin saturation is the percentage of it that is actually loaded with iron. Low in both iron deficiency and anemia of chronic disease.

⚠ The single most testable trap in this lecture

Ferritin is an ACUTE PHASE REACTANT. Inflammation drives it up independently of how much iron the patient actually has. A genuinely iron-deficient patient who also has an active infection, an inflammatory arthritis, or a malignancy can show a normal or even elevated ferritin — and you will falsely conclude that iron deficiency is excluded. When inflammation is in the picture, interpret ferritin with that in mind and lean on the transferrin/TIBC direction and the transferrin saturation.

🧠 Why MMA and homocysteine behave the way they do

These are the lecture's speaker notes, and they are what make the two tests memorable rather than arbitrary.

  • Methylmalonic acid. Vitamin B12 is the cofactor for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. If B12 is low, that enzyme's activity falls → methylmalonyl-CoA is instead converted to methylmalonic acid → MMA accumulates in the blood. So MMA rises specifically in B12 deficiency.
  • Homocysteine. Breaking down homocysteine requires both B12 and folate. Deficiency of either impairs that processing → homocysteine rises. So homocysteine is not specific — it goes up in B12 deficiency and in folate deficiency.
🔑 The discriminator, stated once, cleanly
DeficiencyMethylmalonic acidHomocysteine
Vitamin B12HighHigh
FolateNormalHigh

Both metabolites high → B12. Homocysteine high with a normal MMA → folate. This is the reason MMA is worth ordering when the serum B12 comes back borderline: the serum level is an imperfect measure of what is happening in tissue, and MMA is the confirmatory test.

🩺 Pernicious anemia

An autoimmune condition that prevents the body from absorbing vitamin B12. If B12 deficiency is confirmed, ask whether pernicious anemia is the cause and consider intrinsic factor antibodies. The mechanistic cousin to keep beside it: gastric surgery. Intrinsic factor is produced by gastric parietal cells, so a partial gastrectomy removes the machinery for B12 absorption — the mechanism behind Case 8 below.

Fecal occult blood testing — for any type of anemia

  • Detects hidden, microscopic blood in the stool.
  • Its screening role is for colorectal cancer, but it is also useful when there is concern for gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Usually done in microcytic and iron deficiency anemia — but consider it in normocytic anemia as well.

Q: A patient with a macrocytic anemia has a serum vitamin B12 at the low end of normal and a normal folate. Homocysteine is elevated. Which single test resolves the diagnosis, what result would you expect, and what is the biochemical reason it works?

Answer

Methylmalonic acid, which would be elevated in B12 deficiency. It works because vitamin B12 is the cofactor for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase — without B12, methylmalonyl-CoA is shunted to methylmalonic acid, which then accumulates in blood. Folate has no role in that reaction, so MMA stays normal in isolated folate deficiency. Homocysteine cannot resolve it because both B12 and folate are required to break homocysteine down, so it rises in either deficiency.

LO 4 — Reticulocytes & RPI

Learning Objective 4

The Reticulocyte Count and the Production Index

Abbreviations used in this section — RPI reticulocyte production index · Hct hematocrit · RBC red blood cell · Hgb hemoglobin.

What the raw reticulocyte count tells you

A reticulocyte is an immature red blood cell. The reticulocyte count measures what percentage of circulating red cells are reticulocytes, and it is used as a read on bone marrow function and the marrow's response to anemia — it reflects the rate of red cell production (erythropoiesis).

  • Normal — 1 to 2% of circulating red cells are reticulocytes.
  • High rate of production — increased red cell production. Seen in recent blood loss, hemolytic anemia, and infiltrative marrow disorders.
  • Low rate of production — decreased red cell production. Seen in marrow failure, infections, toxins, renal disease, and iron or vitamin deficiency.
🧠 Why a raw percentage is misleading — the four-day life story

The reticulocyte stage of red cell development lasts four days.

  • Normally: 1 to 3 of those days are spent in the bone marrow, and 1 to 2 days in the circulation. Then the cell matures into a red blood cell.
  • In severe anemia: reticulocytes leave the marrow earlier and therefore circulate for 2 to 3 days instead of 1 to 2.

Two distortions follow, and the reticulocyte production index corrects both. First, a percentage measured against a shrunken total red cell pool looks larger than the true production rate. Second, reticulocytes released prematurely hang around longer in blood, so they are counted for more days — again inflating the percentage. This is the entire basis for the calculation.

The calculation, step by step

Step ONE — correct for the severity of the anemia

Corrected reticulocyte % = reticulocyte % × (actual hematocrit ÷ 45)

45% is the normal hematocrit used as the reference. (Equivalently, some sources use hemoglobin ÷ 15 g/dL.)

Step TWO — correct for the longer lifespan of prematurely released reticulocytes

RPI = corrected reticulocyte % ÷ maturation correction factor

HematocritMaturation correction factor
Greater than 35%1.0
26–35%1.5
16–25%2.0
15% or less2.5
🔑 Interpreting the number
  • RPI 0.5% – 2.5% — the normal range in a healthy patient.
  • RPI below 2 — loss of red cells with decreased production of reticulocytes. The marrow is not keeping up. Think production problem.
  • RPI above 2.5–3 — anemia with increased production to replace lost red cells. The marrow is responding appropriately. Think loss or destruction.

Worked example — from the lecture

Hemoglobin 7.5 g/dL, hematocrit 25%, reticulocyte count 5%. Normal hematocrit 45%.

  • Corrected reticulocyte percentage = 5 × (25 ÷ 45) = 2.78
  • Hematocrit of 25 falls in the 16–25 band → maturation correction = 2
  • RPI = 2.78 ÷ 2 = 1.39

Interpretation: the bone marrow is not responding as we would wish. Is there a production problem? That is the question this number sends you to answer.

⚠ The normal-value trap

A reticulocyte count sitting inside the reference range is not reassuring in a significantly anemic patient. The reference range is calibrated to a normal hematocrit. When the hematocrit is 24%, a healthy marrow should be pouring out reticulocytes, so a "normal" 1.5% is an inappropriately low response. This is precisely the distortion the production index exists to expose — and it is the mechanism behind more than one exam question.

Q: A patient with chronic anemia has a hematocrit of 30% and a reticulocyte count of 4%. Calculate the reticulocyte production index and state what it means.

Answer

Step one: corrected reticulocyte percentage = 4 × (30 ÷ 45) = 2.67. Step two: a hematocrit of 30 falls in the 26–35 band → maturation factor 1.5. RPI = 2.67 ÷ 1.5 = 1.78. Because this is below 2, the marrow response is inadequate for the degree of anemia — there is a production problem to find. Note that the raw 4% looked like a brisk response; both corrections were needed to see that it is not.

Putting it together

The Workup of Anemia, Start to Finish

Abbreviations used in this section — H&H hemoglobin and hematocrit · MCV mean corpuscular volume · MMA methylmalonic acid · ED emergency department · EtOH ethanol (alcohol) · GI gastrointestinal.

This is Dr. Brandt's own sequence — described in the lecture as "my gut instinct."

  1. Evaluate the patient firstIf there is an acute bleed or the patient is unstable, send them to the emergency department. Everything below assumes a stable outpatient.
  2. Reticulocyte count — is the bone marrow responding?This is the question that splits production problems from loss and destruction.
  3. Trend the hemoglobin and hematocritA single value is a snapshot; the trajectory tells you whether this is acute, chronic, or acute-on-chronic.
  4. Order markers according to the MCVFerritin, iron, vitamin B12, and folate. Some clinicians add methylmalonic acid and homocysteine.
    Microcytic (low MCV) → iron deficiency, or a hereditary anemia such as thalassemia (less common).
    Normocytic (normal MCV) → mixed anemia, anemia of chronic disease.
    Macrocytic (high MCV) → vitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, or drugs including alcohol.
  5. Consider fecal occult blood testing and further GI testingEspecially with iron deficiency — but consider it in normocytic anemia too.
  6. Consider a peripheral smearThe morphologic lens. It is how you catch what the indices average away.
  7. Consider a hematology consultFor an abnormal peripheral smear, if there are bone marrow issues, or if a bone marrow biopsy is needed.
  8. Consider the entire pictureThe lecture's closing instruction, in capitals on the slide: look at the patient's other medical problems and see the correlations. Anemia is rarely a free-standing diagnosis.
🩺 History and physical in anemia — what to actually ask and look for

History

  • Underlying medical conditions and medications that can cause or contribute to anemia — check whether your patient has them.
  • Family history of anemia — sickle cell disease, thalassemia.
  • Acquired risk factors: special diet (a vegan diet may lack vitamin B12), travel (parasitic infection), infections, bleeding (menses, blood in stool).
  • Acute or chronic?
  • Symptoms suggesting hemolysis — dark urine, jaundice, history of gallstones.
  • Symptoms of kidney or liver disease.
  • Symptoms of the anemia itself.

Physical examination

  • Vital signs — are they stable? This is the triage question, asked first.
  • Signs of pallor or jaundice — conjunctivae, palmar creases, lips.
  • Signs of abnormal bleeding.
  • Hepatosplenomegaly.

Q: Before any laboratory value is considered, what is the first branch point in the workup of a patient with anemia, and what does each branch lead to?

Answer

Is the patient acutely bleeding or unstable? If yes — send them to the emergency department; nothing else on the list happens first. If no, proceed with the outpatient sequence: reticulocyte count, trend the hemoglobin and hematocrit, then order markers based on the MCV. Vital signs are the physical-exam item that answers this branch point.

Case 1 — 37-year-old woman with fatigue and menorrhagia for one year

She reports menses of 10 days' duration with 7 very heavy days: 5 pads per day and 5 super tampons per day for 7 days.

  • Vitals: BP 100/55, HR 60, RR 14, oxygen saturation 99% on room air.
  • HEENT: normocephalic and atraumatic, conjunctival pallor. Palmar surfaces of the hands are pale. Lips are a lighter pink than expected.
  • Respiratory: clear to auscultation, no wheezes, rales, or rhonchi.
  • Cardiac: regular rate and rhythm, 1–2/6 systolic murmur over the left sternal border. She denies any past history of a murmur.

Q: What do you see? Why is the heart rate so normal? What might happen if she becomes more anemic? What would happen to her heart rate if this were acute blood loss? Is her blood pressure low? Why is her platelet count elevated? What further tests are you thinking?

Answer

What you see: a severe microcytic, hypochromic anemia — hemoglobin 7.2, MCV 68.3, MCHC 28.3 — with a markedly elevated red cell distribution width of 18.3 (anisocytosis, the fingerprint of iron deficiency) and reactive thrombocytosis at 459.

Why the heart rate is normal at 60: the anemia developed slowly over a year. Chronic anemia is compensated by mechanisms that do not require tachycardia — plasma volume expansion, a rightward shift of the oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve increasing oxygen offloading, and an increase in stroke volume. She has lost iron gradually, not intravascular volume acutely, so there is no baroreceptor-driven sympathetic surge.

If she becomes more anemic: compensation fails. Expect tachycardia, exertional dyspnea, worsening flow murmur, angina if there is coronary disease, and eventually a high-output state and heart failure.

If this were acute blood loss: her heart rate would be high. Acute loss removes intravascular volume, triggering the baroreceptor reflex and sympathetic tachycardia — see Case 3, where the same process is in progress at a heart rate of 104.

Is her blood pressure low? 100/55 is low-normal with a widened pulse pressure and a low diastolic, consistent with the reduced blood viscosity and vasodilation of chronic anemia. She is compensated, not in shock.

Why the platelets are elevated: reactive thrombocytosis of iron deficiency — one of the named reactive causes on the platelet differential.

Further tests: ferritin first, plus iron, total iron-binding capacity, and transferrin saturation; a reticulocyte count and production index; a peripheral smear. Then the cause: the irregular firm suprapubic mass points to uterine fibroids as the source of the menorrhagia — pelvic imaging and gynecologic evaluation. Remember the rule: replace the iron and find the cause.

Answer

Diagnosis: acute-on-chronic blood loss anemia from an upper gastrointestinal source. Answer

Q: Why is he anemic? Why is the anemia normocytic? Why are his platelets elevated?

Answer

Why anemic: surgical blood loss from the coronary artery bypass graft, compounded by hemodilution from intraoperative fluids, the cardiopulmonary bypass circuit, and repeated inpatient phlebotomy. This is loss, not a production problem.

Why normocytic: acute blood loss removes whole, normal-sized red cells — you lose plasma and cells together, so the cells that remain are ordinary ones. There has been no time for iron depletion to shrink the cells, and no nutritional deficiency to enlarge them.

Why the platelets are elevated: reactive thrombocytosis driven by tissue injury and the postoperative inflammatory response — surgical trauma releases inflammatory mediators that stimulate megakaryocytes. It typically peaks around the first to second week after surgery and then resolves on its own. Note it is reactive, not primary; nothing here suggests a myeloproliferative disorder.

Q: What is her past medical history? What do you see on the smear, and what does the third finding tell you?

Answer

Past medical history: sickle cell disease, and this is a vaso-occlusive pain crisis precipitated by the febrile illness her children brought home — fever, dehydration, and hypoxemia all promote hemoglobin S polymerization.

On the smear: elongated, crescent-shaped sickled erythrocytes (solid arrows) — rigid cells that occlude the microvasculature and produce the bone pain. Target cells (dashed arrow) — cells with excess membrane relative to hemoglobin content. And a Howell-Jolly body (arrowhead), a retained nuclear DNA remnant inside a red cell.

Why the Howell-Jolly body matters: a functioning spleen removes these remnants. Seeing them means the spleen is not working — in sickle cell disease, repeated infarction produces autosplenectomy. That is not a curiosity: a functionally asplenic patient is at risk for overwhelming infection with encapsulated organisms, which is why her fever demands a real infection workup and not just analgesia.

Also note: the leukocytosis of 13 is expected during a crisis and does not by itself prove infection, and the hemoglobin of 6 with a hematocrit of 18 reflects the chronic hemolytic anemia of the disease.

Answer

Diagnosis: iron deficiency anemia.

Evidence line 1 — the red cell indices: microcytic anemia (MCV 72), hypochromic (MCHC 30), high red cell distribution width (17), with reactive thrombocytosis (420). Evidence line 2 — the iron studies: the complete iron-deficient pattern — low ferritin (8), low iron (25), high total iron-binding capacity (450), low transferrin saturation (6%). The high TIBC is what separates this from anemia of chronic disease, where TIBC is low or normal and ferritin is normal or high.

The reticulocyte trap: 0.5% sits inside the reference range, but that range assumes a normal hematocrit. At a hematocrit of 28 the marrow should be producing far more, and the production index of 0.4 — far below 2 — exposes it as an inadequate response. That makes sense mechanistically: without iron, the marrow cannot mount the response the anemia demands.

Treatment, both halves: replace the iron AND find the cause of the iron deficiency. Here the cause is in the history — heavy menses with clots. The vegetarian diet is a decoy that pulls toward vitamin B12, which would have raised the MCV, not lowered it.

The lecture calls this out deliberately — the anemia visit is also a preventive care opportunity.

🎯 CBC & Anemia — High-Yield One-Pager

By learning objective — LO1 CBC & differential · LO2 anemia by MCV · LO3 iron/B12/folate markers · LO4 reticulocytes & RPI

LO 1 · CBC & DIFFERENTIAL

The one rule

  • Result ≠ clinical picture → may be spurious → repeat it.
  • Result = clinical picture, or acute → act, don't repeat.
  • Example: WBC 24 in an asymptomatic man → repeat. Same WBC 24 with a hot swollen leg → treat cellulitis.

Indices — what each means

  • RBC count ≠ O₂ capacity. Hgb is O₂ capacity. Hct = % volume red cells.
  • MCV 80–100 fL — average size, the sorting tool. It's a mean — can hide a mixed population.
  • RDW 11.5–15% — variation in size (anisocytosis). High early in iron deficiency; normal in thalassemia.
  • MCH — Hgb per cell, tracks MCV. MCHC — Hgb density per cell.
  • Low MCHC = hypochromia → ↓production (iron deficiency) or reticulocytosis.
  • High MCHC → usually artifact (lipemia, hemolysis, agglutination, leukocytosis, hyperbilirubinemia); if real → spherocytosis or hemoglobinopathy.

WBC differential quick-picks

  • Neutrophils ↑ bacterial infection, inflammation, necrosis/trauma, steroids, stress, smoking, myeloid neoplasm. ↓ destruction, drugs, myelodysplasia.
  • Lymphocytes ↑ viral, lymphoproliferative. ↓ autoimmune, immunosuppression.
  • Monocytes ↑ chronic infection/inflammation, myeloid neoplasm.
  • Eosinophils ↑ allergy, parasites, asthma, drug hypersensitivity.
  • Basophils ↑ myeloproliferative neoplasms, chronic hypersensitivity.
  • Any line off → consider a marrow disorder.

Platelets

  • ↑ Reactive: infection, inflammation, tissue damage, iron deficiency, malignancy, hemolysis, drugs. ↑ Primary: essential thrombocythemia, polycythemia vera.
  • ↓ ↓Production: marrow disorders, chemo/radiation. ↓ ↑Destruction: immune, drugs, infection, splenic sequestration.
  • Microcytic anemia + thrombocytosis = think iron deficiency.
LO 2 · ANEMIA BY MCV

Three buckets

  • Micro <80: iron deficiency · thalassemia · anemia of chronic disease.
  • Normo 80–100: mixed · chronic disease · acute bleed · renal · marrow.
  • Macro >100: B12 · folate · alcohol · drugs (hydroxyurea, methotrexate) · myelodysplasia · liver · hypothyroid · reticulocytosis.

Microcytic algorithm

  • Check ferritin first (± retic, smear).
  • Low ferritin → iron deficiency. Find the source.
  • Normal/high ferritin → iron deficiency unlikely. Is microcytosis chronic? → thalassemia → hemoglobin electrophoresis + heme consult.
  • Acquired, not iron → anemia of chronic disease → find the systemic disease.

  • Exclude treatable: bleeding, nutritional, renal, hemolysis.

LO 3 · MARKERS

Iron studies

  • Ferritin = storage. ↑ hemochromatosis, chronic infection, inflammation. ↓ iron deficiency, hypothyroid, vitamin C deficiency.
  • Serum iron ↑ overload; ↓ in both iron deficiency and chronic disease — does not discriminate.
  • TIBC ↑ iron deficiency, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, viral hepatitis. ↓ inflammation, hemochromatosis, liver disease, malnutrition/nephrotic.
  • Transferrin carries iron; Tsat = how full it is.

⚠ Ferritin is an ACUTE PHASE REACTANT

  • Inflammation raises it independent of iron stores.
  • A truly iron-deficient patient with infection/inflammation/malignancy can show a normal or high ferritin → you will falsely exclude iron deficiency.
  • Fall back on TIBC direction and Tsat.

B12 vs folate — the discriminator

DeficiencyMMAHomocysteine
B12↑↑
Folatenormal↑
  • Why: B12 is the cofactor for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase → no B12 → methylmalonyl-CoA → MMA accumulates.
  • Both B12 and folate are needed to break down homocysteine → either deficiency raises it → not specific.
  • Order B12 + homocysteine; if only one is off, check MMA. High MMA also in renal insufficiency.
  • Neuro findings (vibration, proprioception, Romberg) = B12, not folate.
  • B12 low → ask about pernicious anemia (intrinsic factor antibodies) or gastric surgery.
LO 4 · RETICULOCYTES & RPI

The calculation

  • Step 1: corrected retic % = retic % × (Hct ÷ 45).
  • Step 2: RPI = corrected % ÷ maturation factor.
  • Maturation factor by Hct: >35 → 1.0 · 26–35 → 1.5 · 16–25 → 2.0 · ≤15 → 2.5.
  • Retic stage = 4 days (1–3 marrow, 1–2 circulation). Severe anemia → released early, circulate 2–3 days.

Interpretation

  • Normal RPI 0.5–2.5.
  • <2 = loss with ↓ production — marrow not keeping up → production problem.
  • >2.5–3 = appropriate response → loss or destruction.
  • ⚠ Normal-value trap: a retic % inside the reference range is inappropriately low in significant anemia.
  • Worked: Hgb 7.5, Hct 25, retic 5% → 5×(25/45)=2.78 → ÷2 → RPI 1.39 = marrow not responding.

Workup sequence

  • Unstable / acute bleed → ED. Otherwise:
  • Retic count → trend H&H → markers by MCV → fecal occult blood → smear → heme consult if smear abnormal or biopsy needed.
  • Consider the whole patient and other medical problems.
