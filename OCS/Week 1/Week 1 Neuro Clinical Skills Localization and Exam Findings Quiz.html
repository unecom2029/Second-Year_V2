Week 1 Intro Neuro - High-Yield One-Pager

1. Patient interaction & professionalism

  • Knock, enter, introduce yourself/role, confirm patient name and DOB.
  • Sanitize hands before and after any contact, visibly, in front of the patient.
  • Explain what you're about to do in plain language (no jargon) and get verbal consent before touching.
  • Warn before anything uncomfortable, drape appropriately, check in ("let me know if this hurts").
  • Stay professional with both patient and preceptor: eye contact, active listening, thank the patient at the end.

2. Mental status: screen vs. full assessment

  • Bedside mini-screen: A&O x3 (person/place/time), draw a clock set to a specific time (visuospatial/executive), recall 3 objects at 5 minutes (memory).
  • MMSE: 30 pts, <24 suggests impairment; less executive/visuospatial weighting than MoCA.
  • MoCA: 30 pts, normal usually ≥26; add 1 pt if ≤12 years education; more sensitive for mild cognitive impairment.
  • SLUMS: 30 pts, cutoff shifts with education level (high school vs. less); good for detecting mild impairment.
  • Only chart what you actually tested — don't default to "A&Ox3" unassessed.

3. Cranial nerve exam (I-XII)

  • CN I: smell each nostril (rarely tested routinely).
  • CN II: visual acuity, confrontation visual fields, pupillary light reflex, fundoscopy.
  • CN III/IV/VI: extraocular movements (H pattern), pupil size/reactivity, ptosis, convergence.
  • CN V: facial sensation x3 divisions, motor (clench masseter/temporalis), corneal reflex.
  • CN VII: symmetry — raise brows, shut eyes tight, smile, puff cheeks; forehead spared = UMN, forehead involved = LMN/Bell's palsy.
  • CN VIII: hearing (finger rub/whisper), Weber/Rinne.
  • CN IX/X: palate rises symmetrically, gag reflex, voice/swallow quality.
  • CN XI: shoulder shrug and head turn against resistance (trapezius/SCM).
  • CN XII: tongue protrusion — deviates toward the lesion side (LMN).

4. Pathways overview

  • Spinothalamic: pain/temp/crude touch — crosses near entry level, ascends contralaterally.
  • Posterior columns: vibration/proprioception/fine touch — ascend ipsilaterally, cross in medulla.
  • Corticospinal: voluntary movement — crosses at medullary pyramids (UMN tract).
  • Cerebellum: coordination, balance, posture — ipsilateral control.

5. Central/peripheral exam components

  • Gait: casual, tandem, heel and toe walk — screens motor, cerebellar, sensory, and vestibular systems together.
  • Cerebellar/coordination: finger-to-nose, rapid alternating movements, heel-to-shin.
  • Sensory screen: light touch, pinprick, vibration/proprioception in all 4 limbs; map to dermatomes if a deficit is found.
  • Myotomes: key muscle by nerve root (e.g., C5 deltoid, L4 tibialis anterior) to localize radiculopathy.
  • DTRs: biceps C5/6, brachioradialis C6, triceps C7, patellar L4, Achilles S1.
  • Abdominal reflexes: stroke each quadrant toward umbilicus; absence suggests a UMN lesion above the level tested.

6. Motor strength & DTR grading

  • Strength 0-5: 0 no contraction, 1 flicker/trace, 2 moves with gravity eliminated, 3 moves against gravity only, 4 moves against some resistance, 5 normal full strength.
  • DTR 0-4: 0 absent (try Jendrassik reinforcement), 1 diminished, 2 normal, 3 brisk without clonus, 4 brisk with clonus.
  • Pattern matters as much as grade: symmetric mild weakness with normal reflexes ≠ focal weakness with hyperreflexia (UMN).

7. Sensory tract-specific testing

  • Spinothalamic (pain/temp): pinprick with broken cotton swab; temp with tuning fork sides or cold object.
  • Posterior columns (position/vibration): 128 Hz tuning fork on a bony prominence; move great toe/finger up/down, eyes closed.
  • Both tracts (light touch): cotton wisp or fingertip, light touch bilaterally.
  • Cerebellar screen: Romberg — feet together, eyes closed; falling = proprioceptive/posterior column loss, not cerebellar (cerebellar ataxia is present even with eyes open). Pronator drift — arms out, palms up, eyes closed; downward drift/pronation = contralateral UMN lesion.
  • Cortex (needs intact primary sensation first): two-point discrimination, stereognosis (identify object by touch), graphesthesia (identify number drawn on skin) — abnormal with parietal lobe lesion.

8. The 8 pathologic gaits

  • Hemiplegic: stiff extended leg circumducts, arm flexed/adducted (stroke, unilateral UMN).
  • Spastic diplegic: stiff, scissoring legs, small steps (bilateral UMN, e.g., cerebral palsy).
  • Neuropathic/steppage: high-stepping with foot slap (foot drop, peripheral nerve/L5).
  • Myopathic: wide-based waddle, Trendelenburg (proximal weakness).
  • Parkinsonian: stooped, shuffling, short steps, reduced arm swing, festination.
  • Choreiform: irregular, dance-like, unpredictable (basal ganglia, e.g., Huntington's).
  • Ataxic/cerebellar: wide-based, staggering, irregular — same whether eyes open or closed.
  • Sensory (ataxic): wide-based, watches the floor, slapping/stomping steps — worse in the dark or eyes closed (proprioceptive loss).

9. Sensory localization by lesion site

  • Cerebrum/thalamus: contralateral loss of discriminative sensation (two-point, stereognosis, graphesthesia) with relatively preserved crude touch/pain; often with cortical signs (neglect, aphasia).
  • Spinal cord: a sensory level below a specific dermatome; dissociated loss (pain/temp vs. vibration/proprioception on opposite sides) suggests Brown-Séquard or syrinx.
  • Nerve root: dermatomal pattern, often radiating pain, paired with matching myotome weakness/reflex loss.
  • Peripheral nerve: symmetric distal > proximal "stocking-glove" loss = polyneuropathy; a single nerve territory = mononeuropathy.

10. Labs, imaging & diagnostic tests

  • Acute stroke/brain: non-contrast CT head first (rule out hemorrhage) → MRI brain to characterize further.
  • Spinal cord concern: MRI spine (with contrast if infection/tumor suspected).
  • Nerve root/radiculopathy: MRI spine if progressive or red flags; EMG/NCS if unclear.
  • Peripheral neuropathy: glucose/HbA1c, B12, TSH, SPEP, EMG/NCS.
  • NMJ disorder: AChR/MuSK antibodies, repetitive nerve stimulation, EMG.
  • Myopathy: CK, ESR/CRP, thyroid studies, biopsy if needed.
  • Seizure/encephalopathy: EEG; suspected infection: LP for CSF.

11. UMN vs. LMN vs. myopathy vs. NMJ

  • UMN (brain/cord): extensor-pattern weakness, spasticity, hyperreflexia, positive Babinski, no early atrophy/fasciculations, +/- clonus.
  • LMN (anterior horn/root/nerve): flaccid weakness, hyporeflexia/areflexia, atrophy, fasciculations, no Babinski.
  • Myopathy (muscle): proximal > distal, symmetric, reflexes normal until late, no sensory loss or fasciculations; +/- myalgia, elevated CK.
  • NMJ (e.g., myasthenia gravis): fatigable weakness (worse with repetition/end of day), ptosis/diplopia/bulbar symptoms, normal reflexes and sensation, no early atrophy.

12. Weakness: atrophy, tone, fasciculations, DTR, Babinski, sensory

  • LMN weakness → atrophy, flaccid/decreased tone, fasciculations, decreased/absent DTRs, flexor (normal) plantar response, sensory loss if nerve/root involved.
  • UMN weakness → little/no early atrophy, spastic/increased tone, no fasciculations, hyperactive DTRs +/- clonus, extensor plantar response (positive Babinski), central-pattern sensory loss if present.
  • Myopathic weakness → mild late disuse atrophy, normal/decreased tone, no fasciculations, normal-to-reduced DTRs late, flexor plantar response, no sensory loss.
  • NMJ weakness → no atrophy until very late, normal tone, no fasciculations, normal DTRs, flexor plantar response, no sensory loss — fatigability is the distinguishing clue.
