Orientation

The one sentence that organizes everything

Overview: The Problem Is the Response, Not the Organism

🔑 Core Definition

Sepsis is the body's extreme response to an infection, resulting in life-threatening organ dysfunction or failure caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, involving the widespread release of inflammatory mediators. Rather than dealing with the infection, the immune system attacks "self."

Almost every difficulty in this lecture follows from that definition. Because the damage comes from the host response rather than the pathogen directly, no single organism defines sepsis, cultures are negative in 30–50% of cases, and — as the outline states twice — no assessment or diagnostic criterion has sufficient sensitivity or specificity. That is why there are five competing scoring systems rather than one test.

⚠️ Two Statements the Outline Makes Explicitly
  • "Assessments of sepsis — none to date have sufficient sensitivity or specificity."
  • "Diagnosis — no reliable criteria."
Section A — Sepsis Terms

Definitions

The Three Terms, in Order of Severity

  1. Early sepsis Infection and bacteremia are sepsis risk factors — they are the setup, not the diagnosis itself.
  2. Sepsis Life-threatening, time-dependent organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Note the phrase time-dependent — it is what justifies the treatment bundle.
  3. Septic shock A subset of sepsis in which underlying circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially increase mortality. Can lead to multi-organ dysfunction (MODS — an older term).
🧠 How to Keep Them Straight

Infection/bacteremia → risk factor. Add organ dysfunction → sepsis. Add profound circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormality → septic shock. Each step is a strict subset of the one before, and each adds one requirement.

Q: What single feature separates septic shock from sepsis?

Answer

Septic shock is the subset of sepsis in which circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities are profound enough to substantially increase mortality. Both parts matter — it is not simply hypotension; the cellular/metabolic derangement is part of the definition, which is why lactate (a marker of cellular metabolic failure) sits in the treatment bundle.

SIRS Criteria — Measurable Systemic Responses to Stress

CategoryCriterion
TemperatureHypothermia (< 38 °C) or hyperthermia (> 38 °C)
Heart rateTachycardia > 90 bpm
RespiratoryIncreased respiratory rate > 20/min, OR PaCO₂ < 32 mmHg
White cell countLeukopenia < 4,000/µL, OR leukocytosis > 12,000/µL, OR > 10% bands
🩺 Note the Bidirectional Criteria

Three of the four SIRS categories can be tripped in either direction — temperature (low or high) and white cell count (low or high or left-shifted). A septic patient can be hypothermic and leukopenic, which is easy to misread as "not infected." That bidirectionality is a favorite exam trap.

SOFA — Sequential Organ Failure Assessment

  • Assesses performance of several organ systems — neurologic, liver, kidney, blood/hemodynamics — to determine mortality risk.
  • Better predictive validity than SIRS.
  • Score ≥ 2 = mortality risk of approximately 10%.

Slide — The SOFA score in full. Six organ systems, each scored 0–4. Note that the cardiovascular row is scored by vasopressor dose, not blood pressure alone, once MAP falls below 70.

qSOFA — Three Bedside Criteria

1

Respiratory rate ≥ 22/min

The most sensitive early vital sign change.

2

Systolic BP ≤ 100 mmHg

Note: ≤ 100, not < 90 — qSOFA catches patients before frank hypotension.

3

Altered mentation

Assessed by Glasgow Coma Scale.

Other Assessments

📊

NEWS2 — National Early Warning Score

Assesses risk of death from sepsis. Aggregate score of 6 parameters:

  • Respiration rate
  • Oxygen saturation
  • Systolic blood pressure
  • Pulse rate
  • Consciousness level
  • Temperature
🔍

MEWS — Modified Early Warning Score

More sensitive. 5 parameters:

  • Systolic blood pressure
  • Heart rate
  • Respiratory rate
  • Temperature
  • AVPU / mental status

Slide — MEWS scored out. The five parameters, each weighted 0–3 in both directions — a heart rate of 40 and a heart rate of 130 both score badly.

🔑 Distinguishing NEWS2 from MEWS

Both are early-warning scores built from vital signs. NEWS2 has 6 parameters and adds oxygen saturation; MEWS has 5 and is the more sensitive of the two. Every parameter in MEWS also appears in NEWS2 — oxygen saturation is the one NEWS2 adds.

Q: Which sepsis assessment is described as more specific with better predictive validity than SIRS, and what does a score of ≥ 2 indicate?

Answer

SOFA — the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment. A score of ≥ 2 indicates a mortality risk of approximately 10%. It assesses the performance of several organ systems (neurologic, liver, kidney, blood/hemodynamics). Remember that SIRS is no longer a defining criterion for sepsis, though it remains in use by the US Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services.

Sections B & C — Risk Factors and Epidemiology

Who gets it, and how often

Risk Factors and Epidemiology

Risk Factors for Sepsis

  • Intensive care unit admission
  • Bacteremia
  • Age: ≥ 65 years, and children < 1 year (risk increased if premature)
  • Pregnancy
  • Immune suppression co-morbidities — e.g. diabetes, chemotherapy, recent organ transplant
  • Community-acquired pneumonia
  • Prior hospitalization
  • Genetic factors leading to decreased immune response or reduced pathogen recognition

🔑 Source of Infection — Memorize the Order

Sepsis most commonly follows respiratory infection > urinary > abdominal > head > other (skin, gastrointestinal, meningitis). Respiratory first is the single most useful fact here — it also explains why community-acquired pneumonia appears in the risk factor list.

Section D — Etiologies

Which organisms

Etiologies: Bacterial First, but Nothing Is Excluded

🧬 The Overall Picture
  • Many organisms — bacterial, viral, fungal, protozoal, helminth. Bacterial etiology is more common than the others.
  • No organism (culture negative) is detected in 30–50% of cases.
  • Gram-positive bacteria are the most commonly identified etiologies: S. aureus, Enterococcus, S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes.
  • Gram-negatives remain important: E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacter.
  • 20% of cases are polymicrobial.
  • Viral causes are often underdiagnosed.
  • Fungal (Candida) cases are increasing in the immunocompromised, but remain lower than bacterial.
  • Protozoan and helminth agents are mostly seen in endemic niches.

Q: A patient develops sepsis following an intra-abdominal source. Which anaerobe should be at the front of your differential, and what proportion of sepsis cases overall are polymicrobial?

Answer

Bacteroides fragilis — the abdominal site is the one the outline explicitly pairs with B. fragilis and mixed anaerobes (along with E. coli and Candida). 20% of sepsis cases are polymicrobial, and abdominal sources are a major reason why.

Sections E & F — Microbial Triggers and Mechanism

Pathophysiology

PAMPs, DAMPs, and the Sepsis Paradigm

🦠

PAMPs — Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns

Molecular signatures carried by the organism itself.

💥

DAMPs — Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns

Released from the host's own damaged tissues — the mechanism by which viral infection triggers sepsis.

ClassTrigger typeSpecific molecules
Bacterial — gram-positivePAMPsLipoteichoic acid, cell wall peptidoglycan
Bacterial — gram-negativePAMPsLipopolysaccharide (LPS) — most prominent
Bacterial — otherPAMPsSuper-antigens (TSST 1&2, SPE), also DNA, RNA, surface glycoproteins
ViralDAMPsReleased from tissues damaged by the virus → stimulates a cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6)
FungalPAMPsCell wall β-glucans, mannans, chitin — all polysaccharides
ParasiticBothPAMPs and DAMPs
🔑 The Cleanest Pairings to Memorize
  • Gram-negative → LPS (most prominent trigger overall)
  • Gram-positive → lipoteichoic acid + peptidoglycan
  • Fungal → β-glucans, mannans, chitin (all polysaccharides — one rule covers all three)
  • Viral → DAMPs, not PAMPs — the exception in the list, and therefore the likely question

Mechanism — The Sepsis Paradigm

  1. Patient is injured by some means The initiating insult.
  2. Activation of host defenses (i) Release of inflammatory cytokines. (ii) Activation of other agents which exert control over bodily systems.
  3. Persistent inflammation → the cascade to organ failure Leads to endothelial dysfunction and ischemic tissue injury → sequential organ damage/failure → activation of cell death pathways: necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy.

Slide — The sepsis cascade. Every arm — coagulation, neutrophils, platelets, eicosanoids, T cells, cytokines — converges on the same three-step ending: endothelial damage → tissue injury → organ dysfunction.

🧠 Why "Sequential" Matters

Organ failure in sepsis is described as sequential, not simultaneous — which is precisely what the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment is built to track. The mechanism and the scoring system share the same word for the same reason.

Q: Name the four cell death pathways activated by persistent inflammation in sepsis, and the two upstream events that precede them.

Answer

Cell death pathways: necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy.

The two upstream events are endothelial dysfunction and ischemic tissue injury, which together produce sequential organ damage and failure. The chain to reproduce is: persistent inflammation → endothelial dysfunction + ischemic tissue injury → sequential organ failure → cell death pathways.

Section G — Signs, Symptoms and Diagnosis

Recognition

Diagnosis: No Reliable Criteria, Only Useful Findings

General signs and symptoms are early and non-specific. The outline is explicit that there are no reliable diagnostic criteria — you are assembling a picture from findings that are individually weak.

Useful Clinical Findings

  • "SIRS-like" symptoms
  • Peripheral vasodilation; shock
  • Mental acuity alteration
  • Jaundice
  • Metabolic acidosis
  • Thrombocytopenia
  • Respiratory alkalosis
⚠️ The Acid-Base Pairing

Sepsis classically produces metabolic acidosis (from lactate — tissue hypoperfusion) alongside respiratory alkalosis (from tachypnea). Both appear on the outline's list together. Seeing them simultaneously is more suggestive than either alone.

🩺 The One Lab That Points to a Cause

Most of the table tells you how sick the patient is. Procalcitonin is different — an elevation is indicative of a bacterial etiology, which makes it the one marker on the list that helps distinguish what kind of organism you are dealing with.

Q: A septic patient has a normal white blood cell count. Does this argue against the diagnosis?

Answer

No. The laboratory criterion is satisfied by leukocytosis (> 12,000/µL), leukopenia (< 4,000/µL), or a normal white count with > 10% immature forms — a left shift with a normal total count.

The same bidirectional logic runs through SIRS, where both hypothermia and hyperthermia qualify. In sepsis, a "normal-looking" number can still be an abnormal response.

Sections H & I — Treatment and Prognosis

Management

Treatment and Prognosis

The Early Sepsis Bundle

a

Bactericidal antimicrobial treatment

Note the outline specifies bactericidal, not merely bacteriostatic.

b

Intravenous fluids

Addresses the peripheral vasodilation and hypoperfusion.

c

Lactate measurement

The marker of the cellular/metabolic abnormality in the septic shock definition.

d

Assess tissue perfusion

Confirms whether the fluids are working.

e

Vasopressors

When fluids alone cannot maintain perfusion pressure.

Other Treatment

  • Surgical removal or drainage of the infection site (if known) — source control.
  • Oxygen therapy.
🔑 Why the Bundle Is a Bundle

Recall the Section A definition: sepsis is time-dependent organ dysfunction. That single word is the justification for delivering all five elements together and early, rather than sequentially as results return.

Prognosis

OutcomeFigure
Mortality (patients with risk factors)10–52%
Post-sepsis syndrome (PSS)50% of sepsis survivors
Survival in young / healthy patients90%
🩺 Post-Sepsis Syndrome

Half of all sepsis survivors develop post-sepsis syndrome — surviving the admission is not the end of the disease. Together with the high rate of re-hospitalization noted in the epidemiology section, this is why sepsis carries such a large ongoing cost burden ($62 billion annually).

Slide — What post-sepsis syndrome actually looks like. Note how much of it is neuropsychiatric — PTSD, flashbacks, panic attacks, cognitive decline — not just physical deconditioning.

Q: A previously healthy 24-year-old and a 71-year-old with diabetes and a recent hospitalization both present with sepsis. How does the prognosis differ, and what happens to survivors afterward?

Answer

The young, healthy patient has roughly 90% survival. The older patient carries several listed risk factors — age ≥ 65, immune suppression co-morbidity (diabetes), and prior hospitalization — placing them in the 10–52% mortality range.

For either survivor, post-sepsis syndrome affects 50%, and the rate of re-hospitalization is high.

Overall
