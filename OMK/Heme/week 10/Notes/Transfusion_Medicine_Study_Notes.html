Blood as a Medicine

The pivot this lecture asks you to make: for two hours you were taught blood as an organ and a disease. Now it becomes a drug — packaged from a human being, put in a bag, and given to somebody else. And unlike every other drug you will prescribe, it cannot be manufactured. It can only come from a person who volunteered.

🧬 Why people choose this field

Worth a paragraph, because it explains the shape of the lecture. The path into transfusion medicine runs through a pathology residency, then a transfusion medicine and blood banking fellowship — a board-certified subspecialty — and, for those drawn to it, a degree in public health.

The reason people choose it: it is like everything. Trauma and the emergency department (fast, high adrenaline), immunology (genuinely complicated), hematology and oncology, gene therapy and advanced cellular therapies, and — because the product only comes from volunteers — a strand of humanitarianism and global health running through all of it. The advice that follows: on your rotations, stay open to things you did not come here for.

Objective 1 · the products

The Four Blood Products, and How One Donation Becomes Three

Everything in a blood bank refrigerator started as one unit of whole blood from one person. A centrifuge and a plasma expressor turn it into separate medicines with separate indications, storage conditions and shelf lives.

One donation, three products — plus a fourth made by thawing the plasma cold.

🔑 The split that organizes the whole field

Look at the colours in that diagram. The red product is given to carry oxygen. Every yellow product is given for hemostasis. That single distinction sorts the indications, and it is why a bleeding patient needs more than red cells — when you bleed, you lose whole blood, so you must replace the coagulation factors and platelets too, not only the red cells.

What is actually in the bag

Citrate anticoagulant

in every bag

Without it the unit would simply clot. Citrate chelates calcium, and calcium is the trigger for the coagulation cascade — remove it and the cascade cannot start. Relevant clinically: rapid massive transfusion can cause citrate toxicity and hypocalcemia.

Additive solution

red cell food

The phrase used in lecture. Adenine and mannitol plus dextrose — fuel for the red cell's ATP pumps so it can keep living in the bag. This is what extends storage to 42 days.

Breathable plastic

the bag itself

The cells in that bag are alive and need oxygen, so the plastic is specially formulated to allow gas exchange.

Leukocyte reduction

a filter, before bagging

White cells are filtered out. They are the main source of the cytokines that cause febrile reactions, and of the HLA exposure that causes alloimmunization. Almost every product transfused in the United States is leukocyte reduced — it is the standard of care.

Pathogen reduction

platelets

A treatment that inactivates residual bacteria, viruses and parasites. Implemented in the United States specifically to reduce the risk from bacterially contaminated platelets.

Irradiation

selected patients

Prevents donor lymphocytes from proliferating in the recipient — the only defence against transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease. Blood banks serving children and cancer patients keep an irradiator on site.

⚠️ Platelets are the exception to everything

Every other product is kept cold. Platelets cannot be — cold activates them and they are cleared rapidly after transfusion. So they are stored at room temperature, on a rotator that agitates them continuously. The description of walking into a blood bank: it looks like a mad scientist's laboratory.

Room temperature storage has a direct consequence: any bacteria introduced during collection can grow. That is why the most common infectious risk of transfusion is a bacterially contaminated platelet, and why the shelf life is only 5–7 days rather than 42.

🩸

Whole-blood-derived platelets

Recovered from the buffy coat of routine whole blood donations. Two to five donors are pooled to make one adult transfusion.

Downside: the recipient is exposed to several donors at once.

⚙️

Apheresis platelets

A single donor sits at an instrument roughly the size of a small washing machine. Blood is drawn out, centrifuged in line, the platelets are removed into a bag, and everything else is returned to the donor.

Advantage: an entire transfusion from one donor — and often enough for two to three patients.

Cryoprecipitate — the Thanksgiving turkey

🧠 How you make it, and why it is fibrinogen-rich

The analogy used in lecture: you buy a turkey frozen and thaw it in the refrigerator, not on the counter. Do the same with a unit of frozen plasma — thaw it at refrigerated temperature (1–6 °C) rather than at room temperature — and certain proteins fall out of solution as a precipitate.

That cold-insoluble precipitate is enriched in fibrinogen, and also carries factor VIII, von Willebrand factor, factor XIII and fibronectin. It is deliberately old-fashioned technology, and it is still the product you reach for when fibrinogen is low.

Why fibrinogen specifically matters: go back to the coagulation cascade. The intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converge on the common pathway, and at the bottom is fibrinogen becoming fibrin — the protein that actually cross-links the clot. Cryoprecipitate is the bagged product for treating fibrinogen deficiency.

Plasma-derived medicines — the ones that come from the pharmacy

🔑 A category most students miss entirely

There are drugs you will prescribe routinely that are made from human plasma donors, and that arrive from the pharmacy rather than the blood bank. It is easy to forget where they came from.

  • Albumin
  • Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) — widely used in immune-mediated disease
  • Coagulation factor concentrates — including 3-factor and 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrates, and specific factors

The difference from blood bank plasma is the processing. Manufacturers pool plasma from many donors, then heat and solvent-detergent treat it to inactivate pathogens, before purifying the protein of interest. So albumin has had a pathogen-inactivation step that a bag of thawed plasma from your blood bank has not.

🩺 Two developments to know about — whole blood and freeze-dried plasma
  • Whole blood. Since the Gulf Wars, military medicine has driven renewed interest in transfusing whole blood unseparated. The framing from lecture: platelets are really good for a cancer patient; if you are a soldier who has just been blown up by an improvised explosive device, whole blood is probably what you need. Different patients, different products.
  • Freeze-dried plasma. The first FDA approval in the United States came in 2025. It is a bag of freeze-dried plasma you reconstitute with fluid — so it can sit in a backpack rather than a freezer. Compelling for far-forward military settings and very rural care. Most hospitals you rotate through will not stock it; they are set up to thaw conventional plasma.
  • Blood on ambulances. There is active work in the field to put blood on ambulances in the United States, because even here it can take a long time to reach a trauma surgeon.

Q: Why is citrate added to a unit of red cells, and what electrolyte problem can it cause during a massive transfusion?

Answer

Citrate is the anticoagulant. It works by chelating calcium, and calcium is the trigger for the coagulation cascade — without free calcium the unit cannot clot in the bag. During massive transfusion the recipient receives a large citrate load faster than the liver can metabolize it, so the citrate chelates the patient's calcium too, producing hypocalcemia — with paresthesias, tetany, QT prolongation and impaired hemostasis. Monitor ionized calcium and replace it.

Q: A patient needs plasma urgently. Your colleague suggests giving albumin instead since both are 'plasma products'. What is wrong with that?

Answer

They do completely different jobs. Plasma contains the coagulation, fibrinolytic and complement proteins and is given to correct a coagulation factor deficiency. Albumin is a single purified protein, manufactured from pooled plasma and pathogen-inactivated; it is a colloid volume expander and contains no coagulation factors at all. Giving albumin to a coagulopathic bleeding patient replaces volume while doing nothing for hemostasis.

Objective 2 · evidence-based transfusion

Red Cells — and the Trial That Changed Everything

This section is really about evidence. The instinct — see a low number, fix the number — was tested in a randomized trial, and the trial found the opposite of what everyone expected.

🔑 What red cells are for, and what they are not for
  • Increase oxygen-carrying capacity.
  • Increase red cell mass after significant hemorrhage.
  • Chronic anemia when other treatments have failed — for example the chronic transfusion programs in sickle cell disease and thalassemia major.
  • NOT for wound healing. NOT to shorten time to ambulation. Both were once believed and neither is supported.

The case that opens the argument

Case A — 26-year-old woman in the intensive care unit

🧍

Presentation

  • Sepsis, in the intensive care unit
  • Afebrile at present, with stable vital signs
  • Not bleeding — euvolemic
🧪

Laboratory

  • Hematocrit 27% normal 37–47
  • Hemoglobin 9 g/dL normal 12–16
  • Platelet count 147,000/µL normal 150–400k

Q: Does she need a red cell transfusion?

Answer

No.

Compared with the reference range her hematocrit looks clearly low, and until the late 1990s she would have received two units without much thought — because two units would raise her toward the normal range. That practice was tested and found to be wrong.

She is euvolemic (not actively bleeding), hemodynamically stable, and her hemoglobin is above 7 g/dL. Under a restrictive strategy she does not meet a transfusion threshold. She has plenty of other problems — but transfusion is not the treatment for any of them.

The sidebar the lecture insisted on: how we know

⏪

Retrospective study

You identify people who already had the outcome — say, a stroke — and look backward for risk factors.

Valuable and much easier to do as a student. But when testing a treatment it is vulnerable to bias, because you are selecting patients who already had the problem.

⏩

Prospective study

You do something to a group of people and then watch what happens forward in time.

The randomized clinical trial is the gold-standard form: take a group of similar patients, flip a coin for each one to assign treatment or control, and compare. It is the best tool medicine has for deciding whether something works — and it is expensive, slow and hard, which is why we do not have one for every question.

🩺 Where to look

Practical advice: the best randomized trials are often published in the New England Journal of Medicine and JAMA. If you are choosing two journals to follow, follow those. And note that trialists give their studies memorable acronyms on purpose — so you can remember them.

⚠️ The finding was a genuine surprise, and it reset the field

Clinicians training as this landed described the challenge of implementing it: we used to just give everybody two units and not think about it. This really changed the paradigm.

More blood was not better. More blood was worse. The default in a stable, non-bleeding patient is now a restrictive threshold of 7 g/dL, and to transfuse one unit at a time and reassess rather than ordering two reflexively.

🪝 Memory hook — Two reasons transfusion is never free

When a student answered that blood is a critical resource, that is right, and there is a second half. There are two independent reasons not to transfuse without an indication:

  • Supply. Blood cannot be manufactured. Every unit came from a volunteer, and there is a finite number of them.
  • Harm. Every unit carries a risk of transfusion reaction and a residual infectious risk. Historically that meant HIV and hepatitis, and thousands of people — hemophiliacs above all — died of it.

So the question is never is the number low? It is will this unit help this patient more than it might hurt them?

🩺 What this looks like on the wards in 2027

When you put a transfusion order into the electronic medical record, it may stop you: this patient's hemoglobin is above threshold — do you really need a transfusion? Transfusion thresholds have been coded into ordering systems specifically to change prescriber behaviour toward evidence-based practice.

Q: A hemodynamically stable postoperative patient has a hemoglobin of 8.1 g/dL and is asymptomatic. The surgical resident orders two units of red cells 'to help her heal and get her walking sooner'. Give two reasons this is wrong.

Answer

First, the indication is invalid. Red cells are given to increase oxygen-carrying capacity — explicitly not for wound healing or to shorten time to ambulation. Both were once believed and neither is supported.

Second, the threshold is wrong. The TRICC trial randomized 838 euvolemic intensive care patients to a restrictive (<7) versus liberal (<10) strategy and found no difference in 30-day mortality, with the liberal arm doing worse. At 8.1 g/dL in a stable, asymptomatic patient, the correct action is no transfusion — and if one were indicated, one unit, then reassess, not two reflexively.

Q: Why was it important that the patients in the TRICC trial were euvolemic?

Answer

Because the trial was asking about anemia, not hemorrhage. An actively bleeding patient is losing whole blood — plasma volume, coagulation factors and platelets along with red cells — and needs volume resuscitation and the full range of products regardless of the hemoglobin, which in early bleeding can still look deceptively normal. Restricting the trial to euvolemic patients isolated the question of how anemic is too anemic from the entirely separate question of how do you resuscitate someone who is bleeding out. The restrictive 7 g/dL threshold does not apply to active hemorrhage.

Objective 2 · evidence-based transfusion

Platelets — Why the Threshold Is 10,000

The same evidence story, with an even more counterintuitive number. The normal platelet count is 150,000. The prophylactic transfusion threshold is 10,000.

🔑 Indications
  • Thrombocytopenia — a low platelet count.
  • Platelet function disorders — a normal count but platelets that do not work.
  • Not for general prophylaxis to prevent bleeding outside these settings, though doctors often try.
🧠 Why platelets matter — the first phase of hemostasis

When a vessel wall is cut, the subendothelial matrix is exposed. The collagen in it is the stimulus that starts hemostasis, and the first cells to arrive are platelets. They adhere and spread over the exposed collagen — that is primary hemostasis. The coagulation factors then build a fibrin mesh over the top to hold it — secondary hemostasis.

Platelets first, mesh second. That order is why platelet count and coagulation tests are treated as separate problems with separate products.

The case

Case B — 16-year-old with acute lymphoblastic leukemia

🧍

Presentation

  • Acute lymphoblastic leukemia, status post stem cell transplant
  • Awaiting count recovery
  • Nausea and fatigue; stable vital signs; febrile; has lost her hair
  • Not bleeding
🧪

Laboratory

  • White cell count profoundly low — a number you only see after chemotherapy or transplant. She cannot fight infection and cannot safely be out in public
  • Platelet count 7,000/µL normal 150–400k

Q: Does she need a platelet transfusion?

Answer

Yes.

She is the archetype for the guideline: a non-bleeding patient with hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia — meaning she is not making platelets, because of chemotherapy and stem cell transplant — and her count is below 10,000.

Note how far below normal that threshold sits. We do not transfuse toward the normal range; we transfuse at the point where the risk of spontaneous bleeding becomes real. Counts like this are seen every day at a pediatric cancer centre.

The evidence

🧠 The bleeding time relationship

The bleeding time — an old-fashioned test measuring how long a standardized cut takes to stop bleeding — is not used clinically anymore, but it made the point. As the platelet count falls, the bleeding time becomes prolonged, confirming that platelets really are essential to the first stage of hemostasis.

But essential does not tell you at what number to intervene. That took a trial.

🪝 Memory hook — “Ten for chemo, twenty for a tap, twenty-five for a preemie”

Three numbers, in ascending order of how much you are willing to risk:

  • 10,000 — a stable non-bleeding patient who simply is not making platelets. Nothing is about to be done to them.
  • 20,000 — you are about to put a needle into the spinal canal, where a hematoma would be catastrophic.
  • 25,000 — a preterm neonate, whose germinal matrix vessels are fragile and where intraventricular hemorrhage is devastating.

And the fourth entry is the one that teaches the principle: in dengue the thrombocytopenia is consumptive, not hypoproliferative — the platelets are being used up, so transfused platelets are consumed too. Treat the mechanism, not the number.

Q: A patient with immune thrombocytopenic purpura has a platelet count of 8,000 and no bleeding. Should you transfuse platelets on the basis of the 10,000 threshold?

Answer

No — the threshold does not apply here. The 10,000 figure is specifically for hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia — a marrow that is not producing platelets, as after chemotherapy or stem cell transplant. In immune thrombocytopenic purpura the mechanism is peripheral destruction by autoantibody: the marrow is making platelets normally and the antibody is destroying them. Transfused platelets are destroyed just as fast. Treatment is directed at the immune process — corticosteroids, IVIG, thrombopoietin receptor agonists — with platelet transfusion reserved for serious active bleeding. This is the same logic as the dengue recommendation: consumptive thrombocytopenia is not treated by adding platelets.

Objective 2–3 · hemostatic products

Plasma and Cryoprecipitate — the Ocean and the Teacup

The lecture perseverated on this section deliberately, and said so: plasma tends to be a product we use when we do not need it. The reason is a single number — 30% — and one very good metaphor.

🔑 What plasma is
  • All of the proteins of the coagulation, fibrinolytic and complement systems, in a bag, frozen.
  • Called fresh frozen plasma (FFP), or just plasma. Stored frozen for a year and thawed before use.
  • Used to correct coagulopathy — a multiple factor deficiency.
  • NOT used for single factor deficiencies. If a patient is factor VIII deficient (hemophilia A), you give factor VIII, not plasma. We have specific factor concentrates now.

The 30% rule

Why the same two units of plasma are useless in one patient and decisive in another.

🧠 The single number that governs plasma transfusion

You only need about 30% coagulation factor activity to form a clot. Above that line hemostasis is adequate; below it, it is not.

Now the metaphor from lecture. Your circulating plasma is an ocean. A unit of transfused plasma is a teacup.

  • If your ocean already has 50–60% factor activity, pouring in a teacup changes essentially nothing — and it did not need changing, because you could already clot. Who cares? It already works.
  • If your ocean is down at 10–15%, that same teacup — and a few more — actually moves the needle.

Translated into the number you will actually see: an INR of 1.1 corresponds to activity well above the threshold. Giving plasma for an INR of 1.1 is waste plus exposure to risk. A PT around 2 to 2.2 times normal is roughly where you reach 30%.

The case

Case C — 76-year-old man on warfarin

🧍

Presentation

  • Atrial fibrillation, anticoagulated with warfarin
  • Sudden onset of severe headache with mental status changes
  • Clinical concern: intracranial hemorrhage
🧪

Laboratory

  • Prothrombin time 30 s normal ~11–13
  • INR 2.7 normal ~1.0
  • Partial thromboplastin time 34 s normal ~25–35

Q: Does he need plasma — and is plasma the best available option?

Answer

He needs urgent reversal — but plasma is probably not the best way to do it.

His INR of 2.7 is genuinely low enough to matter, unlike the INR-of-1.1 patient who needs nothing. Warfarin inhibits the vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X, so he has a multiple factor deficiency and a possible intracranial bleed.

But prothrombin complex concentrates — 3-factor and 4-factor — are more efficacious than plasma for warfarin reversal. They are essentially processed, concentrated plasma factors: they work faster, in a far smaller volume (important in a 76-year-old with atrial fibrillation who cannot tolerate volume overload), and they achieve a more reliable INR correction.

And whichever product you choose, also give vitamin K. The concentrate corrects the INR now; vitamin K restores the patient's own ability to make the factors, so the INR does not rebound as the concentrate clears.

🔑 Indications for plasma — the whole list
  • A documented factor deficiency AND active bleeding. Both halves are required.
  • A markedly prolonged coagulation test in a patient about to undergo a procedure.
  • Warfarin reversal — but also give vitamin K, and consider prothrombin complex concentrate first.
⚠️ The commonest plasma error

Someone sees an INR of 1.1, or 1.3, and orders plasma to “normalize” it. Look at the diagram again: at that INR the patient is far above the 30% threshold and can already form a clot. The transfusion achieves nothing measurable, while exposing them to a transfusion reaction and an infectious risk. You are not treating the patient; you are treating the number.

Cryoprecipitate

Case D — 27-year-old woman, postpartum

🧍

Presentation

  • Just delivered her third baby
  • Vaginal bleeding — obstetric hemorrhage
  • Already transfused 3 units of red cells and 1 unit of plasma
  • Still bleeding
🧪

Laboratory

  • Fibrinogen 90 mg/dL normal ~200–400
  • Other coagulation values deranged in keeping with ongoing hemorrhage

Q: Which product does she need?

Answer

Cryoprecipitate.

The value that decides it is the fibrinogen of 90. Cryoprecipitate is the bagged product for fibrinogen replacement — and fibrinogen is what actually cross-links the clot at the bottom of the common pathway. Without it, the rest of the cascade has nothing to build with.

Note the second point the lecture made: she was bleeding, and when you bleed you lose whole blood — which is why she had already received red cells and plasma, not red cells alone.

And while she continues to bleed she needs everything — red cells, plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitate in rotation — until the uterus stops bleeding. Women who hemorrhage after childbirth drop into a low-fibrinogen state very quickly, which is why cryoprecipitate matters so much in obstetric bleeding specifically.

🩺 Massive transfusion protocols — why the blood bank stops asking questions

When a patient is exsanguinating, the blood bank does not want a conversation about which product you would like next. A massive transfusion protocol is activated with a single call, and the blood bank prepares a standard box of all the products and sends it to the floor — so you can give them fast, in a balanced ratio, without a round trip for each decision.

The reasoning is blunt: otherwise the patient may die of hemorrhage while you are ordering.

Q: A patient with an INR of 1.2 is scheduled for a liver biopsy. The proceduralist requests two units of plasma 'to be safe'. What is your response?

Answer

Decline, and explain the physiology. Roughly 30% coagulation factor activity is sufficient for hemostasis, and an INR of 1.2 corresponds to activity well above that threshold — this patient can already form a clot. Because the patient's own plasma volume is an ocean and a unit of plasma is a teacup, two units will produce essentially no measurable change in the INR. You would be accepting a transfusion reaction risk, an infectious risk, and a volume load in exchange for no hemostatic benefit. Plasma is indicated for a documented factor deficiency with bleeding, or a markedly prolonged coagulation test before a procedure — not for mild INR elevations.

Q: A patient with disseminated intravascular coagulation after obstetric hemorrhage has a fibrinogen of 80 mg/dL. Why is plasma alone an inadequate answer?

Answer

Because plasma is not a concentrated source of fibrinogen — it contains fibrinogen at physiologic concentration, so correcting a profound deficiency would require a volume the patient cannot tolerate. Cryoprecipitate is the cold-insoluble fraction of thawed plasma, enriched in fibrinogen (along with factor VIII, von Willebrand factor and factor XIII), and delivers the same fibrinogen in a fraction of the volume. Since fibrinogen becoming fibrin is the step that actually cross-links the clot, replacing it is what allows any of the rest of the cascade to produce a stable clot.

Objective 4 · immunohematology

Blood Groups and Antibodies

A warning up front: this is the part that feels hardest when you first learn it, and it is worth hearing twice. But the whole field reduces to one idea applied over and over — self versus non-self: which antigens you express, which antibodies you have, and what happens when they meet.

🔑 Far more complicated than ABO
  • A blood group is essentially anything on the red cell surface that could provoke an immune response in someone else.
  • There are about 30 blood group systems — 30 different gene systems — and over 300 different blood group antigens.
  • Major: ABO and Rh (Rhesus; blood bankers usually just say D).
  • Minor: Duffy, Kidd, Kell, Lewis, MNS and many more.

Transfusion medicine was, literally, the first personalized medicine — and the first forensic test.

ABO — genetics

🧠 Chromosome 9, three alleles
  • The ABO gene sits on chromosome 9.
  • A and B are codominant alleles; O is recessive.
  • There are two antigens (A and B) and four phenotypes: A, B, AB and O.

So an A parent who is heterozygous (AO) and a B parent who is heterozygous (BO) can produce a child of any of the four types — and a child is group O only if they inherit two copies of the recessive O allele.

🔑 Why you already have antibodies you were never transfused with

This is the fact that makes ABO uniquely dangerous. A newborn has no ABO antibodies. Over the first six months to two years of life the gut is colonized by bacteria — Escherichia coli and others — whose surface carbohydrates resemble the A and B antigens.

Your immune system makes antibody against the antigen you do NOT have. So by early childhood everybody carries the antibodies in the right-hand column above, predictably and without any transfusion or pregnancy exposure. The framing from lecture: you are an ocean of anti-B antibody if you are group A. Give that person group B blood and the reaction is immediate.

🪝 Memory hook — Front type and back type must agree

The blood bank types you twice, in opposite directions, as a built-in cross-check:

  • Front type — test the patient's red cells for A and B antigen. This gives the phenotype.
  • Back type — test the patient's plasma for anti-A and anti-B. This must be the mirror image.

Group A front-types as A and back-types with anti-B. If the two do not agree, something is wrong — a mislabeled sample, a recent transfusion, or an unusual subgroup — and the blood bank stops and investigates before releasing anything.

Two ways an antibody destroys transfused red cells

💥

ABO incompatibility → INTRAVASCULAR hemolysis

The ABO antibodies are IgM, and IgM is very good at activating complement.

  • IgM cross-links the incompatible red cells and fixes complement.
  • Complement is activated to completion and cells are lysed inside the vessel.
  • Free heme spills into the plasma and reaches the kidney — the mechanism of acute kidney injury and death.
  • These antibodies are naturally occurring and present in everyone, so this reaction needs no prior exposure.
🫧

Alloantibody (e.g. anti-D) → EXTRAVASCULAR hemolysis

Antibodies formed after exposure to foreign red cells are IgG.

  • IgG coats (opsonizes) the red cell rather than lysing it in the vessel.
  • The tagged cells are cleared in the spleen.
  • Slower, less explosive — but still causes hemolysis and transfusion failure.
  • Requires prior exposure, so it can only follow transfusion or pregnancy.

RhD and hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn

  1. A D-negative person receives D-positive bloodThey lack the D antigen, so it is foreign. They may form anti-D.
  2. Nothing happens — this timeIf they never receive another transfusion, forming the antibody alone causes no harm. This is called red cell sensitization, or alloimmunization (allo- meaning different).
  3. The next exposure is the problemGive D-positive blood again and the anti-D now causes hemolysis. This is why the blood bank must detect existing antibodies before every transfusion.
  4. And in pregnancy it is worseIf a D-negative woman with anti-D carries a D-positive fetus, the IgG anti-D crosses the placenta and destroys fetal red cells — hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn.
🔑 The rule that follows, and the arithmetic behind it

Only about 15% of the population is RhD negative, so RhD-negative units are relatively scarce. The blood bank therefore rations them:

  • Save RhD-negative units for females of childbearing potential. If a girl or woman forms anti-D, a future pregnancy with a D-positive fetus can be seriously harmed.
  • For male trauma patients, give RhD-positive when supply is tight. They will never carry a pregnancy, so the consequence of alloimmunization is far smaller.
  • Group O RhD-negative is only about 6% of people — roughly 1 in 15. If that is your type, the donor center will call you forever, because it is the emergency-release product.

And the emergency reality: if you have just been hit by a truck and arrive in the emergency department, we do not test your blood — we give you group O immediately, because otherwise you may die while we type it. If you are male, we will give O positive and save the O negative.

Q: A group A patient is mistakenly transfused with group B red cells. Describe the immunology, and name the organ most at risk.

Answer

A group A person has naturally occurring anti-B, formed in infancy from gut bacterial colonization — no prior transfusion needed. That antibody is IgM, which is highly efficient at fixing complement. It cross-links the transfused B cells and activates the complement cascade to completion, lysing them inside the blood vessels — an acute intravascular hemolytic transfusion reaction.

The organ most at risk is the kidney: free heme released into the plasma is nephrotoxic, causing tubular injury and acute kidney injury. This reaction can be fatal and is classified as a never event.

Q: Why is a woman who is RhD negative treated differently from a man who is RhD negative when blood is scarce?

Answer

Because alloimmunization has a second, larger consequence in someone who may become pregnant. If a D-negative female of childbearing potential forms anti-D, that antibody is IgG and therefore crosses the placenta. In a future pregnancy with a D-positive fetus it will destroy fetal red cells — hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn. A man cannot carry a pregnancy, so his risk is limited to difficulty with future transfusions. Since only 15% of the population is RhD negative, blood banks preserve scarce D-negative units for girls and women and give D-positive to male trauma patients.

Compatibility Testing — Type and Screen, Type and Cross, Coombs

Two phrases you will say on the wards without necessarily knowing what you are asking for. Here is what the blood bank actually does when you say them.

🔑 What the blood bank must get right, every time
  1. The ABO typeBecause getting it wrong can kill the patient — everyone has naturally occurring ABO antibodies.
  2. The RhD typeTo avoid alloimmunizing a D-negative patient, especially a girl or woman.
  3. Any unexpected antibodiesThe alloantibodies formed from previous transfusion or pregnancy. If you have never been transfused and never been pregnant, you will not have them.

The two phrases

🔍

Type and SCREEN

Determine the ABO and RhD type, and screen the patient's plasma for unexpected antibodies.

The blood bank now knows what you would need and is ready to transfuse — but no specific units have been set aside. This is what you order when transfusion is possible but not planned.

🏷️

Everything above, PLUS crossmatch actual units and reserve them for this patient.

This is what the surgical attending means by type and cross four units for the spine case tomorrow — physical units are labelled and held, so the transporter can collect them in the morning. Order this when transfusion is expected.

🪝 Memory hook — Screen = ready. Cross = reserved.

Type and screen means the blood bank is prepared. Type and cross means specific bags have your patient's name on them and are sitting in the refrigerator waiting. Crossmatched units are unavailable to anyone else, so over-ordering type-and-cross wastes a scarce resource — which is why the distinction is worth getting right.

The antibody screen — the indirect antiglobulin test

The two Coombs tests are photo negatives of each other — one asks about the patient's cells, the other about the patient's plasma.

🧠 How the screen actually works
  1. Add reagent red blood cellsCommercially produced cells with known antigen profiles — real human red cells with a published list of which antigens each one carries.
  2. Mix with the patient's plasmaIf the patient has an antibody against an antigen on those cells, it binds.
  3. Add Coombs reagent — anti-IgGIgG bound to a cell is too small to see. The anti-IgG reagent cross-links the coated cells to each other, building clumps large enough to be visible.
  4. Read for agglutinationClumping = POSITIVE — an antibody is present. No clumping = NEGATIVE — no unexpected antibodies.
🩺 What happens when the screen is positive

The blood bank runs an antibody identification panel: a set of reagent cells, each with a different published antigen profile, laid out in a grid. The technologist records which cells the patient's plasma reacts with — say cells 1, 2 and 5 — and then works out by process of elimination which single antigen those three cells share and the non-reacting cells lack.

That names the antibody. From then on the patient must receive units that are negative for that antigen — for the rest of their life, since the antibody may later fall below detection but will re-emerge on re-exposure. This is the work behind a phrase like the crossmatch is taking a while.

Q: A patient with a known anti-Kell antibody needs two units of red cells. Why does this take longer than usual, and what must the units be?

Answer

The units must be Kell antigen negative as well as ABO and RhD compatible, and antigen-negative units must be identified and often sourced from a wider inventory or a reference laboratory, then individually crossmatched. That takes time.

The antibody was formed by previous transfusion or pregnancy — the only two routes of exposure to foreign red cells. It is IgG, so re-exposure would cause extravascular hemolysis with splenic clearance of the transfused cells. Importantly, the requirement is lifelong: the antibody titre can fall below detection over time, but it will rise rapidly on re-exposure, which is why patients carry antibody cards.

Q: A patient develops anemia and jaundice. The direct antiglobulin test is positive. What does this tell you, and what does it NOT tell you?

Answer

It tells you the patient's red cells are coated with antibody — so the hemolysis is immune-mediated rather than due to an intrinsic membrane, enzyme or hemoglobin defect. That distinguishes it immediately from hereditary spherocytosis, G6PD deficiency, thalassemia and sickle cell disease.

It does not tell you why. A positive direct antiglobulin test is compatible with autoimmune hemolytic anemia (warm or cold), a delayed or acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, or a drug-induced immune hemolysis. It also does not tell you the antibody's specificity — that needs further work-up.

Donor selection — the harder half of safety

🧠 The lesson of the 1980s and 1990s

Testing is only half the system. The other half is who you collect from, and that lesson was learned at enormous cost.

Historically, blood collection centres were sometimes placed in poor neighbourhoods and donors were paid. Paid donors had a higher rate of infectious disease — and, critically, if you induce people to donate, they are not truthful on the screening questionnaire. One lecturer called it human economics. Thousands of people, hemophiliacs above all, died of HIV and hepatitis acquired from blood products.

The resulting mantra in the field: we only want volunteer donors.

🩺 Why the donor questionnaire is so invasive

If you have donated, you have answered detailed questions about sexual activity, drug use, who you live with, travel, tattoos and medical history. Each question maps to a documented infectious risk. The questionnaire and the laboratory testing are two independent layers, because neither is perfect on its own — testing has a window period, and self-report has limits.

🧬 Voluntary versus replacement donation — a global divide

Much of the world does not have a culture of voluntary donation the way the United States, Canada, Europe and parts of Asia do. Instead they rely on replacement donors.

How that works in practice: you bring your child to a hospital and they need blood for malaria. The blood bank may hold only a few units. You may not be allowed to leave until you bring an uncle or a relative to donate and replace what your child used. It has been called subsistence blood banking.

Two consequences follow: the supply never builds a reserve, and the donors are — by definition — induced, which is exactly the condition that made paid donation unsafe.

Q: A patient refuses a needed transfusion because she is afraid of contracting HIV. How do you counsel her?

Answer

Acknowledge that the fear is historically grounded — thousands of people did acquire HIV and hepatitis from blood products, and hemophiliacs were devastated. Then give her the actual numbers: the residual risk of transfusion-transmitted HIV is now roughly comparable to being struck by lightning.

Explain the two layers of protection: every unit is tested for a panel of transmissible diseases and any positive unit is discarded, and the supply comes exclusively from unpaid volunteer donors who complete a detailed health questionnaire — a system built specifically in response to the 1980s. It is also honest to add that the more common infectious risk today is a bacterially contaminated platelet, not a virus, and that non-infectious reactions are far more common than either.

Q: Two reactions produce this picture. Name both, and say which is common and which is dangerous.

Answer

Septic transfusion reaction versus febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reaction.

The dangerous one — septic transfusion reaction. Bacterial contamination of the product. The usual route is startling: when the donor's arm is accessed, the bore of the needle punches out a tiny plug of skin, and everyone's skin carries flora. That plug lands in the bag and, because platelets are stored at room temperature, the bacteria grow. Three countermeasures now exist: a diversion pouch that captures and discards the first ~100 mL of the donation, scrupulous arm preparation, and pathogen reduction.

The common one — febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reaction. Centres like this see these multiple times a week. The product is living cells in a bag, and while they survive they are not entirely happy — they release cytokines. Give those cytokines to a patient and they can produce a fever. It is self-limited and, as the name says, there is no hemolysis.

White cells are the main cytokine source, which is why leukocyte reduction — filtering white cells out before bagging — drops the febrile reaction rate sharply. It is now the standard of care for essentially every product transfused in the United States.

Everything downstream is flawlessly wrong.

This is why hospitals are hyper-vigilant about sample labelling at the bedside and why the clerical check is step 3 in the reaction algorithm — it is where this error is caught.

🧠 Two mechanisms, one spectrum
  • The strawberry model. The way this was explained in lecture: the donor ate strawberries. A soluble allergen from the donor's diet is present in the plasma, the recipient has pre-formed anti-strawberry IgE, and they react. A hypersensitivity reaction — usually just rash and itching.
  • IgA deficiency. The mechanism behind true anaphylaxis. Every plasma-containing product contains IgA. A patient who is IgA deficient and has formed anti-IgA can react anaphylactically to a tiny volume — note that Case 8 reacted to 5 mL. These patients need washed or IgA-deficient products.

TRALI — transfusion-related acute lung injury

🔑 A mechanism worth knowing, and a public health success
  1. A woman becomes pregnantDuring pregnancy she is exposed to fetal cells carrying paternal tissue antigens.
  2. She makes anti-HLA antibodiesThese are anti-tissue antibodies. They do not harm her at all — she simply has them. 14–20% of women who have been pregnant do.
  3. More pregnancies, more antibodyEach pregnancy adds to the repertoire.
  4. She becomes a blood donorHer plasma now carries a load of anti-HLA antibody into the product.
  5. The recipient's white cells are the targetIf those antibodies react with the recipient's leukocytes, the cells agglutinate in the pulmonary capillaries — leukoagglutination in the lungs — and the result is acute lung injury.
🩺 What it looks like, and why you almost never see it now

Radiographically it is ARDS: bilateral pulmonary opacities with a hypoxic patient. It can cause death.

Then the mitigation, which was offered as a real success story: blood centres now test female donors for anti-HLA antibodies and avoid making high-plasma-volume products from donors who have them. As put in lecture — this used to kill people a generation ago and now we never see it. Not never in principle, but genuinely rare.

Q: What happened, who is at risk, and how is it prevented?

Answer

Transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease. He died.

You have met graft-versus-host disease in the context of stem cell transplant, where a new stem cell source attacks the recipient. Here the graft is the blood product itself: residual donor lymphocytes in the unit engraft and proliferate in the patient, then attack the recipient's tissues — including the bone marrow, which is why every cell line collapsed.

Who is at risk — two groups:
• Patients with a severely dysfunctional immune system, who cannot reject the donor lymphocytes. The extreme example is a baby born with no immune system at all.
• Patients whose donor partially matches their HLA type — which is why directed donation from relatives is discouraged. If a mother is HLA A2/A3 and her child inherited A3 from her and A3 from the father, the child's immune system tolerates the mother's cells as sufficiently self, while the mother's donor lymphocytes recognize the child's paternal antigen as foreign and attack. One-way recognition.

Prevention: irradiate the product. Blood banks serving children and cancer patients keep an irradiator on site. Irradiation means that any residual white cells in the bag cannot proliferate in the recipient. It is the only defence, and it is why the take-home message is simply: irradiate the product.

Treatment is largely futile — the only rescue is a stem cell transplant, and it is very hard to get one in time.

Q: A patient becomes acutely dyspneic and hypoxic one hour into a transfusion, with bilateral pulmonary infiltrates. Blood pressure is 88/50 and the jugular venous pressure is not elevated. What is the diagnosis and what is the mechanism?

Answer

TRALI — transfusion-related acute lung injury. The discriminating features against TACO are the hypotension and the normal jugular venous pressure; circulatory overload would produce hypertension with a raised JVP and would respond to diuresis, which TRALI does not.

Mechanism: the donor — classically a woman sensitized during pregnancy, since 14–20% of parous women carry anti-HLA antibodies — passed anti-HLA antibodies in the plasma. These react with the recipient's leukocytes, causing leukoagglutination in the pulmonary capillaries and acute lung injury with an ARDS picture. Management is supportive. It has become rare because blood centres now screen female donors and avoid making high-plasma-volume products from those with antibodies.

Q: Why is irradiation of blood products required for a severely immunocompromised child, and what does irradiation actually do?

Answer

To prevent transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease. Blood products contain residual donor lymphocytes. A patient with a competent immune system rejects them; a severely immunocompromised patient cannot, so the donor lymphocytes engraft and proliferate, then attack the recipient's tissues including the bone marrow — producing pancytopenia, rash, diarrhea and liver dysfunction, usually fatally.

Irradiation damages the DNA of the residual white cells so they cannot proliferate in the recipient. The red cells, which have no nucleus, still function. Irradiation is also required for products from related donors, because partial HLA matching allows the donor lymphocytes to escape recognition while still recognizing the recipient as foreign.

Global Transfusion Medicine

A field of active research, and the part of the lecture most worth hearing: when we practise in the United States, the adequacy of the blood supply is simply not on our radar — because there is enough blood in every hospital we will work in. That is not true for most of the world.

🧬 Where transfusion began

James Blundell, an obstetrician, is credited with the first successful human-to-human blood transfusion. He gave a husband's blood to a woman who was dying of postpartum hemorrhage.

That origin is not incidental. For the whole of human history before it, women bled to death in childbirth as a matter of course — and as the next section shows, in much of the world they still do, for want of the thing Blundell demonstrated two centuries ago.

Martinho

🩺 A clinic in Angola

He was essentially dead, suffering from severe anemia due to malaria. In the clinic's phrase, his blood was as thin as tea.

There was no stocked blood bank. So the walking blood bank protocol was activated: fresh, tested, compatible whole blood was collected from a donor — his father. (Note the tension with everything in the previous section about related donors and graft-versus-host disease. In a setting with no irradiator and no alternative, you take the risk in front of you over the certainty of death.)

After transfusion he was eating and walking, and was discharged the next day. He also received antimalarial medicine.

Why the need is different

1

Unsupported childbirth

Bleeding is the number one cause of maternal mortality, and most of it occurs in sub-Saharan Africa. A great deal of childbirth happens with no blood bank within reach.

2

Malaria

Severe malarial anemia in children is one of the largest single drivers of transfusion demand in endemic countries — Martinho's diagnosis.

3

Trauma

Motor vehicle accidents, just as on roads everywhere.

4

Endemic disease

In northern Kenya, where these programs work, visceral leishmaniasis — kala-azar, transmitted by a fly bite — is one of the most common reasons for transfusion.

The modeling study — need, supply, and the gap

🔑 What the study did and what it found
  1. Model the needUsing epidemiological databases, estimate how much blood each country's population and disease burden actually requires, by patient population and diagnosis. A large data study.
  2. Measure the supplyEvery country reports to the World Health Organization how much blood it collects annually. Map it.
  3. Do the gap analysisSubtract. This was, in the lecture's phrase, the money shot.
  • Green — supply meets or exceeds need. Light green — roughly twice the needed supply.
  • Dark red — need exceeds supply by up to 75-fold. South Sudan, ravaged by decades of warfare, is the extreme case.
  • Most of sub-Saharan Africa falls short, and India stands out too.
  • A useful surprise: some countries that look alarming on a raw-supply map — Russia, for example — actually have enough blood in aggregate. It is simply not always in the right place.

The paper was picked up worldwide, including by NPR — because the finding is invisible from inside a well-supplied system.

The gold mine study — public health as transfusion medicine

🧠 Why a mining company built a malaria control program

Early in a blood banking career, a visit to Ghana turned up a hospital owned by a gold mine — which seems odd until you understand the economics. The company's workers, the men going down into the mine, were constantly sick with malaria. So the company built the Obuasi Integrated Malaria Control Program: laboratories testing mosquitoes against larvicides, and teams spraying regularly to suppress the vector.

But it did not only protect the workers. It reduced malaria in the surrounding women and children too — which is what made it studiable.

🪝 Memory hook — The most useful idea in this whole section

The mine spent that money to keep its workforce productive. The result was a 40% reduction in malaria and a 12.8% reduction in the need for blood transfusion in children.

The most effective intervention on the blood supply was not a blood bank intervention at all. When blood is scarce and not fully testable, preventing the disease that consumes it beats collecting more of it. That is why the argument is that public health and healthcare infrastructure are transfusion medicine.

🩺 Blood Safe

A program called Blood Safe works across three African countries to improve transfusion safety and access — one example of the applied side of this field, and a reminder that laboratory medicine has a global health arm you can actually build a career in.

The closing slide

🔑 Five things to carry out of this lecture
  • Blood donation and the blood supply are critical public health resources.
  • Everything advanced depends on them. Stem cell transplant, gene therapy, ECMO, major surgery, heart transplantation — none of it happens without a blood bank and a laboratory behind it.
  • The world does not have enough blood where it is needed — decisively outside the United States, and not always here either.
  • Prevalent local diagnoses drive demand. Sickle cell disease is a case in point: the highest disease burden is in Africa, which also has the least access to blood banking.
  • Be an evidence-informed doctor. Sometimes we should not be transfusing when we think we should — and most transfusion reactions are not infectious. Take care of your patient, then call your blood bank doctor.

Q: Why does sickle cell disease illustrate the global blood supply problem better than almost any other diagnosis?

Answer

Because the disease burden and the resource are inversely distributed. Sickle cell disease prevalence and mortality are highest in sub-Saharan Africa — the same region where the gap between blood need and blood supply is largest, reaching up to a 75-fold shortfall in the worst affected countries.

The disease is also unusually transfusion-hungry: chronic transfusion programs to keep HbS below 30% for stroke prevention, exchange transfusion, and acute transfusion for sequestration and aplastic crises. So the population needing the most blood has the least access to it — and where transfusion is available, the infectious testing panel is narrower, so each unit carries more risk. The Ghanaian colleagues summarized the historical outcome bluntly: if they make it to 5, they'll make it to 10.

Glossary and Abbreviations

Every abbreviation and technical term used in this file, expanded and defined.

Products and processing
Whole blood
Blood as donated, not separated into components. Used mainly in military and some trauma settings.
Packed red blood cells
The red cell fraction. Stored at 1–6 °C for up to 42 days. Given for oxygen-carrying capacity.
FFP — fresh frozen plasma
The plasma fraction, frozen. Contains all coagulation, fibrinolytic and complement proteins.
Cryoprecipitate
The cold-insoluble precipitate that forms when frozen plasma is thawed at refrigerated temperature. Rich in fibrinogen, factor VIII, von Willebrand factor and factor XIII.
Apheresis
Drawing blood from a donor, separating one component in line, and returning the rest. Yields a full platelet transfusion from a single donor.
Citrate
The anticoagulant in every blood bag. Works by chelating calcium. A large load during massive transfusion can cause hypocalcemia.
Additive solution
Adenine, mannitol and dextrose added to red cell units — 'red cell food' — extending storage to 42 days.
Leukocyte reduction
Filtering white cells out of a product. Reduces febrile non-hemolytic reactions and HLA alloimmunization. Standard of care in the United States.
Pathogen reduction
Treatment that inactivates residual bacteria, viruses and parasites, used for platelets.
Irradiation
Damaging the DNA of residual donor lymphocytes so they cannot proliferate. The only prevention for transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease.
Prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC)
A processed, concentrated plasma-derived product containing vitamin K-dependent factors. 3-factor and 4-factor forms. Preferred over plasma for urgent warfarin reversal.
IVIG
Intravenous immunoglobulin — pooled, purified human antibody, dispensed from pharmacy.
Freeze-dried plasma
Plasma reconstituted with fluid at the point of use. First FDA approval in the United States in 2025.
MTP — massive transfusion protocol
A single activation that sends a standard box of all products to the bedside, so products can be given fast in a balanced ratio.
Evidence and thresholds
RCT — randomized clinical trial
The gold-standard prospective study: participants are randomly assigned to treatment or control.
Prospective study
Intervene first, then observe forward in time.
Retrospective study
Identify people with an outcome and look backward for causes. More prone to bias.
TRICC trial
1999 randomized trial in 838 euvolemic ICU patients comparing restrictive (<7 g/dL) with liberal (<10 g/dL) transfusion. No mortality difference; the liberal arm did worse.
Restrictive transfusion strategy
Transfusing only below a low hemoglobin threshold, typically 7 g/dL, one unit at a time.
Euvolemic
Normal circulating volume — not actively bleeding. The TRICC threshold does not apply to active hemorrhage.
Hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia
A low platelet count because the marrow is not producing — as after chemotherapy or stem cell transplant. Threshold for prophylactic transfusion: 10,000/µL.
Consumptive thrombocytopenia
A low platelet count from peripheral destruction or consumption. Transfused platelets are consumed too, so the count-based threshold does not apply.
Petechiae
Pinpoint skin hemorrhages from capillary rupture; a sign of low platelets.
Wet purpura
Bruising and bleeding on mucosal surfaces, notably inside the mouth. A more worrying bleeding sign than petechiae.
INR — international normalized ratio
A standardized prothrombin time. Roughly 30% factor activity — the hemostatic threshold — corresponds to a PT about 2–2.2 times normal.
30% rule
Approximately 30% coagulation factor activity is sufficient to form a clot. Above it, plasma transfusion achieves nothing.
Blood groups and testing
Blood group system
A set of red cell antigens produced by one gene system. About 30 systems and over 300 antigens are known.
ABO
The major blood group system, on chromosome 9. A and B are codominant; O is recessive.
Rh / RhD
The Rhesus system; D is its principal antigen. About 15% of people are RhD negative.
Minor blood groups
Duffy, Kidd, Kell, Lewis, MNS and others. A frequent source of alloantibodies in chronically transfused patients.
Front type
Testing the patient's red cells for A and B antigen — the phenotype.
Back type
Testing the patient's plasma for anti-A and anti-B. Must mirror the front type.
Naturally occurring antibody
ABO antibodies formed in infancy from gut bacterial colonization, without transfusion or pregnancy. Mostly IgM and complement-fixing.
Alloantibody / unexpected antibody
An antibody formed after exposure to foreign red cells — only from transfusion or pregnancy. IgG. Present in 1–3% of the general population but 20–60% in sickle cell disease.
Alloimmunization / red cell sensitization
The process of forming such an antibody.
Type and screen
Determine ABO and RhD type and screen the plasma for unexpected antibodies. The blood bank is ready but no units are reserved.
Type and cross
Type and screen plus crossmatching and reserving specific units for that patient.
Crossmatch
Testing a specific donor unit against the patient's plasma before release.
IAT — indirect antiglobulin test
The antibody screen. Patient plasma plus reagent red cells plus anti-IgG; agglutination means an antibody is present.
DAT — direct antiglobulin test (direct Coombs)
Tests whether the patient's own red cells are already antibody-coated. Used to diagnose immune-mediated hemolysis.
Coombs reagent
Anti-IgG. It cross-links antibody-coated cells so that agglutination becomes visible.
Agglutination
Visible clumping of red cells — the positive read-out of both antiglobulin tests.
Antibody identification panel
Reagent cells with known antigen profiles, used to determine an antibody's specificity by process of elimination.
Universal donor / recipient
Group O red cells can go to anyone (no A or B antigen); group AB can receive from anyone (no ABO antibodies). For plasma the relationship inverts — AB is the universal plasma donor.
HDFN
Hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn — maternal IgG crossing the placenta and destroying fetal red cells.
Adverse events
Febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reaction
Fever and chills from cytokines in the product, without hemolysis. Very common; reduced by leukocyte reduction.
Septic transfusion reaction
Bacterial contamination, most often of platelets. Mitigated by the diversion pouch, arm preparation and pathogen reduction.
Diversion pouch
A satellite bag capturing the first ~100 mL of a donation, discarding the skin plug punched out by the needle.
Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction
ABO-incompatible transfusion. IgM fixes complement, causing intravascular hemolysis; free heme threatens the kidney. A never event.
Never event
An error so preventable that it should never occur.
WBIT — wrong blood in tube
Drawing a blood bank sample from one patient and labelling it with another's identity. The classic route to a fatal ABO error.
Allergic transfusion reaction
Hives, flushing and itching with stable vital signs, from soluble allergens in donor plasma.
Anaphylaxis
Severe hypersensitivity with hypotension, dyspnea and throat tightness, sometimes after only a few millilitres. Consider IgA deficiency with anti-IgA.
IgA deficiency
A patient lacking IgA who has formed anti-IgA can react anaphylactically to any plasma-containing product. Needs washed or IgA-deficient products.
TRALI
Transfusion-related acute lung injury. Donor anti-HLA antibodies cause leukoagglutination in pulmonary capillaries, producing an ARDS picture. 14–20% of parous women carry these antibodies.
TACO
Transfusion-associated circulatory overload — volume overload. Distinguished from TRALI by hypertension, raised jugular venous pressure and response to diuresis.
Transfusion-associated GVHD
Donor lymphocytes engrafting and attacking the recipient, including the marrow — pancytopenia, usually fatal. Prevented only by irradiation.
HLA — human leukocyte antigen
The tissue type system. Relevant to TRALI, platelet refractoriness and graft-versus-host disease.
Hemoglobinemia / hemoglobinuria
Free hemoglobin in plasma (pink plasma) / in urine (dark urine). Signatures of intravascular hemolysis.
Global health
Volunteer donor
An unpaid donor. The mantra of the field, learned from the era when paid donation drove untruthful screening answers and transfusion-transmitted HIV.
Replacement donor
A relative or friend who donates to replace blood a patient has used. Common in low- and middle-income countries; described as subsistence blood banking.
Walking blood bank
Collecting fresh whole blood from an on-site donor when no stocked supply exists.
Cold chain
The unbroken refrigeration required from collection to transfusion. A major constraint where power is unreliable.
Kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis)
A parasitic disease transmitted by a fly bite. One of the most common reasons for transfusion in northern Kenya.
Gap analysis
Comparing modeled blood need with reported supply, country by country. Shortfalls reach up to 75-fold in the worst affected countries.
Obuasi Integrated Malaria Control Program
A Ghanaian gold mine's vector control program, associated with a 40% decline in malaria and a 12.8% decline in the need for pediatric transfusion.
ECMO
Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation — one of many advanced therapies that cannot exist without a blood supply.

Products & evidence · LO 1–3

The four products

  • One donation → centrifuge → red cells / platelets / plasma; thaw plasma cold → cryoprecipitate.
  • Red = oxygen. Every yellow product = hemostasis.
  • RBC: 1–6 °C, 42 days. Platelets: room temp, agitated, 5–7 days. Plasma & cryo: frozen, 1 year.
  • In the bag: citrate (chelates calcium → hypocalcemia in massive transfusion), additive solution (adenine, mannitol), breathable plastic.
  • Leukocyte reduction ↓ febrile reactions. Pathogen reduction for platelets. Irradiation prevents GVHD.
  • Pharmacy products from pooled, pathogen-treated plasma: albumin, IVIG, factor concentrates, PCC.

Red cells — TRICC 1999

  • For oxygen-carrying capacity. NOT wound healing, NOT ambulation.
  • 838 euvolemic ICU patients, restrictive <7 vs liberal <10.
  • No difference in 30-day mortality; the LIBERAL arm did WORSE.
  • Default: 7 g/dL, one unit at a time. Does not apply to active hemorrhage.
  • Two reasons never to transfuse casually: finite supply and real harm.

Platelets

  • For thrombocytopenia and platelet function disorders. First phase of hemostasis.
  • 2002 RCT: threshold 20,000 vs 10,000 in transplant patients — no difference.
  • JAMA 2025 guidelines: hypoproliferative non-bleeding 10,000 · lumbar puncture 20,000 · preterm neonate 25,000 · dengue: do not transfuse.
  • Consumptive thrombocytopenia (dengue, ITP) is not fixed by adding platelets.

Plasma & cryoprecipitate

  • Plasma = all coagulation, fibrinolytic, complement proteins. Not for single factor deficiency.
  • 30% factor activity is enough to clot. Above it, plasma is a teacup into an ocean.
  • INR 1.1 → give nothing. PT ~2–2.2× normal ≈ 30%.
  • Indication: documented deficiency AND bleeding; or markedly prolonged test pre-procedure; warfarin reversal + vitamin K.
  • Warfarin inhibits II, VII, IX, X. PCC beats plasma for urgent reversal.
  • Cryo = cold-insoluble fraction, fibrinogen-rich (+ VIII, vWF, XIII). For low fibrinogen, DIC, obstetric hemorrhage, massive transfusion.
Immunohematology · LO 4

Blood groups

  • ~30 systems, >300 antigens. Major: ABO, RhD. Minor: Duffy, Kidd, Kell, Lewis, MNS.
  • ABO on chromosome 9; A and B codominant, O recessive.
  • A → anti-B · B → anti-A · AB → neither · O → both.
  • Antibodies form from gut bacterial colonization in the first 6 months–2 years. No transfusion needed.
  • Front type = cells/antigen. Back type = plasma/antibody. They must mirror.

Two hemolysis routes

  • ABO = IgM = fixes complement = INTRAVASCULAR. Free heme → kidney. Worse.
  • Alloantibody (anti-D etc.) = IgG = opsonizes = EXTRAVASCULAR, cleared in the spleen.
  • Alloantibodies come from only two exposures: transfusion and pregnancy.
  • Frequency 1–3% general, 20–60% in sickle cell disease (repeated exposure to minor antigens).
  • IgG anti-D crosses the placenta → hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn.

Compatibility & supply

  • O = universal RED CELL donor. AB = universal red cell recipient. Inverts for plasma: AB is the universal plasma donor.
  • Only 15% are RhD negative; O negative ≈ 6% (1 in 15).
  • Save O negative for females of childbearing potential; give males O positive.
  • Emergency: give group O without testing — otherwise the patient dies while you type.

Type and screen vs cross · Coombs

  • Type and screen = ABO/RhD + antibody screen. Ready, nothing reserved.
  • Type and cross = plus crossmatch and reserve named units.
  • INDIRECT antiglobulin test = the screen. Patient plasma + reagent cells + anti-IgG.
  • DIRECT antiglobulin test (DAT) = are the patient’s own cells antibody-coated? Diagnostic for immune hemolysis.
  • Positive screen → antibody identification panel → issue antigen-negative units, lifelong.
Adverse events & global · LO 5–6

Infectious risk

  • Most common infectious risk = bacterially contaminated PLATELET (room-temperature storage).
  • HIV risk ≈ being struck by lightning.
  • Every unit is tested; positives are discarded.
  • Volunteer donors only — paying donors made screening answers untruthful and killed thousands in the 1980s–90s.
  • Low- and middle-income countries rely on replacement donors — subsistence blood banking.

Reaction algorithm

  • 1 STOP · 2 keep IV open · 3 clerical check · 4 see and treat · 5 send everything to the blood bank.
  • Stop first because the two lethal reactions are dose dependent.

The reactions

  • Febrile non-hemolytic — fever, no hemolysis. Cytokines. Commonest. ↓ by leukoreduction.
  • Septic — usually platelets; skin plug from the needle bore. ↓ by diversion pouch, arm prep, pathogen reduction.
  • Acute hemolytic (ABO) — back/flank pain, pink plasma, hemoglobinuria, positive DAT. Never event; cause is wrong blood in tube.
  • Allergic — hives, stable vitals. Anaphylaxis — tiny volume, throat tightness; consider IgA deficiency + anti-IgA.
  • TRALI — ARDS picture, hypotension, normal JVP; donor anti-HLA (14–20% of parous women). Prevented by screening female donors.
  • TACO — hypertension, raised JVP, responds to diuresis. Too much volume.
  • Transfusion-associated GVHD — pancytopenia weeks later, usually fatal. Risk: immunodeficiency or a related donor. Prevented only by IRRADIATION.

Global health

  • James Blundell — first successful human-to-human transfusion, for postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Bleeding is the #1 cause of maternal mortality, mostly sub-Saharan Africa. Plus malaria, trauma, kala-azar.
  • Gap analysis: need exceeds supply by up to 75× (South Sudan); India also short. Some countries have enough but not in the right place.
  • Constraints are cold chain and power, not just donors; narrower infectious testing panels.
  • Obuasi malaria control: 40% ↓ malaria → 12.8% ↓ pediatric transfusion need. Preventing the disease beat collecting more blood.
