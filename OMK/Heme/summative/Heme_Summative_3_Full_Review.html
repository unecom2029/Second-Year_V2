1 — Hematopoiesis & Marrow
✗ 4 missed here

Stem cell hierarchy · sites across life · the niche · reading a marrow

Where Blood Is Made, and How to Read the Marrow

Sites across a lifetime

Yolk sac → fetal liver and spleen → bone marrow. The liver is the dominant site for the whole second trimester; marrow begins around month 5 and takes over by birth. Adult marrow is axial — vertebrae, ribs, sternum, pelvis.

  • A hematopoietic stem cell does two things: self-renew and differentiate. Too much self-renewal is how leukemia starts.
  • Myeloid line: red cells, megakaryocyte → platelets, neutrophil, monocyte → macrophage, eosinophil, basophil. Lymphoid line: T cells (mature in thymus), B cells (mature in marrow) → plasma cells, natural killer cells.
  • A platelet is an anucleate membrane fragment — no nucleus means no new enzyme, which is why aspirin lasts the platelet's whole 7–10 day life.
  • Extramedullary hematopoiesis plus skull and facial marrow expansion → frontal bossing and "hair-on-end" skull in thalassemia.

The hierarchy — one stem cell, two lineages, every mature cell downstream.

✗ Missed — where erythropoietin is actually made
You pickedProximal convoluted tubular epithelium
AnswerPeritubular interstitial cells of the renal cortex

Beside the tubule, not inside it. These fibroblast-like cells sit in the interstitium between tubules and capillaries — that position is the design. From there they sense oxygen in blood already delivered to the energy-hungry tubules, making them a true tissue oxygen sensor rather than a pressure sensor.

Keep the kidney's three jobs separate: peritubular interstitium → erythropoietin · proximal tubule → activates vitamin D (the other endocrine failure of kidney disease, giving renal bone disease) · juxtaglomerular cells → renin, which senses pressure.

The buried clue was the low reticulocyte count — it proves a production failure, which is what a missing hormone signal looks like.

🔑 The reticulocyte count is the fork in every anemia

Reticulocytes LOW = a production problem. Reticulocytes HIGH = loss or destruction. Ask this before you ask anything else — it splits the entire anemia differential in one number.

✗ Missed — the niche, and how stem cells are harvested
You pickedP-selectin and its glycoprotein ligand
AnswerCXCR4 and CXCL12 — the drug is plerixafor

The niche is an anchor, not a warehouse. Marrow stromal cells secrete CXCL12 (also called stromal cell-derived factor 1); stem cells carry its receptor CXCR4. That bond is the rope tying them in place. Plerixafor is a reversible CXCR4 blocker — cut the rope and stem cells flood into blood within hours, which is exactly the 12-hour rise the stem described.

Why selectins fail: they mediate white cells rolling onto inflamed endothelium to get into tissue. Wrong compartment, wrong direction.

The marrow biopsy, and the number that means nothing without an age

  • Position and site: prone, posterior superior iliac spine. The aspirate gives cytology (individual cell detail); the core gives architecture and cellularity.
  • Hypercellular → leukemia, or a marrow driven hard to compensate. Hypocellular → aplastic anemia, myelodysplasia, post-chemotherapy.
  • Do not biopsy for a routine anemia — the iron studies and reticulocyte count answer it.
  • Normal myeloid-to-erythroid precursor ratio is about 3 to 1.

AnswerCellularity ≈ 100 − age in years

Right variable, backwards — pattern 1 again. Marrow is progressively replaced by fat with age, so cellularity falls. Newborn ≈ 100% · you in your twenties ≈ 75% · a 70-year-old ≈ 30%.

✗ Missed — the three layers in a spun tube
You pickedPlasma proteins and immunoglobulins
AnswerWhite cells and platelets — the buffy coat

Only particles sediment. Proteins are dissolved — they give the top layer its colour and viscosity but can never form a discrete band. Spinning separates by density: red cells at the bottom (~45%, the hematocrit), white cells and platelets at the interface (~1%), plasma on top (~55%).

One tube is a diagnostic map: bottom = the anemias and polycythemias · middle = the leukemias and low white counts · top = the paraproteins and clotting disorders. A visibly thick buffy coat means marked leukocytosis; milky plasma means fat.

🧠 Reading the complete blood count before anything else
  • Hematocrit ≈ 3 × hemoglobin — but only when the average cell size is normal. Breaking that rule is itself a finding, not noise.
  • Reference ranges: hematocrit 37–47% female, 42–52% male.
  • A wide red cell distribution width means two populations of different sizes sitting side by side.
  • Acute hemorrhage: the initial hemoglobin can be normal — you lose whole blood, so the concentration hasn't changed yet. It falls once plasma refills.

Q: A 62-year-old woman with diabetes has a hemoglobin of 9.2, a reticulocyte count of 0.4%, normal iron studies, and a creatinine of 3.4 mg/dL. Deficient production of the responsible hormone occurs where?

Answer

Peritubular interstitial cells of the renal cortex. Normocytic anemia + low reticulocytes + normal iron + renal failure = the kidney has lost its endocrine function, not just its filtering function.

The Anemias of Underproduction

🔑 The algorithm, in order ★ HY

Three buckets: blood loss · impaired production · increased destruction.

Confirm the anemia → read the average cell size first → then the reticulocyte count → then a targeted study → then look at the smear.

Size bands: under 80 microcytic · 80–100 normocytic · over 100 macrocytic.

The branching algorithm — cell size first, then reticulocytes.

Iron deficiency

  • The row: serum iron ↓, ferritin ↓, total iron-binding capacity ↑, transferrin saturation under 15%, cell size ↓, marrow iron absent. Reactive high platelets often ride along.
  • The stations: duodenum (absorbs ferrous, Fe²⁺, only) → transferrin carries it → cells use it → ferritin stores it.
  • Small AND pale because the precursor divides extra times before it matures, trying to reach a hemoglobin concentration it can't make.
  • Ferritin is an acute phase reactant — inflammation can push it into the normal range and hide a real deficiency.

Anemia of inflammation (anemia of chronic disease) ★ HY

The most common anemia in hospitalized patients. Causes: chronic infection, chronic immune disease, and malignancy — Hodgkin lymphoma by name. It can appear as early as 1–2 months into the illness.

Interleukin-6 → the liver makes hepcidin → hepcidin destroys ferroportin → iron is locked inside macrophages and gut cells. Plus reduced erythropoietin and reduced erythroid proliferation.

Vitamin B12, pernicious anemia and folate

🧠 The one-line split

Vitamin B12 does DNA synthesis AND myelin. Folate does DNA only. B12 has the Brain. That is why you can never give folate alone to an undiagnosed macrocytic anemia — it corrects the blood count while the spinal cord degenerates.

  • Megaloblastic picture: large oval red cells, hypersegmented neutrophils (6 or more lobes), a hypercellular marrow, a low reticulocyte count and a high lactate dehydrogenase — that combination is ineffective erythropoiesis: cells are made and destroyed before they leave.
  • The discriminating metabolite: methylmalonic acid is high in B12 deficiency and normal in folate deficiency. Homocysteine rises in both.
  • The absorption path: pepsin frees it → haptocorrin carries it → pancreatic proteases release it → intrinsic factor binds it → absorbed in the terminal ileum → transcobalamin II carries it in plasma.
  • Pernicious anemia: autoimmune loss of parietal cells → no intrinsic factor. Anti-parietal antibody is sensitive (85–90%); anti-intrinsic factor is specific (75%). Triad: anemia + gastric atrophy (lifelong gastric cancer risk) + subacute combined degeneration of the cord.
  • Drug causes: metformin blocks it at the ileum; proton pump inhibitors block release in the stomach.
  • Folate in pregnancy: the neural tube closes at about 4 weeks — supplement before conception, not after the test is positive.
✗ Missed — raw lake fish, ribbon-like segments in stool

AnswerDiphyllobothrium latum (fish tapeworm)

The mechanism is competitive uptake — something in the gut eats the vitamin before the patient can. The fish tapeworm comes from raw or undercooked freshwater fish, grows to several metres, sheds flat ribbon-like segments in stool, and absorbs B12 avidly. Treat with praziquantel plus B12.

Why hookworm fails: it enters through skin from soil and feeds on blood → that's iron deficiency and small cells. This patient's cells were large. Opposite direction.

The other competitive-uptake cause to keep with it: small bowel bacterial overgrowth — suspect it with blind loops, strictures or dysmotility.

✗ Missed — why high-dose oral vitamin B12 works in pernicious anemia
You pickedTranscobalamin II uptake across the gastric mucosa
AnswerPassive diffusion of a fraction of a large oral dose

No intrinsic factor means the normal ileal route is permanently shut. So B12 must go by a route that doesn't need it: intramuscular injection, or high-dose oral.

Passive diffusion absorbs about 1% of an oral dose regardless of intrinsic factor. 1% of 1,000 micrograms ≈ 10 micrograms a day — comfortably above requirement. That arithmetic is the whole reason the dose is enormous.

Transcobalamin II is a plasma carrier delivering B12 to tissues after absorption, and the stomach is not an absorptive site. Don't drop the third problem either: the atrophic gastritis still needs gastric cancer surveillance.

The intrinsic factor route — blocked in pernicious anemia.

Hypersegmented neutrophil — 6+ lobes, the megaloblastic signature.

Heme synthesis gone wrong — porphyria and lead

🧠 One mechanism explains every porphyria

Heme normally inhibits the first enzyme, ALA synthase 1. A block downstream → less heme → less inhibition → ALA synthase 1 revs up → precursors above the block flood out. These are diseases of precursor accumulation, not of heme deficiency.

Every precipitant raises that enzyme: fasting or low carbohydrate, P450-inducing drugs, sex hormones, alcohol, infection, stress. So treatment is carbohydrate loading (glucose and insulin suppress the driver) and hemin (restores the feedback).

  • Acute intermittent porphyria: porphobilinogen deaminase, autosomal dominant, only half-deficient so onset 15–50, women more than men. Triad = visceral abdominal pain with normal imaging + neurologic + psychiatric, plus hypertension, tachycardia, low sodium, and urine that darkens on standing. Diagnose with a spot urine during the attack.
  • Porphyria cutanea tarda: the most common porphyria. Blistering photosensitivity, milia, hypertrichosis of cheeks and temples, tea-coloured urine. Associated with hepatitis C, alcohol, iron overload; two-thirds carry hemochromatosis mutations. Treat: antivirals, phlebotomy, hydroxychloroquine, sun avoidance.
  • The discriminator ★ HY: Lead blocks ALA dehydratase, which sits ABOVE porphobilinogen → ALA rises, porphobilinogen stays NORMAL. Acute intermittent porphyria blocks below it → BOTH rise. Lead also inhibits ferrochelatase → raised zinc protoporphyrin, plus microcytic anemia with basophilic stippling, Burton's lines, wrist and foot drop.

The pathway with each enzyme block marked.

Lead hits two enzymes — and the level of the block is the exam answer.

Q: A 24-year-old woman of Mediterranean ancestry has a lifelong mild anemia. Hemoglobin 10.6, cell size 66, red cell count 6.1 million, distribution width 13.1%, ferritin 96. Diagnosis?

Answer

Beta-thalassemia trait. Many small cells with a narrow spread and a normal ferritin. Iron deficiency would give few small cells, a wide spread and a low ferritin — and giving her iron would be actively harmful.

The four inherited defects

Hereditary spherocytosis

Membrane / cytoskeleton · autosomal dominant

Genes: ankyrin, spectrin, band 3.

Tells: high hemoglobin concentration per cell, increased osmotic fragility, Coombs test NEGATIVE (that's what separates it from autoimmune hemolysis).

Treat: folate, transfusion, splenectomy — defer splenectomy past age 4.

G6PD deficiency

Enzyme · X-linked

The enzyme is unstable, so old cells fail first. Bite and blister cells; Heinz bodies need a supravital stain.

Triggers: infection (commonest), sulfa drugs, antimalarials, nitrofurantoin, fava beans.

Trap: the assay is falsely normal during a crisis — the deficient cells are already destroyed. Repeat at 2–3 months.

Thalassemia

Globin quantity · alpha ×4 (chr 16), beta ×2 (chr 11)

The unpaired partner chain is the poison, not the missing one.

Hemoglobin H = beta tetramers (1 alpha gene left). Hemoglobin Barts = gamma tetramers (0 alpha) = hydrops fetalis.

Beta major presents at 3–6 months, when the globin switch happens. Electrophoresis: high F, high A2. Treat: transfusion + chelation.

Sickle cell disease

Globin quality · 6th codon, beta globin gene

Glutamic acid → valine: a charged, water-loving residue swapped for a hydrophobic one, giving the molecule a sticky patch. Deoxygenated hemoglobin S polymerizes.

Disease = 90–100% S, no A. Trait = 40–45% S plus A.

  • Splenic sequestration — reticulocytes HIGH plus a huge spleen. Infants under 2; hemoglobin falls more than 2 g/dL below baseline and it is fatal within hours.
  • Vaso-occlusive crisis — hemoglobin near baseline. Dactylitis in infants.

  • Prevention package: penicillin prophylaxis in children, pneumococcal/meningococcal/Haemophilus type b vaccines, folic acid. Chronic transfusion to keep hemoglobin S under 30% for stroke prevention.

Q: Forty minutes into a red cell transfusion a patient develops fever, back pain and dark urine. Plasma from a fresh sample is pink. What happened, and what is the underlying error?

Answer

Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction from ABO incompatibility. Naturally occurring IgM fixed complement and lysed the donor cells intravascularly. The underlying error is nearly always clerical — the wrong blood in the tube, which is why the bedside identity check is the only real prevention.

Anticoagulants, Antiplatelets and Thrombolytics

🔑 White clot versus red clot — pick the class before you pick the drug ★ HY

Arterial clots are platelet-rich ("white") — high shear, ruptured plaque → ANTIPLATELET. Heart attack, ischemic stroke, peripheral arterial disease, stents.

Venous clots are fibrin-and-red-cell rich ("red") — stasis → ANTICOAGULANT. Deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and the clot in atrial fibrillation.

The cascade and the two clotting times

  • Read it as two arms joining one trunk. Left arm starts on a negatively charged surface and runs 12 → 11 → 9 → 8 — the intrinsic pathway, measured by the activated partial thromboplastin time. You named the VENOUS mechanism — the exact one the question asked you to contrast against. Pattern 2.

The primary function of the lymphatic system is to return PROTEINS and fluid to the blood. The immune role is real but secondary in this framing. Lymphatic walls are MORE permeable than blood capillaries, so they admit large proteins that cannot re-enter venous capillaries at all — lymph is therefore protein-heavy.

Venous obstruction raises hydrostatic pressure; fluid is pushed out but the protein-clearing route is intact → a low-protein transudate that pits and responds to elevation.

Lymphatic obstruction removes the only route those proteins have. They accumulate, raise interstitial oncotic pressure, and draw water after them. Over time retained protein drives collagen deposition — which is why chronic lymphedema becomes firm, fibrotic and stops pitting.

Two consequences from the same anatomy: the vessels are tiny (thoracic duct 2–3 mm; peripheral lymphatics the width of pencil lead), so there is no good surgical repair. And nodes enlarge because the bacteria, allergens and tumour cells collected there drive lymphocyte proliferation.

The lymphatic capillary is the leaky one — that permeability is the point, and losing it is the problem.

Splenic injury — stability decides, not grade

  • The most commonly injured solid organ in blunt abdominal trauma. Left lower rib fractures are the warning.
  • Kehr sign = referred LEFT shoulder pain from blood irritating the left hemidiaphragm (phrenic nerve, C3–5), worse lying flat.
  • UNSTABLE → FAST ultrasound → operating room. Never send an unstable trauma patient to CT. STABLE → CT with contrast, which grades it and finds the "blush."
  • Three rungs of management: (1) medical — observation, bed rest, serial hematocrit; the default, and near-universal in children · (2) interventional — angioembolization for a blush, pseudoaneurysm, or grade IV–V · (3) surgical — splenorrhaphy if stable, total splenectomy if unstable.
  • Grading: I–III track depth (under 1 cm / 1–3 cm / over 3 cm); IV–V involve the vessels.
  • Delayed rupture classically day 5–6. Complications: pseudoaneurysm, abscess, pancreatic tail leak, gastric necrosis from the short gastrics, splenic or portal vein thrombosis, subphrenic abscess with a left pleural effusion, platelets over 1 million → aspirin, splenosis, overwhelming infection.

CT grades the injury and finds the blush — but only in a stable patient.

Lymphatic dissemination of carcinoma

  • Carcinoma spreads by lymphatics; sarcoma spreads by blood. Lymphatic capillaries have no basement membrane and loose junctions — that is why.
  • The route: basement membrane → lymphovascular invasion → afferent lymphatic → subcapsular (marginal) sinus (where metastasis is seen first) → efferent → next echelon → thoracic duct → left subclavian vein, at which point it becomes hematogenous.
  • Sentinel node = first node draining the tumour. If negative, spare the dissection — and spare the secondary lymphedema it causes. Skip metastases happen when tumour obstructs lymphatics.
  • Virchow node = LEFT supraclavicular (Troisier sign), because the thoracic duct drains there → an abdominal or gastric primary. Right supraclavicular → lung, esophagus, mediastinum, head and neck.
  • Drainage map: testis → para-aortic, NOT inguinal (embryologic descent); scrotal skin → inguinal. Anal canal ABOVE the dentate line → internal iliac; BELOW → superficial inguinal. Breast → axillary levels I–III, defined by pectoralis minor.
  • Hard, fixed, painless, matted = metastatic. Fixation means extranodal extension.

The Geriatric Functional Assessment

🔑 Why function, not the problem list ★ HY

The life expectancy of a 75-year-old disabled in activities of daily living is the same as that of an INDEPENDENT 85-year-old. An activity-of-daily-living disability is worth roughly ten years of age.

75-year-olds without limitations live about 5 years longer than those with such a limitation, and over a year longer than those limited in mobility. That is why the assessment is multidimensional and multidisciplinary, and why the traditional medical review is not enough for an older adult with multiple conditions.

The tools, and the escalation logic ★ HY

🧠 The pattern is always the same

Brief screen → positive → longer instrument → positive → intervention. And note where the intervention is not a drug.

  • Cognition: Mini-Cog → MoCA / SLUMS / MMSE → formal neuropsychiatric testing
  • Mood: PHQ-2 → PHQ-9 or Geriatric Depression Scale → social work, therapy and community engagement FIRST, then medication
  • Sleep: sleep quality scale → sleep hygiene first, then medication
  • Falls / activity: Timed Up and Go, 4-Stage Balance (STEADI), RAPA → physical therapy referral
  • Function: Katz (basic activities) / Lawton–Brody (household activities) → occupational therapy referral
  • Frailty: Fried Frailty Scale, Clinical Frailty Scale — weight loss, exhaustion, slowness
  • Substances: CAGE-AID · Medication review → harm potential by the Beers Criteria
🩺 The testable point about the tools

You do not administer all of them. They are deployed when the history raises suspicion — "administering every tool is not logical, not practical, and will upset the average patient." The skill being examined is choosing the right tool for the complaint you just heard.

And one striking finding: physical and occupational therapists predict which patients will successfully return home; physicians and social workers do not. The people who assess function predict the outcome better than the people who manage the diagnosis.

Age-related hematologic change

  • Normal marrow cellularity = (100 − age)%; myeloid-to-erythroid ratio 3:1.
  • Disease prevalence shifts with age — monoclonal gammopathy, myelodysplasia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia all rise.
  • "Inflammaging" drives cardiovascular, bone and cognitive disease in treated HIV despite an undetectable viral load. Monitor lipids, bone density, renal function, cognition and cervical screening every 6–12 months; labs every 3–6 months.

Q: An 82-year-old is admitted to a skilled nursing facility after a urinary tract infection complicated by delirium. He previously lived alone. Whose assessment best predicts a successful transition back to the community?

Answer

The physical and occupational therapists. A longitudinal cohort found therapists could predict successful discharge home while medical providers and social workers could not — the section's thesis in one item: function predicts outcome better than diagnosis.

16 · PEDS, SPLEEN & RX — 2 missed

The child and the spleen

  • ITP: isolated low platelets, megakaryocytes INCREASED, splenomegaly RARE. Acute child 80% resolve.
  • WELL child + no organomegaly + ACELLULAR marrow = aplastic. SICK child + splenomegaly + PACKED marrow = leukemia. Lymphadenopathy occurs in both. White count may be LOW in leukemia.
  • Transfuse 10–15 cc/kg (raises ~2–3 g/dL), target ~7 NOT normal — chronic anemia is compensated; fast correction → heart failure. Then iron + cut cow's milk.
  • Fanconi = absent THUMBS; TAR = thumbs PRESENT. Wiskott-Aldrich = SMALL platelets.
  • Portal hypertension = commonest hypersplenism; splenectomy NOT indicated. Splenic vein thrombosis → splenectomy IS curative.
  • Prescription: the commonest omission is the quantity. No trailing zero; always a leading zero; write "units" and "daily".
