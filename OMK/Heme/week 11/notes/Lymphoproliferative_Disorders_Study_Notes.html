Orientation — the two questions that organize everything

Dr. Sirotnikov defines the subject in the first sentence: lymphoproliferative disorders are a group of disorders characterized by controlled reactive proliferations (benign) and uncontrolled neoplastic proliferations (malignant) of lymphoid cells, involving lymph nodes and occasionally bone marrow, peripheral blood and extranodal sites. This file covers the malignant half; the companion Lymph Node & Reactive Lymphadenopathy file covers the benign half.

🔑 Two questions get you most of the way through any lymphoma
  1. Is it Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin?Hodgkin lymphoma means classic Hodgkin lymphoma plus nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma is literally everything else — the precursor (lymphoblastic) neoplasms and all the peripheral B-cell, T-cell and NK-cell neoplasms.
  2. For the mature B-cell lymphomas: what is the CD5 / CD10 status?Dr. Sirotnikov returns to this repeatedly and says so explicitly. CD5 positive → CLL/SLL or mantle cell lymphoma. CD10 positive → germinal center origin: follicular, Burkitt, a subset of diffuse large B-cell. CD5 and CD10 both negative → marginal zone, lymphoplasmacytic, hairy cell. Two stains cut the differential into thirds.
🧠 The naming convention that is worth ten facts

“-oma” means mass. So when a disease has both a leukemic and a lymphomatous name, the two names are telling you where the disease is, not what it is:

  • CLL (chronic lymphocytic leukemia) = peripheral blood and bone marrow. SLL (small lymphocytic lymphoma) = lymph node. Same disease process.
  • T-lymphoblastic leukemia = marrow. T-lymphoblastic lymphoma = a mass in the thymus, because that is where T cells mature.
  • Mycosis fungoides = the skin. Sézary syndrome = the blood.

🔑 Zone → neoplasm, and why the pattern comes first
  • Superficial cortex / follicles = B-cell zone. Secondary follicles have reactive germinal centers, so germinal-center-derived neoplasms arise here: follicular lymphoma, Burkitt lymphoma, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. When they take over you see architecture effaced by a nodular process — which is the pattern that makes you think of the disease in the first place.
  • Mantle zone surrounds the germinal center → mantle cell lymphoma.
  • Marginal zone lies beyond the mantle → marginal zone lymphoma.
  • Paracortical / interfollicular = T-cell zone → peripheral T-cell lymphomas, seen as paracortical expansion.
  • Bone marrow → B-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma; thymus → T-lymphoblastic lymphoma presenting as a mediastinal mass.

The WHO classification

The classification has changed repeatedly since the original Rappaport classification of 1976. We are currently on the World Health Organization 5th edition (2022), with plans for an integrated WHO and International Consensus Classification. The history is interesting rather than testable — base everything on the WHO.

🧠 Why the classification kept changing: the tools kept improving

Alongside the classification, the ancillary studies improved dramatically:

  • 1960s: one-color flow cytometry and simple immunohistochemistry.
  • 1970s: better immunohistochemistry, plus Sanger sequencing.
  • 1980s: four-color flow.
  • 1990s: eight-color flow, advances in mass cytometry.
  • Today: clinical laboratories commonly run 6- to 12-color flow, with some institutions reaching 16 colors.

Why more colors matter: more colors means more antibodies in a single tube, so you can characterize one population of interest in one tube instead of several — more efficient, and a better characterization because every marker is measured on the same cells.

🩺 The four patterns Dr. Sirotnikov singled out
  • Small lymphocytes → CLL/SLL. Remember these are one disease: CLL is the blood/marrow counterpart, SLL is the nodal counterpart.
  • Follicular / nodular → follicular lymphoma, with benign lymphoid hyperplasia as the main differential.
  • Polymorphous with scattered large cells → Hodgkin lymphoma. “Polymorphous” means a mixture of cell types — small lymphocytes, numerous eosinophils, neutrophils, plasma cells, histiocytes — with scattered Hodgkin and Reed–Sternberg cells in it.
  • Sinus pattern → anaplastic large cell lymphoma or metastatic carcinoma. The sinuses under the capsule are the first place a pathologist looks to rule out metastatic involvement.
  • Sheets of large cells → diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

Q: A lymph node is effaced by a monotonous population of small mature lymphocytes with pale nodular areas scattered through it. What is the top differential, and what are the pale nodules?

Answer

CLL/SLL — the “small lymphocyte” pattern. The pale nodules are proliferation centers (pseudofollicles) containing prolymphocytes and paraimmunoblasts: larger, more activated cells with more open chromatin and distinct nucleoli. They are a characteristic feature of CLL/SLL and should not be mistaken for reactive germinal centers — true germinal centers sit at the periphery in a preserved node, whereas here the architecture is effaced.

Q: Why does the phrase “polymorphous background with scattered large cells” point to Hodgkin lymphoma rather than to a large cell lymphoma?

Answer

Because in classic Hodgkin lymphoma the neoplastic cells are a tiny minority — Reed–Sternberg and Hodgkin cells represent less than 10% of all cells, often only 1–2%. What fills the node is a reactive polymorphous infiltrate: small lymphocytes, histiocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, eosinophils and fibroblasts. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, by contrast, the large cells are the tissue — you see sheets of them. Scattered versus sheets is the discrimination.

Topic 2 — Framework

How lymphomas get started

Pathogenesis and risk factors

Three routes to a lymphoid neoplasm — grow faster, renew more, or die less

Pro-growth mutations

Increase cell division

These are the proto-oncogenes. Tyrosine kinase mutations and MYC translocation. Effect: increased cell division and Warburg metabolism.

Transcription factor mutations

Increase self-renewal

KMT2A translocation, PML::RARA fusion gene. These are discussed further in the hematopoiesis lecture. Effect: increased self-renewal.

Pro-survival mutations

Decrease apoptosis

BCL2 translocation is the paradigm. Effect: decreased apoptosis. This is the mechanism of follicular lymphoma.

🔑 The single most important mechanistic sentence in this section

Proto-oncogenes are often activated in lymphoid cells by errors that occur during attempted antigen receptor gene diversification — and potentially oncogenic mutations occur most frequently in germinal center B cells.

The reason: the germinal center B cell deliberately breaks and re-joins its own DNA during class switch recombination and somatic hypermutation (as the immature B cell does during VDJ recombination in the marrow). Mistakes during those controlled breaks can splice an oncogene next to the transcriptionally hyperactive immunoglobulin locus, which then drives it permanently. MYC is the classic example — activated by translocation to the immunoglobulin locus.

Inherited predispositions

  • Fanconi anemia — a DNA repair disorder.
  • Ataxia telangiectasia — ATM gene, a DNA damage response defect.
  • Down syndrome — associated with both B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and an increased risk of acute myeloid leukemia.
  • Neurofibromatosis type 1.

Q: A patient with long-standing Helicobacter pylori gastritis develops a low-grade B-cell lymphoma of the stomach. Why might antibiotics alone cure this lymphoma, when antibiotics obviously do not cure lymphoma in general?

Answer

Because in gastric extranodal marginal zone (MALT) lymphoma the neoplastic clone remains, at least early on, dependent on chronic antigenic stimulation from the organism. Remove the antigen and the growth stimulus is removed: these tumors respond remarkably well to H. pylori-directed therapy. It is the clearest clinical demonstration of the “chronic inflammation drives lymphomagenesis” principle. The same logic applies to celiac disease and enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma and to breast implants and anaplastic large cell lymphoma.

Q: Why are germinal center B cells — not marrow precursors, not plasma cells — the commonest site of oncogenic translocation?

Answer

Because germinal center B cells are doing deliberate, programmed DNA breakage and re-ligation: class switch recombination and somatic hypermutation. Errors during those processes can join a proto-oncogene to the transcriptionally active immunoglobulin heavy chain locus on chromosome 14, which drives it constitutively. This one mechanism produces t(14;18) BCL2 in follicular lymphoma, t(8;14) MYC in Burkitt lymphoma, and t(11;14) cyclin D1 in mantle cell lymphoma.

🔑 The contiguous spread map — a genuine pattern, not trivia

Because Hodgkin lymphoma spreads to adjacent node groups in order, the pattern of involvement is predictable. Dr. Sirotnikov walks it explicitly:

  • In limited stage disease (stage I or II), the cervical and/or supraclavicular nodes are involved most frequently.
  • Patients with nodular sclerosis classic Hodgkin lymphoma also commonly have mediastinal lymphadenopathy.
  • Left supraclavicular involvement is often followed by abdominal para-aortic node involvement.
  • Right supraclavicular involvement tends to be associated with mediastinal disease.
  • When axillary nodes are involved, disease can spread to infraclavicular nodes.
  • Abdominal nodes are not affected unless cervical or mediastinal nodes are involved first.
  • Para-aortic involvement usually precedes involvement of the spleen, which in turn is followed by liver or bone marrow involvement, or both.
  • If extranodal disease does occur in Hodgkin lymphoma, the sites are bone, lung, spleen and liver.

Q: A patient has biopsy-proven lymphoma involving abdominal para-aortic nodes and the spleen, with no cervical or mediastinal disease anywhere on imaging. Does this favor Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and why?

Answer

Strongly favors non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Hodgkin lymphoma spreads contiguously in an orderly fashion, and abdominal nodes are not affected unless cervical or mediastinal nodes are involved first. Skipping straight to the abdomen is noncontiguous spread, which is characteristic of non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The involvement of mesenteric nodes or Waldeyer ring, or any extranodal presentation, would push the same way.

Topic 4 — Hodgkin

The disease defined by a cell that is barely there

Classic Hodgkin lymphoma — cells, phenotype, staging, treatment

🔑 The basic facts
  • First described in 1832 by Thomas Hodgkin.
  • 10% of all lymphomas worldwide.
  • Bimodal age distribution: first peak 15–34 years, second peak after 50 years. (In pediatric practice it is also seen frequently in children and adolescents under 15.)
  • Four subtypes: nodular sclerosis, lymphocyte-rich, mixed cellularity, lymphocyte-depleted.
🧠 Pathogenesis — two associations to remember
  • Epstein–Barr virus is commonly associated with classic Hodgkin lymphoma, particularly the lymphocyte-depleted subtype, but also seen in lymphocyte-rich and mixed cellularity.
  • At the molecular level, activation of the transcription factor NF-κB is common in classic Hodgkin lymphoma and turns on genes that promote the growth and survival of Reed–Sternberg cells. Epstein–Barr virus is one route to that activation.

The cells

⚠️ The counterintuitive fact that defines the disease

Reed–Sternberg and Hodgkin cells are typically NOT the predominant component. They represent less than 10% of all cells in an involved node — typically closer to 1–2%. The bulk of the tissue is a reactive infiltrate: lymphocytes, histiocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, eosinophils and fibroblasts. This is why the low-power pattern is “polymorphous with scattered large cells,” and why finding the diagnostic cell can take real searching.

Lineage — the defective B cell

🧠 Reed–Sternberg cells are B cells that have switched off their own B-cell program

If anyone asks: Reed–Sternberg and Hodgkin cells are of B-cell lineage — but they have a defective B-cell program. This happens through expression of B-cell repressors and silencing of B-cell differentiation genes. The result is weaker or absent expression of B-cell markers, and that loss is exactly what the immunostain panel is designed to detect.

The marker-numbering shortcut Dr. Sirotnikov gives: CD numbers in the low teens and below (CD2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8) → think T cell. CD numbers in the twenties (CD19, CD20, CD22, CD79a) → think B cell.

Prognosis and treatment

🩺 Hodgkin lymphoma has a genuinely good prognosis
  • 90–95% of patients with early stage disease achieve complete remission.
  • 80–85% with advanced stage disease are cured.
  • Advanced stage: chemotherapy, or combined chemotherapy and radiation.
  • Standard regimen — ABVD: Adriamycin (hydroxydaunorubicin/doxorubicin), Bleomycin, Vinblastine, Dacarbazine.
  • Relapsed disease — targeted therapy: brentuximab vedotin, an anti-CD30 antibody–drug conjugate (which is why CD30 positivity is not just diagnostic but therapeutic), or nivolumab, a checkpoint inhibitor.

Survival by subtype — lymphocyte predominance best, lymphocyte depletion worst

Q: A lymph node contains scattered large binucleate cells with cherry-red nucleoli in a polymorphous background rich in eosinophils. The large cells are CD30 positive, CD15 positive, PAX5 weakly positive, CD20 negative, OCT2 negative, CD45 negative, ALK negative. What is the diagnosis, and what does each of the last two negatives rule out?

Answer

Classic Hodgkin lymphoma. The profile is complete: CD30 strong, CD15 co-expressed, PAX5 weak (versus dark background B cells), pan-B-cell markers lost, OCT2 negative. CD45 negativity — CD45 is a pan-hematopoietic marker — helps separate classic Hodgkin lymphoma from nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma and non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphomas, which retain it. ALK negativity excludes ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma, which is the other major CD30-positive lymphoma.

Q: Why is PAX5 more useful than CD20 for confirming classic Hodgkin lymphoma, even though both are B-cell markers?

Answer

Because PAX5 gives you a graded, comparative result rather than a binary one. In classic Hodgkin lymphoma PAX5 is weak but present, and the background small B cells in the same section stain strongly — so a single slide carries its own internal control, and the weakness itself is the positive finding. CD20 is simply negative, which is less informative because many things are CD20 negative. Dr. Sirotnikov calls the PAX5 comparison “probably your most helpful clue” and stresses that the background B cells are always something to look for.

Q: Brentuximab vedotin is used in relapsed classic Hodgkin lymphoma. What is its target, and what other lymphoma in this lecture would you also expect it to work in?

Answer

Brentuximab is an anti-CD30 agent. The other CD30-positive lymphoma in this lecture is anaplastic large cell lymphoma, which is defined by diffuse CD30 expression. This is a good illustration of why the diagnostic marker and the therapeutic target are so often the same molecule. The alternative relapse option, nivolumab, is a checkpoint inhibitor and works by a completely different mechanism.

When there is a predominance of Hodgkin/Reed–Sternberg cells and variants forming a cohesive-looking group of neoplastic cells, this is called the syncytial variant of nodular sclerosis. It matters because the cohesive appearance can mimic a carcinoma or anaplastic large cell lymphoma. Q: A 24-year-old woman has a mediastinal mass and cervical adenopathy. The node is divided into nodules by thick collagen bands; within them are numerous large cells sitting in clear spaces, in a background rich in eosinophils. Subtype, stage expectation, and prognosis?

Answer

Nodular sclerosis classic Hodgkin lymphoma. The broad bands of collagen and lacunar cells (large cells in clear spaces from retraction artifact) are diagnostic of the subtype. It is the subtype most likely to occur in females, typically in young adults, usually presents at early stage (I or II), is the subtype most associated with mediastinal lymphadenopathy, and has an excellent prognosis.

Q: How do you separate mixed cellularity from nodular sclerosis when both show a polymorphous eosinophil-rich background with Reed–Sternberg cells?

Answer

Fibrosis. Nodular sclerosis has broad bands of collagen dividing the node into nodules; mixed cellularity does not. Supporting differences: nodular sclerosis occurs in young adults, equally in both sexes, at early stage, with an excellent prognosis and numerous lacunar cells; mixed cellularity occurs in elderly males (and children), presents at high stage, is aggressive, and shows many typical Reed–Sternberg cells with an interfollicular pattern that preserves reactive follicles.

Q: An elderly HIV-positive man has disseminated lymphoma. The node shows a fibroblast-rich background with few lymphocytes and numerous large, dark, atypical cells. Subtype and prognosis?

Answer

Lymphocyte-depleted classic Hodgkin lymphoma. The demographic (elderly male, HIV positive), the disseminated presentation, the rare background inflammatory cells with numerous Reed–Sternberg cells, and the fibrosis all fit. It is very aggressive with a poor prognosis — the worst of the four subtypes on the survival curves. It is also the subtype most strongly associated with Epstein–Barr virus.

Topic 6 — Hodgkin

The Hodgkin lymphoma that is proud of being a B cell

Nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma

Now increasingly called nodular lymphocyte predominant B-cell lymphoma, and the name change tells you the whole story: unlike classic Hodgkin lymphoma, this one retains its B-cell program completely. One of the more common lymphomas encountered in pediatric practice.

🔑 Clinical profile
  • 5–10% of all cases of Hodgkin lymphoma.
  • Peak incidence 30–50 years; male-to-female ratio 3:1. Also seen in children.
  • Most common site: cervical nodes. Axillary and inguinal nodes can be involved, as can mesenteric.
  • B symptoms (fever, weight loss, night sweats) are UNCOMMON — a helpful discriminator from classic Hodgkin lymphoma.
  • Most patients present with localized stage I or II disease (about 80%).
  • Advanced stage disease involves multiple node groups, liver, spleen, and bone or bone marrow.
🩺 Prognosis and treatment
  • Clinically indolent: greater than 80% ten-year survival for localized disease — the best of any Hodgkin subtype on the survival curves.
  • But it shows multiple recurrences over time.
  • Children with localized disease: “watch and wait” is the typical strategy.
  • Adults with advanced disease: either ABVD (the classic Hodgkin regimen) or R-CHOP (a regimen normally used for large B-cell lymphomas such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma) — the choice of R-CHOP being another clue that this is really a B-cell lymphoma.

Morphology

Refer back to the pattern-based approach: think follicles / nodular pattern. The architecture is completely effaced by a nodular proliferation of variably sized nodules — more disorganized than the crisp, small, regular follicles of a background reactive node.

Answer

Nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma (nodular lymphocyte predominant B-cell lymphoma). The large cells are popcorn cells. Q: Why is R-CHOP a rational choice for advanced nodular lymphocyte predominant Hodgkin lymphoma, when it is a diffuse large B-cell lymphoma regimen?

Answer

Because the R in R-CHOP is rituximab, an anti-CD20 antibody — and unlike classic Hodgkin lymphoma, the popcorn cells of nodular lymphocyte predominant disease are strongly CD20 positive. The neoplasm has retained its full B-cell program, so a B-cell-directed regimen has a target. This is precisely why the entity has been renamed nodular lymphocyte predominant B-cell lymphoma. Classic Hodgkin lymphoma, being CD20 negative, would not respond to rituximab — it is targeted instead with anti-CD30 brentuximab.

Richter transformation

🔑 Richter transformation — an important takeaway

Richter transformation is progression of CLL/SLL to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Patients develop a rapidly enlarging mass within a lymph node or the spleen. It is driven by newly acquired mutations in TP53 or MYC.

Prognosis

Prognosis depends on stage, the presence of B symptoms, the pattern of bone marrow involvement, the immunophenotype, karyotype/FISH, and gene mutations. The commonest genetic abnormalities are deletions of 13q14.3, 11q, and 17p (TP53), and trisomy 12q.

⚠️ TP53 — the one genetic fact to carry away

TP53 (on 17p) is always associated with a poor prognosis — in CLL, in acute myeloid leukemia, everywhere. Dr. Sirotnikov: “It’s always bad.”

Low stage disease with no adverse prognostic indicators is managed with watchful waiting — treatment is not automatic.

CD10 positive · germinal center origin

Follicular lymphoma

  • 10–20% of all lymphomas.
  • Middle-aged to elderly; median age 65 years.
  • Widespread disease at diagnosis, but typically asymptomatic.
  • Involves peripheral nodes (cervical, axillary, inguinal) and central nodes (mesenteric, thoracic); splenomegaly is typical.
  • Bone marrow involved in 40–70% of cases, showing a PARATRABECULAR pattern — lymphoid aggregates hugging the bony trabeculae.
  • Indolent and incurable, with characteristic waxing and waning of involved sites. Median survival over 17 years.
Phenotype CD10 +CD20 +BCL6 +BCL2 +CD5 −
🔑 BCL2 — why positivity here is abnormal

Normal germinal center B cells are BCL2 NEGATIVE. BCL2 is anti-apoptotic, and the germinal center exists to run somatic hypermutation and then eliminate the defective cells — so the cells must be easy to kill. Switching BCL2 off is how that is arranged.

Follicular lymphoma is defined by BCL2 EXPRESSION, and that is how you distinguish it from follicular hyperplasia. CD10 and BCL6 positivity confirm germinal center origin.

🧠 The translocation you should be able to recite

t(14;18): IgH (immunoglobulin heavy chain, chromosome 14) :: BCL2 (chromosome 18).

The translocation puts BCL2 under the control of the transcriptionally hyperactive immunoglobulin heavy chain promoter, which forces it on. Anti-apoptosis is restored, and the germinal-center-origin cells accumulate because they cannot die. Once you remember that follicular lymphoma is BCL2 positive, the translocation and the mechanism follow from each other.

⚠️ Transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma is the real risk

Progression to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma occurs at a rate of 1–3% per year, with a median time to progression of 2.5–4.1 years. In practice, adults with follicular lymphoma who develop a PET-avid site get an excision or biopsy specifically to look for transformation.

🔑 What you look for at low power
  • A uniformity to the cells — monotonous rather than the mixed centroblast/centrocyte population of a reactive center.

Grading — the pathologist’s job does not stop at the diagnosis

After proving follicular lymphoma with stains and molecular studies, it must be graded based on the proportion of centrocytes to centroblasts:

  • Centrocytes = the small cleaved lymphocytes.
  • Centroblasts = the larger, more activated cells.
  • More than 15 centroblasts in multiple abnormal follicles → higher grade. More centroblasts = higher grade.
  • Think of it as a continuum: more and more centroblasts, until you reach sheets of those cells — which is diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

Q: A 64-year-old with painless generalized lymphadenopathy has a node effaced by crowded, uniform, back-to-back follicles with no tingible body macrophages and attenuated mantle zones. Which single stain confirms the diagnosis, what result do you expect, and what is the translocation?

Answer

BCL2 — and it will be POSITIVE within the follicles, which is abnormal because normal germinal center B cells are BCL2 negative. Diagnosis: follicular lymphoma. Supporting phenotype: CD10 positive, BCL6 positive (germinal center origin), CD5 negative.

Q: A bone marrow biopsy shows lymphoid aggregates tightly apposed to the bony trabeculae. Which lymphoma does this pattern classically suggest, and which other disease in this lecture can mimic it?

Answer

A paratrabecular pattern classically suggests follicular lymphoma, whose marrow is involved in 40–70% of cases. The mimic is CLL/SLL, which has a nodular/paratrabecular pattern among its three marrow patterns (nodular/paratrabecular, interstitial, diffuse). Immunophenotype resolves it: follicular lymphoma is CD10 +, BCL6 +, BCL2 +, CD5 −, whereas CLL/SLL is CD5 +, CD23 +, CD10 −.

Q: A patient with known follicular lymphoma has a single new PET-avid nodal site three years after diagnosis. What is being looked for on biopsy, and what is the annual rate?

Answer

Transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, which occurs at 1–3% per year with a median time to progression of 2.5–4.1 years. Morphologically this is the appearance of sheets of centroblast-like large cells replacing the follicular architecture — the end of the same grading continuum that separates low-grade from high-grade follicular lymphoma.

🧠 Why BCL6 is negative — a logic check, not a fact to memorize

The marginal zone lies OUTSIDE the germinal center. BCL6 and CD10 are germinal center markers, so a marginal-zone-derived neoplasm should not express them. If you remember the anatomy of the follicle (germinal center → mantle zone → marginal zone), the phenotype follows.

  • A panel of immunohistochemical stains to detect aberrant expression or loss of markers.
  • Molecular studies for immunoglobulin heavy chain or light chain gene rearrangements to prove clonality — the formal demonstration that it really is a lymphoma.

🔑 Two buzzwords

Extranodal marginal zone (MALT) lymphoma of the stomach. The named features are monocytoid cells (abundant pale cytoplasm) and lymphoepithelial lesions (lymphoid invasion of glands). 🧠 Burkitt versus follicular lymphoma — both germinal center, opposite BCL2

Both are CD10 positive, BCL6 positive, which places both in the germinal center. But:

  • Follicular lymphoma is BCL2 POSITIVE — t(14;18) forces the anti-apoptotic gene on. The cells accumulate because they cannot die. Indolent.
  • Burkitt lymphoma is BCL2 NEGATIVE — the driver is MYC, on chromosome 8, with the defining t(8;14). The cells accumulate because they divide furiously. Aggressive, with Ki-67 over 95% — so it is both highly mitotically and highly apoptotically active.

Grow-fast versus die-slow. Two germinal center lymphomas with opposite mechanisms and opposite tempos.

  • Pale areas within the nucleus are the nucleoli.
  • Cytoplasmic vacuoles — a helpful clue. Not specific (also seen in B-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma), but in the right clinical setting they put you on high alert.
  • STARRY SKY appearance — the take-home morphology. Created by a diffuse pattern of medium-sized cells that mold against each other, punctuated by tingible body macrophages containing engulfed apoptotic debris. The very high turnover generates the debris.

Q: A 7-year-old in Kenya presents with a rapidly growing jaw mass. Biopsy shows a diffuse sheet of monotonous medium-sized cells with numerous pale macrophages containing debris. Which subtype, which virus, which translocation, and what would Ki-67 show?

Answer

Endemic Burkitt lymphoma, associated with Epstein–Barr virus, with the defining translocation t(8;14) — IgH::MYC (MYC is on chromosome 8). The morphology described is the starry sky. Ki-67 would exceed 95% — essentially every cell cycling, which is why the macrophages have so much apoptotic debris to clear and why the tumor doubles so fast. Phenotype: CD10 +, BCL6 +, CD20 +, MYC +, BCL2 NEGATIVE.

Q: How do you distinguish Burkitt lymphoma from follicular lymphoma on immunostains alone, given that both are germinal-center-derived?

Answer

BCL2 and Ki-67. Both are CD10 positive and BCL6 positive, confirming germinal center origin. But follicular lymphoma is BCL2 POSITIVE with a relatively low proliferation index, while Burkitt lymphoma is BCL2 NEGATIVE with Ki-67 over 95%. The mechanisms are opposite: follicular lymphoma blocks apoptosis (t(14;18) BCL2); Burkitt lymphoma drives proliferation (t(8;14) MYC).

Q: An adult in the United States presents with a large abdominal mass and colonic volvulus. The resection shows omental nodules of sheets of medium-sized lymphoid cells. What subtype of Burkitt lymphoma is this, and why did the volvulus occur?

Answer

Non-endemic (sporadic) Burkitt lymphoma, whose commonest site in this setting is the abdomen, characteristically with nodules in the omental fat around a colon specimen. The volvulus occurs because the mass creates an anchor point around which the bowel twists. Remember the third subtype too: immunodeficiency-associated (HIV), which presents with nodal involvement.

Mycosis fungoides is the most common cutaneous T-cell lymphoma — over 50% of cases. Sézary syndrome is the leukemic (blood) counterpart. Mycosis fungoides is the skin; Sézary syndrome is the blood.

🔑 Diagnostic criteria for Sézary syndrome

In a patient with erythroderma, the diagnosis rests on:

  • An absolute Sézary cell count of over 1,000 cells per microliter.
  • Flow cytometry showing an expanded CD4-positive population with a skewed CD4:CD8 ratio of greater than 10:1.
  • Loss of markers — CD2, CD3, CD7, or CD26.
  • Ideally, T-cell receptor gene rearrangement studies showing the SAME clone in blood and skin — this is what cements the diagnosis.

Note: the presence of Sézary cells alone is not specific, so it must be correlated with the flow phenotype and the clinical picture.

Dermatopathic lymphadenopathy — the staging trap

Dermatopathic node — architecture preserved but distorted, with pale expanded paracortex

High power — bland histiocytes and dendritic cells, plus pigment-laden macrophages

⚠️ Why this matters clinically

Patients with mycosis fungoides frequently develop dramatic dermatopathic lymphadenopathy — a reactive phenomenon in the node draining chronically inflamed skin. It is essential to distinguish this from true nodal involvement by mycosis fungoides, because that distinction changes the stage.

Recognize the reactive node by: architecture preserved but distorted, superficial cortex still showing reactive germinal centers, and paracortical expansion that is pale at low power because it is filled with histiocytes and dendritic cells (bland, pale chromatin, somewhat irregular nuclei, ample cytoplasm) plus increased pigment-laden macrophages. When such a node is received, flow cytometry and immunostains are performed to compare and contrast the phenotype and determine whether there is true involvement.

Q: A 58-year-old man has a 12-year history of scaly red plaques on his buttocks and groin. A skin biopsy shows small atypical lymphocytes with folded nuclei sitting in clear halos, some forming clusters within the epidermis. Diagnosis, name the two morphologic terms, and give the expected phenotype.

Answer

Mycosis fungoides. The terms are epidermotropism (atypical lymphocytes migrating into the epidermis) and Pautrier microabscesses (clusters of them within the upper epidermis). The nuclei are described as hyperchromatic and hyperconvoluted — cerebriform. Expected phenotype: CD2 +, CD3 +, CD5 +, CD4 +, CD8 negative, with CD7 dim or partially lost. The sun-protected “bathing trunk” distribution (axilla, groin, lower trunk, buttocks) and the indolent decade-long course both fit.

Q: The same patient develops total-body erythroderma. Blood shows 1,800 cells/µL with hyperconvoluted nuclei; flow shows an expanded CD4 population with a CD4:CD8 ratio of 18:1 and loss of CD7 and CD26. What has happened, and what one further test would you want?

Answer

Sézary syndrome — the leukemic phase. All three criteria are met: erythroderma, an absolute Sézary cell count above 1,000/µL, and flow showing an expanded CD4 population with a CD4:CD8 ratio greater than 10:1 plus loss of CD7 and CD26. The further test is T-cell receptor gene rearrangement, to demonstrate the SAME clone in the blood and in the skin — that is what cements the diagnosis, since Sézary cells alone are not specific.

Q: An axillary node is removed from a patient with mycosis fungoides. It shows preserved follicles with reactive germinal centers and a pale, expanded paracortex full of bland dendritic cells and pigment-laden macrophages. Is this nodal involvement? Why does the answer matter?

Answer

No — this is dermatopathic lymphadenopathy, a reactive response of the node draining chronically inflamed skin. The giveaways are preserved (though distorted) architecture, reactive germinal centers, and a paracortex expanded by histiocytes, dendritic cells and pigment-laden macrophages rather than by atypical lymphocytes. It matters because mycosis fungoides is staged by T/N/M/B — skin, node, viscera and blood — so calling a reactive node “involved” would upstage the patient incorrectly. Flow cytometry and immunostains are used to compare the node’s phenotype with the skin lesion.

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma

  • More common in the pediatric and adolescent population than in adults — 10–15% of pediatric and adolescent non-Hodgkin lymphoma, but only 3% of adult non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
  • Median age 30 years; male predominance 3:1.
  • Involves lymph nodes and extranodal sites: skin, bone, soft tissue and lung.
⚠️ A tricky diagnosis, because it often does not look lymphoid

These cells can be so large and atypical that lymphoma is not the first thing you think of. When it grows in a sinusoidal pattern with cohesive-looking cells, it mimics melanoma or poorly differentiated carcinoma, especially in adults. It should be on your differential for any ugly, undifferentiated malignancy in a node, and Dr. Sirotnikov says he always does an ALK stain to rule it out when he is thinking Hodgkin lymphoma.

Q: A 14-year-old has a cervical node with sheets of very large atypical cells, some with eccentric horseshoe-shaped nuclei. They are strongly and diffusely CD30 positive, CD3 negative, CD4 positive, EMA positive, and ALK positive. Diagnosis, name the diagnostic cell, and what does the ALK result mean prognostically?

Answer

ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma. The diagnostic cell is the hallmark cell — eccentric, horseshoe-shaped nucleus with a pale paranuclear area. ALK positivity carries a long-term survival of about 80%, versus about 50% five-year overall survival for ALK-negative cases; ALK positivity is also more common in adolescents. Note the CD3 negativity, which is typical and makes proving T-cell lineage harder.

Q: A node shows fibrous bands dividing it into nodules, with scattered-looking large atypical cells that are CD30 positive. Why is this not automatically nodular sclerosis classic Hodgkin lymphoma, and what one stain settles it?

Answer

Because anaplastic large cell lymphoma can also form a nodular pattern with fibrous bands, and both entities are CD30 positive. Two things settle it. First, at higher power, ALCL shows uniform SHEETS of atypical cells, whereas in classic Hodgkin lymphoma the Reed–Sternberg cell is never the predominant cell (under 10%, often 1–2%). Second, the stain: ALK — positive in a substantial fraction of ALCL, always negative in classic Hodgkin lymphoma. Dr. Sirotnikov does an ALK stain routinely when considering Hodgkin lymphoma for exactly this reason. Supporting: ALCL is CD45 and EMA positive; classic Hodgkin lymphoma is CD45 negative.

Topic 15 — T-cell

Geography is the clue

Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma

🔑 The question-stem signature

A question stem that mentions a patient from Japan or the Caribbean is telling you about HTLV-1 (human T-lymphotropic virus 1) infection. The full endemic list is Japan, the Caribbean, intertropical Africa, the Middle East, South America and Papua New Guinea. Median age 68 years.

  • Presentation: generalized lymphadenopathy, skin lesions, and hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Peripheral blood: “FLOWER CELLS” — multilobated, irregular nuclei.
  • Aggressive disease, depending on the clinical subtype.

Flower cells — multilobated, petal-like nuclei

🧠 Flower cell versus Sézary cell — a deliberate contrast

Dr. Sirotnikov presents these two side by side on purpose. Both are abnormal circulating T cells with strange nuclei, but the shapes differ:

  • Flower cell (adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma): MULTILOBATED — distinct petal-like lobes radiating from a center, like a clover or flower.
  • Sézary cell (mycosis fungoides / Sézary syndrome): CONVOLUTED / CEREBRIFORM — the nucleus is one folded mass with a brain-like surface, not separate lobes.

Four clinical subtypes of adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma are recognized — acute, lymphoma, chronic and smoldering — defined by the pattern of organ involvement, LDH and calcium levels, and the degree of leukemic manifestation.

Q: A 66-year-old who emigrated from Japan presents with generalized lymphadenopathy, skin lesions, hepatosplenomegaly and hypercalcemia. The blood smear shows lymphocytes with multilobated, petal-like nuclei. Diagnosis, causative virus, and how do those cells differ from Sézary cells?

Answer

Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma, caused by HTLV-1 (human T-lymphotropic virus 1). The cells are flower cells — multilobated, with distinct petal-like lobes. Sézary cells are convoluted/cerebriform: one folded nuclear mass with a brain-like surface, not separate lobes. The geography (Japan, Caribbean, intertropical Africa, Middle East, South America, Papua New Guinea) is the intended clue, and calcium and LDH help define the clinical subtype (acute, lymphoma, chronic, smoldering).

How the diagnosis is actually made

Techniques used in diagnosis

Immunohistochemistry

Antibody staining on tissue sections

Uses monoclonal antibodies raised against cell-surface antigens. When we talk about clusters of differentiation (CD numbers), these are the targets. Works on fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue.

Flow cytometry

Needs FRESH tissue

Defines patterns of expression across a whole population, in lymph node, blood or bone marrow. Measures many markers on the same cells at once, which is why more colors means better characterization.

Molecular diagnostics — NGS

Next-generation sequencing

Identifies mutations that define particular lymphomas. Used to narrow a broad category down further.

Molecular diagnostics — PCR

Clonality

Polymerase chain reaction, chiefly to look for immunoglobulin heavy chain or light chain gene rearrangements (B cells) or T-cell receptor rearrangements — i.e. to prove clonality.

Cytogenetics

Karyotype and FISH

Several lymphomas are defined by specific rearrangements, so this is often the confirmatory test.

🔑 The CD numbering shortcut, one more time
  • Low CD numbers — CD2, CD3, CD4, CD5, CD7, CD8 — think T CELL.
  • CD numbers in the twenties — CD19, CD20, CD22, CD79a — think B CELL.

It is a rough heuristic, not a rule, but it saves you in a question stem full of unfamiliar markers.

🩺 The two translocations to be able to recite cold
  • Follicular lymphoma: t(14;18) — immunoglobulin heavy chain (chromosome 14) :: BCL2 (chromosome 18). Anti-apoptotic → indolent.
  • Burkitt lymphoma: t(8;14) — MYC (chromosome 8) :: immunoglobulin heavy chain (chromosome 14). Pro-proliferative → aggressive.

Both put an oncogene under the immunoglobulin heavy chain promoter on chromosome 14 — which is the mechanism described in the pathogenesis section.

Q: Why does proving “clonality” matter, and which technique does it?

Answer

Because a reactive lymphoid proliferation is polyclonal — many different B or T cells, each with its own uniquely rearranged receptor gene — whereas a lymphoma is monoclonal, descended from a single cell and therefore all carrying the identical rearrangement. PCR for immunoglobulin heavy/light chain gene rearrangement (B cells) or T-cell receptor gene rearrangement (T cells) is the technique. It is used exactly where morphology and immunohistochemistry leave doubt — for instance in extranodal marginal zone lymphoma, which sits on a continuum with reactive hyperplasia, and in mycosis fungoides, where the same clone must be demonstrated in blood and skin.

🔑 Papillary thyroid carcinoma is diagnosed by its NUCLEI, not its architecture
  • Psammoma bodies — laminated purple calcifications. Often the first thing you notice in a node.

Q: A cervical lymph node from a 45-year-old shows cohesive cell nests in the sinuses with purple laminated calcifications, enlarged nuclei with cleared chromatin, longitudinal grooves and round intranuclear holes. Diagnosis, and name each feature.

Answer

Metastatic papillary thyroid carcinoma. The calcifications are psammoma bodies; the cleared chromatin gives “Orphan Annie eye” nuclei; the folds are nuclear grooves; the round holes are nuclear pseudoinclusions (invaginated cytoplasm). Papillary thyroid carcinoma is defined by its nuclear features, not by having papillae. The cohesiveness and sinus location are what told you it was metastatic rather than lymphoid in the first place.

Q: Give the three-step reasoning that separates metastatic carcinoma from anaplastic large cell lymphoma when both fill the sinuses of a node with large ugly cells.

Answer

(1) Architecture: carcinoma tends to form glands, nests or cords, and produces things such as mucin; anaplastic large cell lymphoma does not, though it can look deceptively cohesive. (2) Cytology: look for hallmark cells — eccentric horseshoe-shaped nuclei with a pale paranuclear zone — which favor ALCL. (3) Immunohistochemistry, which settles it: carcinoma is keratin positive; melanoma is S100 and SOX10 positive; anaplastic large cell lymphoma is strongly and diffusely CD30 positive, EMA positive, often CD4 positive, and may be ALK positive. This is why ALCL must stay on the differential for any undifferentiated malignancy in a node.

Q: You are given a mature B-cell lymphoma and only allowed three stains. Which three, and what does each rule in or out?

Answer

CD5, CD10 and BCL2. CD5 splits off CLL/SLL and mantle cell lymphoma. CD10 splits off the germinal-center-derived group — follicular, Burkitt and a subset of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Both negative pushes you toward marginal zone, lymphoplasmacytic or hairy cell. BCL2 then separates within the CD10 group: positive → follicular lymphoma (t(14;18)); negative → Burkitt lymphoma (t(8;14), Ki-67 >95%). A fourth stain, CD23, would resolve CLL (positive) from mantle cell (negative).

Q: Two lymphomas in this lecture are strongly CD30 positive. Name them, and give two ways to separate them.

Answer

Classic Hodgkin lymphoma and anaplastic large cell lymphoma. Two separations: (1) proportion of neoplastic cells — in classic Hodgkin the Reed–Sternberg cells are scattered, under 10% of cells, in a polymorphous background; in ALCL the atypical cells form uniform sheets. (2) Immunophenotype — ALK is positive in a substantial fraction of ALCL and always negative in classic Hodgkin; CD45 and EMA are positive in ALCL and negative in classic Hodgkin; and classic Hodgkin shows weak PAX5 reflecting its defective B-cell program, whereas ALCL is a T/null-cell neoplasm.

Large cell with an eccentric horseshoe- or kidney-shaped nucleus and a pale paranuclear zone. Anaplastic large cell lymphoma.

An ALCL variant with a central pale/pinkish area within the nucleus, depending on plane of section.
Flower cell
A multilobated circulating lymphocyte with petal-like lobes. Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (HTLV-1).
Psammoma body
A laminated purple calcification. Papillary thyroid carcinoma.
Orphan Annie eye nuclei
Nuclei that look empty because of chromatin clearing. Papillary thyroid carcinoma.
Nuclear pseudoinclusion
Cytoplasm invaginating into the nucleus, appearing as a round hole within it. Papillary thyroid carcinoma.
Richter transformation
Progression of CLL/SLL to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, driven by newly acquired TP53 or MYC mutations. Presents as a rapidly enlarging nodal or splenic mass.

1 · FRAMEWORK

Two questions solve most cases

  • Hodgkin = classic HL + NLPHL. Non-Hodgkin = everything else
  • CD5 + → CLL/SLL, mantle cell (CD23 splits them: CLL +, MCL −)
  • CD10 + → germinal center: follicular, Burkitt, DLBCL subset (BCL6 + too)
  • CD5 − and CD10 − → marginal zone, lymphoplasmacytic, hairy cell
  • “-oma” = mass: CLL = blood/marrow, SLL = node; T-lymphoblastic lymphoma = thymic mass
  • CD numbers <10 → T cell; CD 20s → B cell

Pattern → differential

  • Small lymphocytes → CLL/SLL, MZL, MCL
  • Follicles / nodular → follicular lymphoma vs benign hyperplasia
  • Polymorphous + scattered large cells → Hodgkin
  • Sinus → metastasis, sinus histiocytosis, ALCL
  • Diffuse sheets of large cells → DLBCL

Pathogenesis

  • Pro-growth (MYC, tyrosine kinases) · transcription factor (KMT2A, PML::RARA) · pro-survival (BCL2)
  • Oncogenic mutations happen most in germinal center B cells — because class switch recombination and somatic hypermutation break DNA
  • Inherited: Fanconi, ataxia telangiectasia, Down syndrome, NF1
  • Viruses: HTLV-1 → ATLL · HHV-8 → primary effusion · EBV → classic Hodgkin, endemic Burkitt
  • Chronic inflammation: H. pylori → gastric MALT · celiac → EATL · breast implant → ALCL

Hodgkin vs non-Hodgkin clinically

  • HL: single axial group (cervical, mediastinal, para-aortic); orderly contiguous spread; mesenteric/Waldeyer rare; extranodal rare
  • NHL: multiple peripheral nodes; noncontiguous; mesenteric/Waldeyer common; extranodal common
  • Abdominal nodes not involved in HL unless cervical or mediastinal first; para-aortic precedes spleen, then liver/marrow
2 · HODGKIN

Classic Hodgkin lymphoma

  • 10% of lymphomas; bimodal 15–34 and >50; described 1832
  • RS/Hodgkin cells <10% of cells, often 1–2% — background is reactive and eosinophil-rich
  • B-cell lineage with a DEFECTIVE B-cell program
  • CD30 strong+uniform (Golgi), CD15 ~75%, PAX5 WEAK, MUM1 +
  • Negative: OCT2, BOB1, CD19/20/79a, T-cell markers, ALK1, CD45
  • NF-κB activation; EBV esp. lymphocyte-depleted
  • Ann Arbor I one region · II same side of diaphragm · III both sides · IV disseminated
  • 90–95% CR early; 80–85% cured advanced. ABVD; relapse → brentuximab (anti-CD30) or nivolumab

Four CHL subtypes

  • Nodular sclerosis: F=M, young adults, broad collagen bands + LACUNAR cells, stage I–II, excellent, mediastinal
  • Lymphocyte-rich: lymphocytes predominate, rosettes; main differential NLPHL; may show OCT2 or BOB1, not both
  • Mixed cellularity: elderly males, NO fibrous bands, interfollicular pattern with preserved follicles, high stage, aggressive
  • Lymphocyte-depleted: elderly males, HIV, few background cells, many RS cells, fibrosis, worst prognosis

NLPHL vs classic Hodgkin

  • POPCORN cell (multilobated, vesicular, irregular nucleolus) vs RS cell (binucleate, round cherry-red nucleolus + halo)
  • NLPHL: CD20 +, CD22 +, CD79a +, PAX5 STRONG, OCT2 +, BOB1 +, BCL6 +, CD45 +, EMA +; CD30 −, CD15 −
  • CHL: exact opposite — CD30 +, CD15 +, PAX5 weak, everything B negative, CD45 −
  • NLPHL rosettes = CD4 + CD57 + PD-1 + T-follicular helper cells
  • NLPHL: 5–10% of HL, 30–50 y, M:F 3:1, cervical, B symptoms UNCOMMON, 80% stage I–II
  • >80% 10-y survival but multiple recurrences. Watch and wait in children; ABVD or R-CHOP in adults (R = anti-CD20, works because CD20 +)
3 · MATURE B-CELL

CLL / SLL

  • Commonest leukemia in Europe/N. America; median 65 y
  • CD5 +, CD19 +, CD20 +, CD23 +, CD10 −, dim surface light chain
  • SMUDGE cells, soccer-ball chromatin; node shows proliferation centers with prolymphocytes + paraimmunoblasts
  • Marrow: nodular/paratrabecular, interstitial, or diffuse
  • del 13q14.3, 11q, 17p (TP53 — always bad), trisomy 12q
  • RICHTER TRANSFORMATION → DLBCL, via new TP53 or MYC; rapidly enlarging nodal/splenic mass
  • Low stage, no adverse markers → watchful waiting

Follicular lymphoma

  • 10–20% of lymphomas; median 65 y; widespread but asymptomatic
  • CD10 +, BCL6 +, BCL2 POSITIVE, CD20 +, CD5 −
  • t(14;18) IgH::BCL2 — anti-apoptotic gene forced on by the Ig promoter
  • Normal germinal center B cells are BCL2 NEGATIVE so they can die during selection — that is why BCL2 separates FL from hyperplasia
  • Back-to-back monotonous follicles, NO tingible body macrophages, attenuated mantle
  • Marrow in 40–70%, PARATRABECULAR
  • Grade by centroblasts:centrocytes; >15 centroblasts → higher grade
  • Indolent, incurable, waxing/waning; median survival >17 y. Transforms to DLBCL 1–3%/yr (median 2.5–4.1 y)
  • Duodenal-type = polyps at EGD, same t(14;18)

DLBCL

  • Sheets of large cells: large nuclei, open chromatin, prominent nucleoli
  • CD19 +, CD20 +; CD10 + subset
  • Subtypes worth naming: primary effusion (HHV-8), immune-privileged sites (primary CNS), EBV-positive, primary mediastinal (thymus, young adults)

Extranodal marginal zone (MALT)

  • 5–8% of B-cell lymphomas; median 61 y; arises in chronically inflamed tissue
  • Stomach commonest (33%), then orbit. H. pylori (stomach) · C. jejuni (small bowel) · B. burgdorferi (skin) · C. psittaci (ocular)
  • CD20 +, CD79a +, PAX5 +; CD5 −, CD10 −, BCL6 −, CD23 −; CD21 expanded dendritic meshworks
  • MONOCYTOID cells + LYMPHOEPITHELIAL LESIONS
  • Continuum with reactive hyperplasia → often needs clonality by PCR
  • Indolent: 5-y 90%, 10-y 80%. Cured by H. pylori eradication; or rituximab

Burkitt lymphoma

  • Commonest childhood cancer in equatorial Africa/PNG; in USA 50% adults 20–59, 28% >60, 22% pediatric
  • Endemic = EBV, JAW (mandible/maxilla) · Sporadic = ABDOMEN (omental nodules, can cause volvulus) · Immunodeficiency = HIV, nodal
  • CD10 +, CD20 +, BCL6 +, MYC +, BCL2 NEGATIVE, Ki-67 >95%
  • t(8;14) IgH::MYC (MYC on chromosome 8)
  • STARRY SKY; uniform MEDIUM cells 2–3× a red cell; cytoplasmic vacuoles (not specific)

Hairy cell leukemia

  • Middle-aged white males, median 55 y, M:F 5:1
  • BRAF mutation in >90% (MAP kinase pathway)
  • DRY TAP from marrow fibrosis; pancytopenia; splenomegaly; indolent
  • MONOCYTOPENIA is the giveaway clue
  • CD11c, CD25, CD103, annexin A1; CD5 −, CD10 −
4 · T-CELL

Mycosis fungoides

  • Commonest cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (>50%); peak late 50s, M:F 2:1; can occur in children
  • Indolent over years–decades. Early: patches and plaques. Advanced: nodules/tumors or erythroderma
  • Sun-protected areas (axilla, groin) + lower trunk/buttocks = “bathing trunk”
  • CD2 +, CD3 +, CD5 +, CD4 +, CD8 −; CD7 dim/partially lost
  • CEREBRIFORM haloed lymphocytes · EPIDERMOTROPISM · PAUTRIER MICROABSCESSES · large cell transformation >25% = worse
  • Staged T/N/M/B = skin, node, viscera, blood
  • Sepsis from skin/soft tissue infection and pneumonia is a major cause of hospitalization

Sézary syndrome

  • The blood phase (MF is the skin)
  • Requires erythroderma + >1,000 Sézary cells/µL + flow with expanded CD4, CD4:CD8 >10:1 + loss of CD2/CD7/CD26
  • Ideally same TCR clone in blood AND skin
  • Sézary cells alone are not specific

Dermatopathic lymphadenopathy

  • Reactive node draining inflamed skin, common in MF
  • Architecture preserved but distorted; reactive germinal centers retained; pale expanded paracortex of histiocytes + dendritic cells + pigment-laden macrophages
  • Must be separated from true involvement — it changes the STAGE

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma

  • 10–15% of pediatric/adolescent NHL vs 3% adult; median 30 y, M:F 3:1
  • Nodes + skin, bone, soft tissue, lung
  • HALLMARK CELLS — eccentric horseshoe nuclei, pale paranuclear zone; doughnut cells
  • CD30 strong & DIFFUSE, CD2 +, CD4 +, EMA +; CD3 often −; ALK +/−
  • Patterns: sinusoidal (mimics carcinoma/melanoma) · paracortical · intravascular · diffuse (can be nodular with bands → mimics nodular sclerosis)
  • ALK+ ~80% long-term survival; ALK− ~50% 5-y OS
  • Breast implant-associated: CD4 +, 5-y OS 100%
  • Always ALK-stain when considering Hodgkin

Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma

  • HTLV-1; Japan, Caribbean, intertropical Africa, Middle East, S. America, PNG; median 68 y
  • Generalized LAD, skin lesions, hepatosplenomegaly
  • FLOWER CELLS — MULTILOBATED (contrast: Sézary = convoluted/cerebriform)
  • Four subtypes: acute, lymphoma, chronic, smoldering — by organ involvement, LDH, calcium, leukemic burden
5 · METASTASES & TECHNIQUES

Metastatic disease in a node

  • Look in the SINUSES; the giveaway is COHESION (lymphocytes are discohesive)
  • Glands with lumens = adenocarcinoma; mucin in the background; signet ring single cells = gastric
  • Papillary thyroid carcinoma: psammoma bodies, nuclear enlargement, irregularity, Orphan Annie eye clearing, grooves, pseudoinclusions
  • Keratin + carcinoma · S100/SOX10 + melanoma · CD30 + ALCL

Techniques

  • IHC (fixed tissue) · flow cytometry (needs fresh) · NGS (defining mutations) · PCR (clonality: IgH/light chain, TCR) · cytogenetics/FISH (translocations)
  • Reactive = polyclonal; lymphoma = monoclonal
  • t(14;18) BCL2 → follicular · t(8;14) MYC → Burkitt — both driven by the chromosome 14 IgH promoter
