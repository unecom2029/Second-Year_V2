Orientation — what this lecture is actually teaching

Hematopoiesis and the neoplastic disorders

Dr. Sirotnikov opens by saying this is the last heme lecture, and that it covers hematopoiesis with a particular focus on neoplastic disorders and how to approach classification, with some practical examples. That sentence is the whole shape of the file. This is not a clinical oncology lecture — it is a hematopathologist showing you how the diagnosis is actually made, from the moment the needle enters the iliac crest to the moment an integrated diagnosis is signed out.

🔑 The five things this lecture wants you to be able to do
  1. Know what a good specimen is.Spicules on the aspirate, 1 cm of trabecular bone on the core. A hemodilute aspirate and a core full of cortex tell you nothing, and every downstream study inherits that failure.
  2. Know what each ancillary study answers.Flow cytometry answers what lineage and what maturation stage. FISH answers is this specific rearrangement present. Karyotype answers what does the whole genome look like. They are not interchangeable.
  3. Read a blast.Cell size against a red cell, chromatin texture, cytoplasmic volume, and whether there are Auer rods. Fine chromatin means immature; clumped chromatin means mature.
  4. Use the two-axis classification.Precursor versus mature, then lymphoid versus myeloid. Everything in the WHO scheme hangs off those two questions.
  5. Know why the genetics matter.Not for their own sake — because they decide prognosis and therefore whether the patient gets standard chemotherapy or a bone marrow transplant, and occasionally because they name a targeted drug.
🧠 The habit worth stealing from this lecture

Every case in this file is worked in the same order, and it is worth internalizing as a sequence rather than a list of facts: specimen adequacy → low power (is the marrow replaced?) → high power (what do the cells look like?) → flow cytometry (lineage and maturity) → immunostains on tissue → cytogenetics and FISH → integrated diagnosis. The final line of every case summary in the deck is literally the phrase “final integrated diagnosis,” because the diagnosis is the integration and not any one of the studies.

⚠️ One naming point that trips people up

Dr. Sirotnikov notes that some genetic abnormalities are not unique to a single lineage. KMT2A (previously called MLL) is the notorious one — it occurs in both acute myeloid leukemia and B-lymphoblastic leukemia. He describes real patients who presented with B-lymphoblastic leukemia, were treated, and relapsed as acute myeloid leukemia. A rearrangement is therefore a property of the clone, not a lineage label.

Topic 1 — Getting the sample

Before any diagnosis exists

Bone marrow aspiration and core biopsy

Everything downstream depends on this step, which is why the lecture starts here rather than with disease. A needle enters the posterior iliac crest — most commonly on the right, sometimes the left — and a small volume of liquid marrow is withdrawn.

⚠️ The two ways a specimen fails
  • Hemodilute aspirate — no clear spicules, and the smear is predominantly peripheral blood elements. You will not see the background myeloid precursors, erythroid precursors and megakaryocytes that should be obvious at low power. Case 1 in this lecture is exactly this situation, and it still yielded a diagnosis only because the blasts were so numerous.
  • Wrong tissue on the core — cortical bone, periosteum or cartilage instead of trabecular bone. Dr. Sirotnikov notes this “can hamper our diagnostic abilities,” because there is no marrow to evaluate.

Q: A marrow aspirate smear shows abundant red cells, a few neutrophils, and no recognizable myeloid precursors, erythroid precursors or megakaryocytes at low power. What has happened, and does it invalidate the case?

Answer

The aspirate is hemodilute — no spicules were obtained, so what is on the slide is essentially peripheral blood rather than marrow. Normally at low power you should see background marrow elements: myeloid precursors, erythroid precursors and megakaryocytes, the last being obvious even at low magnification because of their large complex nuclei and ample pink cytoplasm. A hemodilute aspirate does not automatically invalidate the case — in Case 1 the blasts were so numerous that the diagnosis was still reached — but it means the core biopsy carries the weight for cellularity and architecture, and any blast percentage from the aspirate must be interpreted cautiously.

Topic 2 — Ancillary studies
Lineage and maturity

The study that answers “what kind of cell is this?”

Flow cytometry

The flow cytometer — cells pass one at a time through a laser, and scatter plus fluorescence are recorded for each

🧠 What the machine is physically measuring
  • Cells pass individually through a stream, and an optical system of one or more lasers, filters, mirrors and detectors captures the emitted fluorescence and the scattered light.
  • Each cluster of differentiation (CD) marker is bound by an antibody conjugated to a fluorochrome that emits at a different wavelength — so the wavelength tells you which marker, and the intensity tells you how much.
  • The laboratory evaluates roughly 36 markers per case, alongside the two scatter parameters.

Forward scatter

FSC

Cell size.

Side scatter

SSC

Cell complexity — granularity. Granulocytes have the highest side scatter because of their granules; lymphocytes the lowest.

CD45

Pan-hematopoietic

Mature lymphocytes are bright. The blast gate is CD45-dim — weaker than mature lymphocytes, sometimes almost negative.

🔑 Qualitative and quantitative at the same time

Flow answers two questions per marker. Qualitatively: is the marker present or absent? Quantitatively: how strongly? Mean fluorescence is generally not reported as a number — instead the result is expressed as dim or bright relative to a normal internal control population. That relative language matters: “CD10 bright” in B-lymphoblastic leukemia and “CD45 dim” in the blast gate are both intensity statements, not simple positives.

⚠️ Flow needs cells that are alive

Flow cytometry requires a viable single-cell suspension, which is why fresh material must be allocated to it before anything is fixed. This is the same constraint that governs conventional cytogenetics, and it is why the lymph node protocol in the companion lecture insists on triaging fresh tissue.

Q: On a CD45-versus-side-scatter plot, where do you expect to find blasts, and why is that position diagnostically useful?

Answer

In the blast gate — CD45-dim with low side scatter. Blasts sit dim for CD45 because CD45 intensity increases with hematopoietic maturation, so an immature cell expresses less of it than a mature lymphocyte; and they sit low on side scatter because they are agranular compared with granulocytes. The position is useful because it lets the analyst isolate the population of interest before asking any lineage question at all — every subsequent marker is then read only on the cells inside that gate.

Topic 3 — Ancillary studies
Genetics

Targeted questions and whole-genome questions

FISH and karyotype

FISH begins with two elements — a labeled DNA probe and a target sequence — which are denatured, then allowed to anneal

🧠 The mechanics, in one pass

A DNA probe is labeled either directly with a fluorochrome or indirectly with a hapten. Probe and target DNA are denatured to single strands, then combined so complementary sequences anneal. An indirectly labeled probe needs one extra step: an enzymatic or immunologic detection system to make the non-fluorescent hapten visible.

🔑 Once you understand one dual fusion, you understand them all

Dr. Sirotnikov says this twice. For any dual-color dual-fusion probe, the reading is identical: normal is two red and two green sitting apart; abnormal is the appearance of yellow where a red and a green now overlap. The only thing that changes between CML and APL is which chromosomes the probes sit on.

Karyotype — the whole-genome view

A normal male karyotype, 46,XY — the reference against which every abnormality is called

  1. Culture the cells.Marrow aspirate is sent out and grown in medium containing stimulants for cell division. Turnaround is 3 days for blood and marrow, up to 7–14 days for skin and prenatal samples.
  2. Arrest in metaphase and harvest.Chromosomes are only visible as discrete structures during metaphase.

🩺 What karyotype adds that FISH cannot

FISH answers a question you already thought to ask. Karyotype shows you the things you did not ask about — which is why it is sent on every new leukemia and on every bone marrow failure patient, in the latter case specifically to look for additional abnormalities suggesting progression. It captures both numeric change (hypodiploidy and hyperdiploidy in B-lymphoblastic leukemia) and structural change (PML::RARA, BCR::ABL1).

Q: A dual-color dual-fusion FISH for PML::RARA shows, in an interphase nucleus, one isolated red signal, one isolated green signal, and two yellow signals. What does this mean, and why are there two fusion signals rather than one?

Answer

It is a positive result — a balanced t(15;17) translocation. There are two yellow fusion signals because a balanced translocation is reciprocal: material moves in both directions, so a fusion is created on the derivative of chromosome 15 and on the derivative of chromosome 17. The remaining single red and single green signals are the normal, untranslocated copies of each chromosome. This is exactly the pattern the lecture describes for BCR::ABL1 as well, where the two products are the Philadelphia chromosome and its reciprocal partner.

Q: Why would you choose a break-apart probe rather than a dual-fusion probe to work up a suspected Burkitt lymphoma?

Answer

Because in Burkitt lymphoma the clinically necessary question is simply “is MYC rearranged?” — not “is MYC fused to this one specific partner?” A break-apart probe flanks the MYC breakpoint and separates whenever MYC is rearranged, regardless of the partner. A dual-fusion probe would only detect the partner it was designed for and would miss the variant translocations. As Dr. Sirotnikov puts it, you generally just need to prove the rearrangement is present; the partner can be identified afterwards by additional studies such as RNA sequencing.

Topic 4 — The baseline

The map that the classification is built on

Normal hematopoiesis and the classification framework

Normal hematopoiesis — stem cell to committed precursor to mature form, with lineage-specific growth factor receptors on the right

🔑 The two properties that define a hematopoietic stem cell

Pluripotency

Breadth

The ability of a single stem cell to generate all mature blood cells.

Self-renewal

Durability

When the stem cell divides, at least one daughter cell must self-renew — otherwise the stem cell pool is progressively depleted.

Self-renewing divisions occur within a specialized marrow niche, where stromal cells and secreted factors nurture and protect the stem cells. From the multipotent progenitor the tree splits into an early progenitor with lymphoid potential and one with myeloid potential; the myeloid side proceeds through colony-forming units to each mature lineage.

The two-axis framework — the shape of the entire WHO classification

🧠 Two questions, four boxes
  • Axis 1 — precursor or mature? Answered morphologically by chromatin texture (fine and open versus clumped) and immunophenotypically by markers of immaturity (TdT, CD34, CD117).
  • Axis 2 — lymphoid or myeloid? Answered by flow cytometry, and on tissue by immunostains.

Within the mature myeloid box the WHO then subdivides into myeloproliferative neoplasms, myelodysplastic neoplasms, and combined myelodysplastic/myeloproliferative neoplasms. Within the mature lymphoid box sit the lymphomas covered in the companion lecture.

⚠️ Some abnormalities cross the lineage boundary

KMT2A (formerly MLL) rearrangement occurs in both acute myeloid leukemia and B-lymphoblastic leukemia. Dr. Sirotnikov describes patients who presented with B-lymphoblastic leukemia, were treated, and relapsed as acute myeloid leukemia — the same clone, a different lineage output. Do not treat a rearrangement as though it names a lineage.

Q: A marrow contains sheets of cells with delicate, evenly dispersed chromatin, inconspicuous cytoplasm, and no Auer rods. Before any stain is performed, what have you already established and what remains open?

Answer

Established: this is a precursor (blast) proliferation. Fine chromatin is the morphologic signature of immaturity; mature processes show clumped chromatin. Still open: the lineage. Scant cytoplasm leans lymphoid and more ample cytoplasm leans myeloid, and Auer rods would settle it as myeloid — but none are present, so morphology alone cannot finish the job. As the lecture puts it, flow will ultimately dictate the phenotype.

In a 6-year-old you expect marrow cellularity of about 80–90% at most. 🔑 Case 1 summary — the integrated diagnosis
  • Patient presenting with pancytopenia and leg pain.
  • Aspirate hemodilute, with approximately 30% blasts — oval nuclei, fine chromatin, scant cytoplasm.
  • Flow: discrete neoplastic blast population — CD45 dim, CD19+, CD10 bright, TdT+; negative for CD20 and surface kappa and lambda.
  • Core biopsy: hypercellular (100%) with sheets of blasts, PAX5+ CD79a+ TdT+.
  • Final diagnosis: B-lymphoblastic leukemia — pending cytogenetics and FISH, which are sent on every new case for prognostic abnormalities.

Q: Two findings in this flow panel were both used to argue “immature.” Name them, and explain why each works.

Answer

(1) Absence of surface kappa and lambda light chains. Every mature B cell expresses one light chain or the other; a B-lineage population expressing neither has not yet completed its receptor, so it must be a precursor. (2) CD20 negativity. CD20 is acquired at more mature stages of B-cell development — it is present on later hematogones and on all mature B cells until plasma cell differentiation — so its absence on a CD19-positive population argues the same way. TdT positivity then confirms immaturity directly. Note that these are three independent arguments converging, which is what makes the interpretation safe.

Topic 6 — Precursor neoplasms

The disease behind Case 1

B- and T-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma

🩺 Clinical behavior
  • Leukemia involves peripheral blood and bone marrow; lymphoblastic lymphoma presents with lymphadenopathy or a rapidly growing mass. Same disease, named for where it is.
  • Extensive marrow involvement produces bone marrow failure — thrombocytopenia, anemia and/or neutropenia — and the sequelae follow directly: easy bruising, fatigue, and predisposition to infection.
  • Abrupt onset, within days to a few weeks of first symptoms. Think aggressive.
  • Bone pain is a characteristic and helpful clue.
  • Lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly may be seen; in children, B-lymphoblastic lymphoma can present in the head and neck.
  • 75% of cases occur under age 6, with a second peak in the fifth decade.

⚠️ Mixed phenotype acute leukemia — the pitfall behind these marker lists

Lineage-defining markers matter because occasionally a case will not obey them. Two patterns occur:

  • Two separate blast populations with different lineage-defining markers. Dr. Sirotnikov describes a recent case where T-lymphoblastic leukemia was diagnosed on peripheral blood flow, and the subsequent marrow showed an expanded population of monoblasts.
  • A single population expressing multiple lineage-defining markers.

The assignment rules are asymmetric: for B lineage, strong CD19 plus only weak or moderate expression of one other B marker (CD10, CD79a, CD22) suffices. For T lineage, cytoplasmic CD3 is lineage defining. For myeloid, MPO.

Long-term remission in children is >90% in B-ALL and 85% in T-ALL, the latter often treated with more aggressive therapy. Q: A 14-year-old has a mediastinal mass, and flow on the biopsy shows a TdT-positive population co-expressing CD4 and CD8 with cytoplasmic CD3. Why does that CD4/CD8 pattern point to a precursor rather than a mature T-cell process?

Answer

Because no mature peripheral T cell co-expresses CD4 and CD8 — a mature cell has committed to one or the other. Co-expression is a property of the double-positive cortical thymocyte, so a neoplasm showing it is frozen at a thymic stage. This is T-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma, and the mediastinal location follows from the thymus being where T cells mature. Cytoplasmic CD3 is the lineage-defining marker here, and it is emphasized precisely because surface CD3 is frequently lost. Note also that CD4 and CD8 may alternatively be both negative in this disease.

Q: Why does the lecture insist on PAX5 for tissue when flow cytometry has already established B lineage?

Answer

Because flow and tissue answer the question on different material, and in some cases flow is unreliable. Dr. Sirotnikov notes that when marrow involvement is scant, or when the aspirate is hemodilute, he likes to corroborate the findings with stains on the core biopsy — PAX5, CD79a and TdT — because immunohistochemistry shows you where the cells are in the tissue, not just that they exist in a suspension. PAX5 is described as sensitive and specific for B lineage on tissue, which makes it the workhorse in that setting.

Case 2 — gum enlargement and a high white count

Recognizing monocytic differentiation

🩺 Presentation

A previously healthy 68-year-old male presents with a two-week history of worsening fatigue, pallor and decreased appetite, plus easy bruising and unexplained nosebleeds. Examination shows widespread petechiae and ecchymoses, mild hepatosplenomegaly, no lymphadenopathy — and the finding that makes the case: gingival hypertrophy.

Gingival hypertrophy — Dr. Sirotnikov calls this “a good one to commit to memory”

🔑 Why gums point to monocytes

Gingival infiltration is the classic extramedullary manifestation of monocytic and myelomonocytic acute myeloid leukemia. It belongs to the same family of tissue-seeking behavior as leukemia cutis, which is likewise commonest in monocytic and myelomonocytic disease. A high white count with gum enlargement should move monocytic AML to the top of the list before any slide is examined.

🧠 The morphologic clue: blue-grey cytoplasm

Fine chromatin with multiple nucleoli says immature. What says monocytic is the ample blue-grey cytoplasm. Dr. Sirotnikov: “when we think blue-grey cytoplasm, we tend to associate this with monocytic lineage — that’s a morphologic clue.” Monoblasts and promonocytes are counted as blast equivalents, which is how the blast threshold is reached.

Flow cytometry — confirming monocytic differentiation

CD45 vs side scatter — the expanded population sits bright for CD45

CD14 shows subset loss — the left shift that proves immaturity

🔑 The reasoning that makes this a leukemia rather than a monocytosis
  • CD33 bright — a myeloid marker, often brightest in monocytic lineage.
  • CD4, CD14, CD64, CD11c — the monocytic panel, corroborating the blue-grey cytoplasm.
  • CD14 is only partially expressed. If these were all mature monocytes, CD14 would be uniformly bright. The subset loss is the evidence of an expanded immature monocytic population.
  • Aberrant CD2 and CD56 — CD2 belongs to T cells and CD56 to NK cells. Aberrant expression on a myeloid population is evidence of neoplasia.
🩺 Case 2 summary — the integrated diagnosis
  • Anemia, thrombocytopenia and leukocytosis, with gum enlargement on examination.
  • Aspirate with numerous blasts and blast equivalents — oval to bilobed nuclei, fine chromatin, variably prominent nucleoli, ample vacuolated cytoplasm.
  • Flow: discrete population, ~74% of total events, expressing CD45, CD33, CD15, CD2, CD56, CD4, HLA-DR, CD11c, CD13, CD16 (partial), CD14 (subset loss), CD64, CD9, CD38, CD58.
  • FISH returned a KMT2A rearrangement.
  • Final integrated diagnosis: acute monocytic/monoblastic leukemia with KMT2A rearrangement.

Q: The blasts in this case sat bright for CD45, whereas Case 1’s blasts were CD45 dim. Does that argue against a diagnosis of acute leukemia?

Answer

No. CD45 intensity rises with hematopoietic maturation, and monocytic cells are CD45 bright even when they are neoplastic and immature — so a monoblastic leukemia does not sit in the classic CD45-dim blast gate the way a lymphoblastic leukemia does. The immaturity is established a different way here: by the subset loss of CD14, which would be uniformly bright on a fully mature monocyte population, together with the aberrant CD2 and CD56 expression and the morphology. The lesson is that the blast gate is a useful convention, not a rule that every acute leukemia obeys.

Topic 8 — Acute myeloid leukemia

Why the genetics are the classification

AML — clonal hematopoiesis, pathogenesis and classification

🧠 Clonal hematopoiesis — the state that precedes disease

The presence of a clone of hematopoietic cells carrying at least one acquired pathogenic mutation in a cancer gene, in an individual with normal blood counts.

Q: Why is DIC in APL treated as a reason to start therapy before the confirmatory test returns, when in almost every other situation you would wait for the diagnosis?

Q: A 77-year-old has a macrocytic anemia and a hypercellular marrow with dysplastic changes in two lineages and 6% blasts. What are you obliged to do before calling this a myelodysplastic neoplasm?

Answer

Exclude the mimics, because MDN is a clinicopathologic diagnosis of exclusion. Specifically: vitamin B12 and folate deficiency (which also produce macrocytosis and dysplastic-looking change), alcohol and tobacco use, medications, viral infection, liver disease and hypothyroidism — and review whether the patient had prior chemotherapy or radiation, which would make this therapy-related. Only when those are cleared does the morphology mean what it appears to mean. If the diagnosis stands, 6% blasts places it in MDN with increased blasts, type 1, and cytogenetics then drives risk stratification.

Q: Why is a micro-megakaryocyte more useful diagnostically than simply noting that megakaryocytes look abnormal?

Answer

Because different myeloid neoplasms have characteristically different megakaryocyte abnormalities, and the specific abnormality is what discriminates. For myelodysplastic neoplasm, the finding is the micro-megakaryocyte: small and hypolobated, roughly the size of a neighboring myeloid cell. Contrast this with the myeloproliferative neoplasms — bulbous, cloud-like megakaryocytes in primary myelofibrosis and staghorn hyperlobated forms in essential thrombocythemia, and dwarf megakaryocytes in chronic myeloid leukemia. Saying “abnormal” discards the information; naming the shape keeps it.

Because the platelets are qualitatively abnormal as well as numerous — the count does not reflect function. On the bleeding side, roughly one third of patients acquire von Willebrand disease of uncertain etiology, which impairs primary hemostasis. So a raised platelet count in this disease predicts neither safety from bleeding nor protection from thrombosis.

🧠 Why IgM causes the symptoms — the pathogenesis of hyperviscosity

IgM is a pentamer and therefore physically large. Large molecules at high concentration raise serum viscosity (clinically once IgM exceeds roughly 6 g/dL), and hyperviscosity produces blurred vision, headache, epistaxis and shortness of breath. Other associations: cryoglobulin activity with vasculitic skin lesions, renal complications, and IgM-related neuropathy.

Q: A 70-year-old has 12% clonal marrow plasma cells, an M protein of 2.1 g/dL, normal calcium and creatinine, hemoglobin 13 g/dL and a normal skeletal survey. What is the diagnosis?

Answer

Smoldering multiple myeloma. Work the criteria in order. The plasma cell percentage is ≥10%, which already excludes MGUS (which requires <10%, M protein <3 g/dL, and no end-organ damage — all three). But there is no CRAB: calcium and creatinine are normal, hemoglobin is normal, and there are no lytic lesions. Nor are the SLiM criteria met — plasma cells are not ≥60%, and no light-chain ratio or MRI finding is given. Myeloma requires a plasma cell threshold plus a defining event; here the threshold is met and the event is absent, which is precisely the definition of smoldering disease.

Q: Why does Waldenström macroglobulinemia cause blurred vision and epistaxis when other plasma cell dyscrasias with similar M-protein concentrations do not?

Answer

Because the paraprotein is IgM, which is a pentamer — five immunoglobulin units joined together, so it is far larger than the monomeric IgG or IgA of typical myeloma. Size, not just concentration, drives serum viscosity, so an IgM paraprotein produces hyperviscosity at concentrations that would be harmless for a smaller molecule. The clinical consequences follow from sluggish flow in small vessels: blurred vision, headache, epistaxis and shortness of breath. Note also the structural exception this disease represents — plasma cell dyscrasias are marrow-based, except Waldenström.

Topic 16 — Histiocytic

The one histiocytic neoplasm to know cold

Langerhans cell histiocytosis

The histiocytic and dendritic cell neoplasms — a complicated family with one high-yield member

Incidence5–9 per million (pediatric)1–2 per million (adult)
🔑 Clinical — single system or multisystem
  • Single system — skin, pituitary or bone. Multisystem — bone, skin, liver, spleen, lymph node, or lung with cystic nodules.
  • Painless to painful osteolytic lesions when bone is involved.
  • Diabetes insipidus when the pituitary is involved.
  • Risk organs — involvement of liver, spleen or bone marrow — are specified separately because they drive risk.
  • LCH can co-occur with other myeloid neoplasms, and with Rosai–Dorfman disease and other histiocytic neoplasms.

🧠 Morphology, and one thing that is tested but not done

Langerhans cells are defined by nuclear irregularity — described as coffee bean-shaped with nuclear grooves — and have abundant cytoplasm, frequently accompanied by numerous eosinophils. Birbeck granules are the tennis-racket shaped organelles seen on electron microscopy. Dr. Sirotnikov notes dryly that electron microscopy is not performed in practice, “but they like to test this.”

LCH subtypes, including the risk-organ split

🩺 Two named syndromes worth recognizing

Letterer–Siwe disease

Multifocal multisystem

Typically children under 2. Cutaneous lesions on the trunk and scalp, with hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, pulmonary lesions and destructive osteolytic bone lesions.

Hand–Schüller–Christian triad

The classic triad

Calvarial (skull) bone defects, diabetes insipidus, and exophthalmos.

Q: A child has a lytic skull lesion and polyuria. A biopsy shows cells with grooved nuclei and abundant eosinophils. Which two stains confirm the diagnosis, and what mutation should be sought?

Answer

CD1a and Langerin (CD207) — these two define Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and are what separate it from the other S100-positive histiocytoses such as Rosai–Dorfman disease, which is CD1a and CD207 negative. S100 and CD68 are also positive but are shared with other entities. The mutation to seek is BRAF p.V600E, which matters therapeutically because it is targetable. The clinical picture given is the classic combination of an osteolytic bone lesion with diabetes insipidus from pituitary involvement.

Topic 17 — Lymphoid organs

Two organs, and why each one matters

Spleen and thymus

Spleen

Spleen — red pulp with nodules of white pulp around central arterioles

🔑 The four functions
  • Phagocytosis of blood cells.
  • Antibody production.
  • Hematopoiesis — a site in fetal life, and again in myeloproliferative neoplasms as extramedullary hematopoiesis.
  • Sequestration of formed blood elements. The spleen normally harbors 30–40% of the total platelet mass; with splenomegaly this rises to 80–90%, sequestered in the red pulp and producing thrombocytopenia.
⚠️ Splenic insufficiency

Whether from splenectomy or from auto-infarction in sickle cell disease, loss of splenic function causes susceptibility to sepsis from encapsulated bacteria — pneumococci and Haemophilus influenzae.

Q: Why is the thymus the expected site for T-lymphoblastic lymphoma, and what other lymphoma should stay on the differential for an anterior mediastinal mass?

Answer

Because T cells mature in the thymus — a neoplasm of T-cell precursors therefore arises where those precursors normally live, and the “-oma” form presents as a thymic mass while the leukemic form fills the marrow. The other mature lymphoid neoplasm that involves the thymus is primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma. A third consideration for any anterior mediastinal mass is thymoma or thymic carcinoma, which are epithelial rather than lymphoid, and in a young patient nodular sclerosis classic Hodgkin lymphoma from the companion lecture.

Every term and abbreviation, expanded

Glossary and abbreviations

Specimens and studies

Bone marrow aspiration
Withdrawal of liquid marrow through a needle, usually from the posterior iliac crest. Adequacy depends on obtaining spicules.
Spicule
A small red or pink tissue fragment suspended in aspirated marrow fluid — the evidence that true marrow, not blood, was sampled.
Hemodilute
An aspirate composed predominantly of peripheral blood elements, without spicules or background marrow precursors.
Core biopsy
A cylinder of marrow with its architecture intact. Adult adequacy is 1 cm of trabecular bone; cortex, periosteum and cartilage are not evaluable.
Flow cytometry
Cells pass individually through lasers; forward scatter reports size, side scatter reports granularity, and fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies report surface and cytoplasmic markers. Requires a viable cell suspension.
Blast gate
The CD45-dim, low-side-scatter region on a flow plot where immature cells fall.
FISH
Fluorescence in situ hybridization — labeled DNA probes annealed to denatured target DNA. Three designs: break apart, dual fusion, enumeration.
Karyotype
Cultured cells arrested in metaphase, stained to produce G-banding. Detects numeric and structural change genome-wide. 3 days for blood and marrow.
Dry tap
Failure to obtain aspirate. Classically primary myelofibrosis and hairy cell leukemia.

Morphologic terms

Blast
An immature cell with fine, delicate chromatin, typically 3–5 nucleoli in the myeloid form. Contrast with the clumped chromatin of mature cells.
Blast equivalent
A cell counted as a blast for threshold purposes despite not being a classical blast — monoblasts and promonocytes in monocytic AML, abnormal promyelocytes in APL.
Auer rod
A slender pink cytoplasmic rod of fused primary granules. Defines myeloid lineage. Bundles are characteristic of APL.
Myelocyte
A mid-stage granulocyte precursor with an oval nucleus and a perinuclear hof, sitting after the promyelocyte. Its expansion is the myelocyte bulge of CML.
Left shift
An increase in immature granulocyte forms in the blood.
Leukoerythroblastosis
Nucleated red blood cells + immature white cells + teardrop cells in the peripheral blood. Classic for primary myelofibrosis.
Pseudo-Pelger–Huët cell
A hyposegmented, bilobed neutrophil instead of the normal 3–5 lobes. A feature of dysgranulopoiesis.
Ring sideroblast
A nucleated erythroid precursor with iron-positive granules encircling at least one third of the nuclear circumference, demonstrated on an iron (Prussian blue) stain.
Micro-megakaryocyte
A small, hypolobated megakaryocyte, roughly the size of a neighboring myeloid cell. Characteristic of myelodysplastic neoplasm.
Dwarf megakaryocyte
The characteristically small megakaryocyte of CML.
Internuclear bridging
A true extension of nuclear material between erythroid precursors — a real feature of dysplasia, to be distinguished from artefactual cytoplasmic bridges.
Rouleaux
Red cells stacked like coins, caused by increased cationic proteins. Seen with paraproteins, and also in infection, connective tissue disease and chronic liver disease.
Clock-face chromatin
Chromatin clumped at the nuclear periphery — the plasma cell nucleus, accompanied by an eccentric position and a perinuclear hof.
Dutcher body
An intranuclear immunoglobulin pseudo-inclusion.
Russell body
A cytoplasmic immunoglobulin inclusion. A plasma cell packed with them is a Mott cell.
Birbeck granule
The tennis-racket shaped organelle of the Langerhans cell, visible only on electron microscopy.
Hassall corpuscle
The whorled epithelial structure of the thymic medulla; cystically dilated in Down syndrome.

Diseases and states

Clonal hematopoiesis
An acquired pathogenic mutation in a cancer gene in someone with normal blood counts. 10% by age 70, 30% by 90; TET2 and DNMT3A commonest; associated with a pro-inflammatory state.
Myeloid sarcoma
An extramedullary mass of blasts.
Leukemia cutis
Leukemic infiltration of epidermis, dermis or subcutis; commonest with monocytic and myelomonocytic AML, but also seen in ALL, T-cell leukemias and CLL.
Differentiation syndrome
The cytokine-driven complication of ATRA or arsenic therapy in APL, caused by mass differentiation of the arrested promyelocytes.
Leukemoid reaction
Severe reactive leukocytosis with left shift, as in sepsis. Distinguished from CML by absence of basophilia and absence of BCR::ABL1.
Panmyelosis
Increase in all marrow cell lineages — the marrow signature of polycythemia vera.
Aquagenic pruritus
Itching worsened by bathing. A presenting clue in polycythemia vera.
Erythromelalgia
Throbbing and burning of hands and feet from arteriolar occlusion by platelet aggregates. Seen in ET and PV.
M protein
The monoclonal immunoglobulin produced by a plasma cell clone; the M spike on serum protein electrophoresis, in the gamma region.
CRAB
The end-organ damage defining multiple myeloma: hyperCalcemia, Renal insufficiency, Anemia, Bone lesions.
SLiM
Myeloma-defining biomarkers without CRAB: Sixty percent plasma cells, Light chain ratio ≥100, More than one focal MRI lesion.
Waldenström macroglobulinemia
Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma plus an IgM paraprotein. IgM is a pentamer, hence hyperviscosity.
Plasmacytoid lymphocyte
A cell with the eccentric nucleus of a plasma cell but not yet its abundant cytoplasm.
Letterer–Siwe disease
Multifocal multisystem LCH, typically under age 2.
Hand–Schüller–Christian triad
Calvarial bone defects, diabetes insipidus and exophthalmos.

1 · SPECIMEN & STUDIES

Adequacy

  • Aspirate: need spicules. No spicules = hemodilute = peripheral blood
  • Core: 1 cm trabecular bone (adult). Cortex/periosteum/cartilage useless
  • Site: posterior iliac crest

What each study answers

  • Flow → lineage + maturity. Needs live cells. FSC = size, SSC = granularity
  • Blast gate = CD45 dim, low SSC (but monocytic blasts are CD45 bright)
  • FISH → is this lesion present. Karyotype → whole genome, 3 days

Three FISH probes

  • Break apart — normal overlaps, abnormal separates (≥1 signal diameter). MYC, EWSR1
  • Dual fusion — normal 2 red + 2 green apart; abnormal = yellow ×2. BCR::ABL1, PML::RARA
  • Enumeration — centromere vs gene. More = amplification (iAMP21), fewer = deletion (−7, del(5q))

Morphology first principles

  • Fine chromatin = precursor. Clumped = mature.
  • Size against a red cell; scant cytoplasm → lymphoid, ample → myeloid
  • Auer rods define myeloid; blue-grey cytoplasm → monocytic
2 · ACUTE LEUKEMIA

B-ALL

  • CD19+, CD10 bright, TdT+, CD34+; CD20−, surface kappa/lambda −. Tissue: PAX5
  • 75% under age 6; second peak 5th decade. Bone pain, abrupt onset, marrow failure
  • Good: ETV6::RUNX1 t(12;21), hyperdiploidy >50 (4, 10, 17)
  • Bad: hypodiploidy, iAMP21, age <1 or >10, WBC >50,000, residual disease
  • Remission >90% B-ALL, 85% T-ALL (children)

T-ALL

  • Cytoplasmic CD3 = lineage defining (surface CD3 often lost)
  • CD4/CD8 co-expressed or both negative; CD1a, CD99, CD34, CD117, TdT
  • Lymphoma form = thymic/mediastinal mass

AML essentials

  • ≥20% blasts — lowered if a defining genetic lesion is present
  • Myeloblast: delicate chromatin, 3–5 nucleoli, ± Auer rods
  • Good: PML::RARA, RUNX1::RUNX1T1, CBFB::MYH11 → standard chemo
  • Poor: KMT2A, myelodysplasia-related → transplant
  • Megakaryocytic (CD41/CD61) → fibrosis, think Down syndrome

APL — the emergency

  • t(15;17) PML::RARA; abnormal promyelocytes = blast equivalents, bilobed
  • DIC from tissue factor; ↓fibrinogen, ↑D-dimer, ↑PT/PTT, ↓platelets
  • Suspected leukemia + DIC = APL until proven otherwise. Pre-ATRA sample → start ATRA → confirm by FISH
  • CD13/33/117+, CD34− HLA-DR− (may be + in microgranular). High FSC/SSC
  • ATRA + ATO → >90% cure; complication = differentiation syndrome
3 · MDN & MPN

Myelodysplastic neoplasm

  • Cytopenia + dysplasia (≥10% of a lineage); median age 77; diagnosis of exclusion
  • Exclude B12/folate, alcohol, drugs, viral, liver, thyroid; prior chemo/RT → therapy-related
  • Micro-megakaryocytes, pseudo-Pelger–Huët, hypogranular neutrophils, ring sideroblasts (≥1/3 of nucleus, iron stain)
  • Blasts: <5 / 5–9 / 10–19 / ≥20 = AML
  • Worst cytogenetics: −7, del(7q); also 5, 8, 20, complex, TP53

CML

  • BCR::ABL1 t(9;22); Philadelphia = derivative chromosome 22
  • Myelocyte bulge + neutrophilia + basophilia; dwarf megakaryocytes
  • vs leukemoid reaction: no basophilia, no BCR::ABL1
  • >95% present in chronic phase; imatinib; survival 80–95%
  • Blast phase: myeloid (MPO or monocytic markers) or lymphoblastic, usually B

PV / PMF / ET

  • PV — JAK2 only; panmyelosis; aquagenic pruritus; phlebotomy + hydroxyurea; 20% → post-PV MF
  • PMF — JAK2/CALR/MPL; granulocytes + megakaryocytes only; leukoerythroblastosis, teardrops, dry tap
  • ET — indolent, 12–15 yr; cellularity normal/mildly ↑; 1/3 acquired von Willebrand; erythromelalgia

🔑 Megakaryocyte shape decides

  • PMF = bulbous / cloud-like
  • ET = staghorn (hyperlobated, elongated)
  • MDN = micro (small, hypolobated)
  • CML = dwarf
4 · PLASMA CELL · LCH · ORGANS

Plasma cell dyscrasias

  • M protein = gamma-region spike on SPEP; CD138+; marrow-based except Waldenström; no lymphadenopathy
  • MGUS: <10% plasma cells, M <3 g/dL, no CRAB — all three
  • Smoldering: 10–60% or M ≥3 g/dL, no CRAB
  • Myeloma: ≥10% + CRAB or SLiM
  • CRAB = Ca >11, CrCl <40 / Cr >2, Hgb <10, lytic bone
  • SLiM = ≥60% plasma cells, FLC ratio ≥100, >1 MRI lesion
  • Plasma cell: eccentric nucleus, clock-face chromatin, perinuclear hof

LPL / Waldenström

  • Small lymphocytes + plasmacytoid lymphocytes + plasma cells; interstitial marrow pattern
  • WM = LPL + IgM. IgM is a pentamer → hyperviscosity → blurred vision, headache, epistaxis, dyspnea
  • MYD88 L265P; CD5− CD10−
  • Dutcher = intranuclear; Russell = cytoplasmic; Mott = packed with Russell

Langerhans cell histiocytosis

  • CD1a+ Langerin(CD207)+ S100+ CD68+; BRAF V600E
  • Grooved coffee-bean nuclei + eosinophils; Birbeck granules (tennis racket, EM)
  • Osteolytic lesions; pituitary → diabetes insipidus. Risk organs = liver, spleen, marrow
  • Letterer–Siwe (<2 yr, multisystem); Hand–Schüller–Christian = skull defect + DI + exophthalmos

Spleen & thymus

  • Spleen: phagocytosis, antibody, hematopoiesis, sequestration (30–40% of platelets → 80–90% if enlarged)
  • Asplenia/auto-infarction → encapsulated organisms (pneumococcus, H. influenzae)
  • Thymus: involutes after puberty, and with severe illness / HIV; DiGeorge → hypoplasia
  • Hassall corpuscles in medulla — cystic in Down syndrome. T-lymphoblastic lymphoma and primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma
