Hypercoagulable States

A practical lecture on thrombophilia by a working hematologist. Blood clots are a problem in every specialty, and the surprise of this hour is how much of the classic thrombophilia workup turns out not to change what you do. The decision that matters is not which mutation — it is provoked or unprovoked, and how long do we anticoagulate.

Orientation — the spine of the whole lecture

Dr. Winters opened by saying this would be “a very practical talk.” That framing matters, because the practical answer repeatedly contradicts the intuitive one. The intuitive story is: someone gets an unexplained clot, you hunt for the inherited defect that caused it, and you treat according to what you find. Almost every step of that story turns out to be wrong.

🔑 The five questions this lecture answers
  1. Why did this person clot?Not one cause — a threshold. Intrinsic risk plus triggering factors, minus prophylaxis. Cross the threshold and you get a clot.
  2. What do I start them on?Four direct oral anticoagulants, two of which need no heparin lead-in. Guidelines now prefer all four over warfarin — but there is a real list of patients for whom that is wrong.
  3. Should I do a thrombophilia workup?This is the heart of the lecture. Mostly no. Inherited thrombophilias raise the risk of a first clot but do not predict recurrence — and recurrence is the only thing the workup could change.
  4. How long do I anticoagulate?Determined almost entirely by provoked versus unprovoked, plus bleeding risk and patient preference. Three months equals six months. Then you stop, or you go indefinitely.
  5. Who is different?Antiphospholipid syndrome, cancer, pregnancy, renal failure, obesity, upper extremity clots. Each has its own answer, and several of them are the exceptions that break the general rules above.
⚠️ The one paradox to hold onto from the first minute

An inherited thrombophilia increases the risk that you will ever have a clot, but once you have had an unprovoked clot, it does not increase the risk that you will have another one. Those two statements sound contradictory and both are true. The reason is selection: having already had an unprovoked clot is itself such a strong marker of high intrinsic risk that knowing the genotype adds nothing on top. This single fact is why nearly the whole classic workup is being retired.

LO 1 — Risk factors and pathophysiology

Why clots happen

Risk factors and the threshold model of thrombosis

Dr. Winters asked the room whether anyone had memorized the coagulation cascade, then said: “I still haven’t memorized the coagulation cascade, all these years later. It’s never been useful.” What is useful is a much smaller picture — the handful of natural anticoagulants that hold the cascade in check, and what happens when one of them fails.

🧠 The only part of the cascade you need for this lecture

Clotting is a permanent tug-of-war. The cascade drives clot formation; a small set of natural anticoagulants restrain it. Every inherited thrombophilia is a failure on the restraining side, not an excess on the driving side — with one exception.

  • Protein C and its cofactor protein S — together they form activated protein C, which chops up factor Va and factor VIIIa. Lose either one and those two accelerator factors stay switched on.
  • Antithrombin (historically antithrombin III) — inhibits thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa directly. It is the target heparin works through, which is why antithrombin deficiency also causes apparent heparin resistance.
  • Factor V Leiden is the exception in mechanism: the protein is normal in amount but resistant to being cleaved by activated protein C. Same net effect — factor Va is not switched off.
  • The prothrombin gene mutation is the one gain-of-function: it raises the amount of prothrombin (factor II) circulating.

The two categories of risk factor: transient versus persistent

Everything downstream in this lecture — how long to anticoagulate, whether to test, whether to keep going indefinitely — hangs on which of these buckets a patient’s clot falls into. Learn the buckets before the details.

⏱️

Transient / provoked

Something identifiable happened, and then it stopped happening. The risk goes away with the provocation, so anticoagulation can usually stop at three months.

  • Surgery
  • Trauma — major trauma, or lower extremity trauma specifically
  • Acute medical illness — the classic being admission with pneumonia or another severe inflammatory illness
  • Immobilization — including a limb in a cast
  • Estrogen-containing contraception or hormone replacement therapy
  • Pregnancy and the postpartum period
  • Central venous catheter
♾️

Persistent

The risk factor is still there and is not going away. These patients drift toward indefinite anticoagulation, because stopping returns them to the same conditions that produced the first clot.

  • Obesity
  • Chronic medical illness — nephrotic syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, myeloproliferative neoplasms and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, and cancer
  • Paralysis
  • The thrombophilias themselves

Idiopathic sits apart: no cause found. Long air travel and long road trips are the traditional examples given, though they behave much more like weak transient factors.

The threshold model — the mental picture for the whole lecture

No single factor causes a clot. Risks stack, and a clot happens when the stack crosses a threshold. This is the slide to carry with you.

Figure 1 — The venous thromboembolism risk factor model. Inherited and acquired defects set a baseline intrinsic risk; triggers push the total up; prophylaxis pulls it down. Only when the sum crosses the threshold does a clot occur.

🔑 Why the threshold picture matters clinically

It explains the single most common real-world scenario: a woman with factor V Leiden who has lived forty years without a clot, then starts an estrogen-containing contraceptive and clots within months. Neither factor alone crossed the threshold; together they did. It also explains the converse and much more common outcome — as Dr. Winters put it, factor V Leiden is present in 5% of the population, meaning several people in a lecture hall of that size, and most women with it go through their entire adult lives, having children and taking estrogen, and never have a blood clot.

Q: A 24-year-old woman is found on family screening to be heterozygous for factor V Leiden. She has never had a thrombotic event. Using the threshold model, what is the single most important counseling point?

Answer

That her absolute risk remains low, and that the mutation only matters in combination with a trigger. The practical advice is about the additive triggers she can control or anticipate — chiefly estrogen-containing contraception, pregnancy and the postpartum period, surgery and immobilization — not about lifelong anticoagulation, which is not indicated. The lecture’s framing: 5% of the population carries it and most never clot.

Q: Which inherited thrombophilia carries the highest relative risk of a first venous thromboembolic event, and roughly how large is it?

Answer

Antithrombin deficiency — quoted on the risk model slide as approximately 10-fold, and on the prevalence table as 16-fold. It is also the rarest (0.02–0.2% of the general population), which is the general pattern: the rarer the defect, the stronger its effect.

LO 2 — Initial treatment and prognosis

Treating the clot

Initial treatment of proximal deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism

There are two structurally different ways to start anticoagulation, and the difference is whether the patient needs a parenteral drug at all. Knowing which of the four direct oral anticoagulants needs a heparin lead-in is a classic examinable point.

Two-drug versus one-drug approaches

2️⃣

Two-drug approach — a parenteral lead-in is required

Warfarin route (bridging). Start unfractionated heparin (intravenous, monitored with the partial thromboplastin time) or low molecular weight heparin (subcutaneous) overlapping with warfarin for at least 5 days AND until the international normalized ratio is above 2 for 1–2 days. Warfarin is started on day 1; the target range is 2–3.

Dabigatran or edoxaban route (switching). The registration trials used 5–10 days of initial low molecular weight heparin and only then switched to the oral drug. So these two are not monotherapy agents.

1️⃣

One-drug approach — oral from the first dose

Apixaban and rivaroxaban only. No heparin lead-in and no monitoring required. Both use a loading phase followed by a maintenance dose:

  • Rivaroxaban 15 mg twice daily for 3 weeks, then 20 mg daily.
  • Apixaban 10 mg twice daily for 1 week, then 5 mg twice daily.

Apixaban is the choice in renal failure — the one direct oral anticoagulant that remains usable when creatinine clearance is low.

🔑 The memory hook that makes this stick

“The two -xabans that start with A and R go it alone.” Apixaban and Rivaroxaban are monotherapy with a loading dose. Dabigatran and Edoxaban need Days of heparin first. Note the trap: dabigatran and edoxaban are otherwise unrelated drugs — dabigatran is a direct thrombin inhibitor, edoxaban a factor Xa inhibitor — but they share the lead-in requirement because of how their trials were designed.

The 2021 CHEST (American College of Chest Physicians) guideline position

The guideline recommendation is unambiguous: in patients with venous thromboembolism — deep vein thrombosis of the leg or pulmonary embolism — apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban or rivaroxaban is recommended over a vitamin K antagonist (warfarin). The six supporting statements are worth knowing individually because they explain the reasoning:

  1. Efficacy is equal.Direct oral anticoagulants equal warfarin at preventing recurrent clot. The advantage is not efficacy.
  2. The four agents are similar to one another.Based on indirect comparisons — there are no adequate head-to-head trials.
  3. Bleeding is lower, especially intracranial bleeding.This is the real advantage, and intracranial hemorrhage is where it matters most.
  4. Apixaban may have the lowest bleeding risk of the four.Again from indirect comparison, so held loosely.
  5. Lack of a specific reversal agent does not make a major bleed more likely to be fatal.An important counterargument to the common objection that “you cannot reverse them.”
  6. Convenience plus less bleeding means a direct oral anticoagulant is preferred for both initial and long-term treatment in patients without cancer.Note the carve-out — cancer gets its own section.

Prognosis after stopping anticoagulation

The Prandoni cohort (Haematologica, 2007) followed 1,626 patients after anticoagulation was discontinued and produced the two numbers that anchor every duration discussion:

10% per year

early recurrence rate

The cumulative recurrence curve is steepest in the first 1–2 years after stopping. This is the window in which patients relapse.

3% per year

late recurrence rate

Beyond roughly 7–8 years the curve flattens to about 3% per year — but it never becomes zero. The risk is lifelong, just lower.

Idiopathic ≫ provoked

the split that drives everything

The second Prandoni curve separates the two: cumulative recurrence exceeds 50% at ten years for idiopathic events versus roughly 20% for provoked ones. This single graph is the justification for treating unprovoked clots indefinitely.

3 months = 6 months

the duration finding

Prognosis after 3 months of anticoagulation is equivalent to prognosis after 6 months. Extra months of finite therapy buy nothing; only indefinite therapy changes the trajectory.

🧠 Extended anticoagulation is extremely effective — and that is not the whole question

Continuing anticoagulation reduces recurrence by more than 90%. There is no doubt it works. The entire debate about duration is therefore not about efficacy — it is about whether that benefit is worth the bleeding risk and the burden of taking a drug forever. That trade-off is why this decision is explicitly shared with the patient.

Q: A patient is started on dabigatran on the day of pulmonary embolism diagnosis, with no parenteral agent. What is wrong?

Answer

Dabigatran requires a low molecular weight heparin lead-in of 5–10 days before switching. It was never studied as a day-one monotherapy agent. The same is true of edoxaban. Only apixaban and rivaroxaban can be started orally from the first dose, and each uses a higher loading dose first.

Q: A 300+ lb patient with a new proximal deep vein thrombosis and a history of gastric bypass surgery. Which anticoagulant strategy is appropriate?

Answer

Low molecular weight heparin bridging to warfarin. This patient hits two separate contraindications to a direct oral anticoagulant: extreme of weight (above 300 lb), where the fixed dose is unvalidated, and altered gastrointestinal anatomy, which makes absorption of apixaban and rivaroxaban unreliable. Warfarin is monitorable, and monitoring is precisely what an unpredictable patient needs.

Q: Why does the 2021 CHEST guideline prefer direct oral anticoagulants over warfarin, if efficacy is equal?

Answer

Because bleeding — particularly intracranial bleeding — is lower, and because of convenience (no monitoring, no dietary interaction, no dose titration). The guideline explicitly says efficacy is equivalent, so the preference rests entirely on safety and burden. It also notes that despite the lack of reversal agents for some, a major bleed is no more likely to be fatal than on warfarin.

⚠️ Andexanet alfa (Andexxa) — narrow, expensive, protocol-driven

Recombinant inactivated factor Xa, used as a decoy to soak up factor Xa inhibitors. The hospital pathway shown in lecture requires approval by the coagulation pharmacist or emergency department pharmacist, and the patient must meet all of the entry criteria:

  1. Severe or life-threatening bleeding
  2. Confirmed receipt of apixaban or rivaroxaban in the previous 18 hours

From there, the pathway asks whether the bleed is into a fixed space or critical area (intracranial, intraspinal, retroperitoneal, intra-articular, pericardial, or intramuscular with compartment syndrome) and whether the hemorrhage is “survivable” — if it is not survivable, andexanet is not recommended, since there is nothing to save.

Andexanet is explicitly NOT indicated for: reversal of edoxaban, enoxaparin, fondaparinux, dabigatran or warfarin; receipt of apixaban or rivaroxaban more than 18 hours prior; or surgical reversal. For overt bleeding without a fixed-space location, the pathway routes to supportive care first, then to andexanet (if the dose was within 12 hours) or four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (if within 18 hours).

Q: A patient on apixaban presents with a gastrointestinal bleed. Their last dose was 22 hours ago. Should andexanet alfa be given?

Answer

No. Two reasons, either of which is sufficient. First, the protocol requires confirmed receipt within the previous 18 hours, and 22 hours is outside that window. Second, with an 8-hour half-life, a dose taken more than 18 hours ago is already at minimal concentration — there is little drug left to reverse. Manage with supportive care and transfusion, and hold the drug.

Q: A 68-year-old with active colon cancer, hemoglobin 9.8 g/dL and creatinine clearance 45 mL/min is being considered for extended anticoagulation. Estimate their VTE-BLEED score and interpret it.

Answer

Active cancer 2 + anemia 1.5 + creatinine clearance 30–60 1.5 + age over 60 1.5 = 6.5 points. Far above the 2-point cut-off, placing them in the 13% bleeding risk band. This does not automatically forbid anticoagulation — cancer-associated venous thromboembolism usually requires it — but it means the risk-benefit conversation is genuinely close, and the agent, dose and duration all need care.

LO 4 — Inferior vena cava filters

Treating the clot

Inferior vena cava filters — one solid indication, and a long list of harms

This section is a cautionary tale about a device that spread far beyond its evidence. Dr. Winters co-authored a quality improvement study on filter retrieval, and his practical instruction is blunt: once a filter is placed, it should almost always come out — unless the patient is going to hospice or similar.

Indications, in descending order of confidence

Widely accepted

the one real indication

Acute pulmonary embolism or proximal deep vein thrombosis WITH an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation. (Kearon et al., American College of Chest Physicians guidelines, 9th edition.) This is the indication to know.

Possible

weaker ground

Failure of anticoagulation with acute proximal venous thrombosis · a compromised pulmonary vascular bed (massive pulmonary embolism, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension) · proximal deep vein thrombosis in poor cardiopulmonary reserve.

Prophylaxis

most contested

Trauma (Smoot, Journal of Trauma, 2010) and bariatric surgery (Birkmeyer, Journal of Hospital Medicine, 2013). Prophylactic placement is where most of the over-use occurred.

Complications

🩸

Procedural

Vascular access: pneumothorax, hemorrhage, vessel injury.

Insertion: filter misplacement, excessive tilt, vascular injury, and insertion site thrombosis in 2%.

📅

Long-term (Angel et al., Journal of Vascular and Interventional Radiology, 2011)

  • Inferior vena cava penetration — 20% (by far the commonest)
  • Deep vein thrombosis — 5.4%
  • Cava occlusion — 2.8%
  • Filter migration — 1.3%

Note the irony: the device placed to prevent clot itself causes deep vein thrombosis in 5.4%.

⚠️ The FDA alert, August 2010

Since 2005 the Food and Drug Administration had received 921 adverse event reports involving inferior vena cava filters: 328 device migrations · 146 embolizations of device components · 70 inferior vena cava perforations · 56 filter fractures.

The agency stated it was “concerned that these retrievable filters, intended for short-term placement, are not always removed once a patient’s risk for pulmonary embolism subsides,” and that events “may be due to filters remaining beyond time when risk of pulmonary embolism has subsided.” In other words: the harm is a function of dwell time, and dwell time is a failure of follow-up, not of the device.

🩺 Whose job is retrieval? Yours.

The FDA recommendation names not just the implanting interventionalist but explicitly “primary care physicians and all clinicians responsible for the ongoing care of patients with retrievable inferior vena cava filters” — they should consider removing the filter as soon as protection from pulmonary embolism is no longer needed. Dr. Winters’ own paper (A multidisciplinary quality improvement program increases the inferior vena cava filter retrieval rate, Journal of Vascular Medicine, 2017) exists because retrieval rates were poor and needed a system, not just good intentions.

Q: A patient with an acute proximal deep vein thrombosis has just had an intracranial hemorrhage and cannot be anticoagulated. What is the appropriate intervention, and what must be planned at the same time?

Answer

Place a retrievable inferior vena cava filter — this is the one widely accepted indication: acute proximal deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation. At the same time, plan and document the retrieval. The filter must come out as soon as anticoagulation becomes safe, because essentially all of the long-term harm — 20% caval penetration, 5.4% deep vein thrombosis, migration, fracture — accrues with dwell time.

Q: What is the most frequent long-term complication of an inferior vena cava filter?

Answer

Inferior vena cava penetration, at 20% — an order of magnitude commoner than the more dramatic complications people remember (migration 1.3%, cava occlusion 2.8%). Deep vein thrombosis at 5.4% is the second most common and the most conceptually important, because the device causes the very thing it was placed to prevent.

LO 5 — Inherited thrombophilias

The thrombophilias

Inherited thrombophilias — common and weak, or rare and strong

Five inherited defects matter. They divide cleanly into two common but weak ones (factor V Leiden, the prothrombin gene mutation) and three rare but strong ones (protein C, protein S, antithrombin deficiency). All five raise the risk of a first clot. None of them meaningfully raises the risk of recurrence.

Figure 2 — The inherited thrombophilias mapped onto the clotting cascade. Green arrows drive clot formation; red dashed arrows are the brakes. Four of the five defects release a brake; the prothrombin gene mutation presses the accelerator.

Factor V Leiden — the one you will meet

🔑 Factor V Leiden by the numbers
  • 5% of the population carries it — it is genuinely common.
  • 5% lifetime risk of a venous thromboembolic event; 20% in thrombophilic families (where other risk is also concentrated).
  • A meta-analysis of 11,239 venous thromboembolism cases against 21,521 controls found the risk raised in both genotypes: heterozygous odds ratio 4.2, homozygous odds ratio 11.5 (95% confidence interval 6.8–19.3).
  • It does NOT increase the risk of recurrence after an initial unprovoked event. This bolded line appeared on the slide itself.

🔑 The single highest-yield discrimination in this lecture

Every inherited thrombophilia causes venous clot only. Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome is the only one on the list that also causes arterial clot. Dr. Winters said it directly: “antiphospholipid antibody syndrome is always the exception.” If a question gives you an arterial event — stroke, myocardial infarction, digital ischemia — in a young patient and asks which thrombophilia to consider, the answer is antiphospholipid syndrome, and it is essentially never factor V Leiden.

“Don’t draw these tests when the patients have just had a VTE or are on anticoagulants.”

The homocysteine story — a whole line of investigation that was abandoned

🧠 How a risk factor died
  1. Epidemiology, roughly 30 years agoObservational studies showed that mild hyperhomocysteinemia was prothrombotic and atherothrombotic.
  2. Intervention trialsMultiple randomized vitamin B trials successfully lowered homocysteine — and did not reduce thrombosis. Lowering the marker did not lower the disease.
  3. Re-analysisMore recent work (Ospino-Romero, Journal of Epidemiology, 2018) concluded the association with vascular disease was likely due to unmeasured confounders all along.
  4. The conclusion, stated twice on the slide and twice in lectureNever measure homocysteine levels. Never assess for MTHFR polymorphisms.

This is a clean worked example of a marker that was associated with disease but not causal — and the randomized trial is what exposed the difference.

Q: A patient with a new deep vein thrombosis is admitted, started on a heparin drip and then warfarin. On hospital day 3 the team sends a full thrombophilia panel. Protein C, protein S and antithrombin all return low. How should this be interpreted?

Answer

As uninterpretable — almost certainly all three are false positives. Every one of those results is explained by the clinical context rather than by an inherited defect: acute thrombosis lowers all three; heparin lowers antithrombin; and warfarin lowers protein C and protein S, since both are vitamin K-dependent. The rule is explicit: do not draw these tests in the acute setting or on anticoagulants. If testing is genuinely indicated, it must wait until the patient is off anticoagulation and remote from the event.

Q: A 33-year-old man presents with a stroke. A thrombophilia panel is proposed. Which of the classic inherited thrombophilias could plausibly explain an arterial event?

Answer

None of them. Factor V Leiden, prothrombin gene mutation, and protein C, protein S and antithrombin deficiency cause venous thrombosis only. The one condition on the thrombophilia list that causes both arterial and venous events is antiphospholipid antibody syndrome — which is exactly why it remains the standing exception to the “don’t test” advice, and why arterial thrombosis is otherwise on the do not test list.

Q: Should a patient with recurrent venous thromboembolism have a homocysteine level and MTHFR genotype checked?

Answer

No — never, in either case. The association was observational; randomized B-vitamin trials lowered homocysteine without reducing thrombosis, and the original association is now attributed to unmeasured confounding. The instruction from lecture was categorical: “never measure homocysteine levels and never measure MTHFR polymorphisms.”

LO 6 — Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome

The thrombophilias

Antiphospholipid antibody syndrome — the exception to every rule in this lecture

Antiphospholipid syndrome breaks the pattern four separate times. It is acquired, not inherited. It causes arterial as well as venous thrombosis. It genuinely does predict recurrence, so it changes duration. And it is the one setting where a direct oral anticoagulant has been shown to cause harm. Every “do not test / do not extend / prefer a direct oral anticoagulant” statement elsewhere in this file has an implicit “except in antiphospholipid syndrome” attached to it.

Diagnosis: the updated Sapporo criteria (2006)

Diagnosis requires at least one clinical criterion and at least one laboratory criterion — antibodies alone are not the syndrome.

🩺

Clinical criteria

  • Vascular thrombosis — arterial or venous
  • Pregnancy morbidity — recurrent loss, late fetal death, severe pre-eclampsia or placental insufficiency
  • Immune thrombocytopenia and livedo reticularis
🧪

Laboratory criteria

  • Lupus anticoagulant
  • Anticardiolipin antibody (immunoglobulin G or M) at high titre, above 40
  • Anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I antibodies (immunoglobulin G and M)
  • The abnormality must be persistent — present on at least 2 occasions more than 12 weeks apart.

(Miyakis, Journal of Thrombosis and Haemostasis, 2006.)

⚠️ Why the 12-week repeat matters

Antiphospholipid antibodies appear transiently after infections, with certain drugs, and in the setting of acute illness. A single positive result is common and usually means nothing. Persistence over more than 12 weeks is what separates a meaningless transient antibody from a syndrome that will change a patient’s anticoagulation for life. Never make the diagnosis on one draw.

The lupus anticoagulant paradox

🧠 A test that says “bleeds” on a patient who clots

The lupus anticoagulant results from immunoglobulins that bind phospholipids and plasma proteins — beta-2-glycoprotein I and prothrombin — in vitro, and so prolong clotting times. Hence the paradox:

  • In vitro, the lupus anticoagulant PROLONGS clotting tests — the partial thromboplastin time, the kaolin clotting time, the dilute Russell viper venom time, and silica-based tests. It looks like an anticoagulant, which is where the misleading name comes from.
  • In vivo, the lupus anticoagulant INDUCES clot.

The prolonged test is a laboratory artefact of the antibody interfering with the phospholipid surface the assay depends on. The patient is not anticoagulated; the patient is at high thrombotic risk.

The mixing study — how you tell a factor deficiency from an inhibitor

When a patient has an unexplained prolonged partial thromboplastin time, the mixing study is the next step. Mix the patient’s plasma 1:1 with normal plasma and re-run the test. Normal plasma supplies roughly 50% of all factors, which is far more than the ~30% needed to clot.

Patient 68 seconds + normal 30 seconds → mix 32 seconds.

The patient was missing something; the normal plasma supplied it and the time normalized. The problem is a deficiency.

🚫

Does NOT correct → inhibitor

Patient 68 seconds + normal 30 seconds → mix 68 seconds.

Adding normal factors changed nothing, because something in the patient’s plasma is actively interfering. That something is an inhibitor — in this context, the lupus anticoagulant.

⚠️ Direct oral anticoagulants in antiphospholipid syndrome — the trial that had to be stopped

Pengo et al., Blood, 2018. A randomized non-inferiority trial of rivaroxaban 20 mg once daily versus warfarin in high-risk “triple positive” patients — positive for lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I simultaneously.

The trial was terminated prematurely because of an excess of events in the rivaroxaban arm:

  • Rivaroxaban: 19% events — 12% thromboembolic, 7% major bleeding.
  • Warfarin: 3% events — 0 thromboembolic events, 3% major bleeding.

Rivaroxaban was worse on both sides of the ledger — more clots and more bleeding. This is why antiphospholipid syndrome appears on the “do not use a direct oral anticoagulant” list, and it is a favourite examination point precisely because it inverts the general 2021 CHEST preference.

Q: A 29-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus has an unprovoked pulmonary embolism. Her partial thromboplastin time is prolonged at 62 seconds. A 1:1 mix with normal plasma gives 60 seconds. What does this tell you, and what should she be anticoagulated with?

Answer

The mixing study does not correct, which indicates an inhibitor rather than a factor deficiency — in this context the lupus anticoagulant. She should be treated with warfarin, target international normalized ratio 2–3, and not a direct oral anticoagulant, because of the Pengo trial showing excess thromboembolic events and bleeding with rivaroxaban in high-risk antiphospholipid syndrome. Confirm the antibody is persistent by repeating it more than 12 weeks later before committing to the lifelong diagnosis. If confirmed, this is one of the few settings where the thrombophilia result genuinely dictates long-term anticoagulation.

Q: Why is the lupus anticoagulant so badly named?

Answer

Because it is neither reliably associated with lupus nor an anticoagulant in the patient. It is named for its in vitro behaviour: it binds phospholipid and thereby prolongs phospholipid-dependent clotting assays (partial thromboplastin time, dilute Russell viper venom time, kaolin clotting time). In vivo it is prothrombotic. The prolonged test is an artefact; the clinical risk runs in the opposite direction.

Q: What makes a patient with antiphospholipid antibodies “triple positive,” and why does it matter?

Answer

Simultaneous positivity for lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibody and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I antibody. It matters because triple-positive patients are the highest-risk group — they were the population of the Pengo trial, in which rivaroxaban produced 12% thromboembolic events versus 0% on warfarin. Triple positivity is the strongest signal that this patient must be on warfarin.

🧠 Does thrombophilia predict recurrence? Two studies, one answer
  • Lijfering, Circulation, 2010: heterozygosity for factor V Leiden or the prothrombin gene mutation does not substantially increase recurrence risk — and, strikingly, neither do homozygotes for factor V Leiden, nor compound heterozygotes carrying both defects.   • Christiansen et al., JAMA 2005;293(19):2353–2361: in 474 patients, the hazard ratio for recurrence with thrombophilia was 1.3 (95% confidence interval 0.8–2.0) — the confidence interval crosses 1, so the result is not statistically significant. The two cumulative incidence curves separate slightly and then converge.
  • The one thrombophilia that does raise recurrence risk: antiphospholipid antibody syndrome.

Q: A 72-year-old develops a deep vein thrombosis two weeks after a total knee replacement. The surgical team asks whether a thrombophilia workup is indicated. What do you advise, and on what basis?

Answer

No workup. This is a major transient risk factor (recent surgery and immobilization) in an elderly patient with a post-operative event — a scenario that appears on the “NO” list twice over. Choosing Wisely recommendation 2 states it directly: don’t test for thrombophilia in adults with venous thromboembolism occurring in the setting of major transient risk factors. Anticoagulate for 3 months and stop; a positive result would not change that plan, and the panel costs $1,067.

Q: Name three clinical settings in which thrombophilia testing IS appropriate.

Answer

(1) Idiopathic venous thromboembolism at age under 50 with a positive family history; (2) cerebral vein thrombosis; (3) portal or mesenteric vein thrombosis — where you should additionally test for JAK2 and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, since an occult myeloproliferative neoplasm is a classic cause of splanchnic vein thrombosis. Testing is also reasonable for venous thromboembolism associated with oral contraceptives, hormone replacement therapy or pregnancy.

Q: Christiansen et al. reported a hazard ratio of 1.3 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.8–2.0 for recurrence in patients with thrombophilia. Interpret this.

Answer

The point estimate suggests a 30% higher recurrence rate, but the confidence interval includes 1.0, so the result is not statistically significant — the data are compatible with anything from a 20% reduction to a doubling. It cannot support using a thrombophilia result to decide duration of anticoagulation. Combined with Lijfering’s finding that even homozygotes and compound heterozygotes show no increase, the conclusion is that inherited thrombophilia is not a determinant of recurrence.

How long to treat

Duration of anticoagulation and risk stratification

Anticoagulation splits into two conceptually different phases, and the whole duration question is really about the boundary between them.

🧠 The two phases
  1. Initial management — the first 5–21 days after diagnosisHigher-intensity or loading dosing; the acutely dangerous window.
  2. Primary treatment — 3 to 6 monthsTreating the clot that already exists. It ends at a decision point.
  3. Secondary prevention — planned indefinite durationNo longer treating this clot; now preventing the next one. This is a different goal with a different justification.

At the end of primary treatment there are exactly two choices: (1) stop anticoagulation, or (2) continue for secondary prevention. Framing it this way makes clear that continuing is not “more of the same treatment” — it is a new intervention with its own risk-benefit calculation.

Low-intensity extended anticoagulation — two landmark trials

🧠 AMPLIFY-EXTEND (Agnelli et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2013)

Design: randomized, double-blind. Two doses of apixaban — 2.5 mg and 5 mg twice daily — versus placebo, in patients who had already completed 6 to 12 months of anticoagulation and in whom there was genuine clinical equipoise about continuing. Study drug for 12 months.

Result (symptomatic recurrent venous thromboembolism or venous thromboembolism-related death): the placebo curve climbs steadily to roughly 8–9% at 12 months, while both apixaban arms stay under about 2% — and the two doses are essentially superimposed on each other.

Bleeding: major or clinically relevant non-major bleeding reached about 4–5% with apixaban 5 mg, but the 2.5 mg curve tracked placebo almost exactly (~3%).

The conclusion that made this trial matter: the half dose gave the full benefit at essentially no excess bleeding. That is what created “low-intensity extended anticoagulation” as a real third option.

🧠 EINSTEIN-CHOICE (Weitz et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2017)

Design: randomized, double-blind, phase 3. 3,396 patients who had completed 6 to 12 months of anticoagulation and were in equipoise, randomized to rivaroxaban 20 mg daily, rivaroxaban 10 mg daily, or aspirin 100 mg daily, for up to 12 months.

Result: the aspirin curve for fatal or non-fatal venous thromboembolism rises to roughly 4.5%, while both rivaroxaban doses stay at or below about 1.5% — with the 10 mg dose performing at least as well as 20 mg. Major bleeding was low and closely comparable across all three arms (well under 1%).

The message: for extended prevention, aspirin is markedly inferior to a low-dose direct oral anticoagulant, and it is not meaningfully safer. Aspirin remains an option only where anticoagulation is genuinely unacceptable.

Q: A 44-year-old has completed 6 months of anticoagulation after a first unprovoked pulmonary embolism. She wants ongoing protection but is worried about bleeding. What does the evidence support?

Answer

Low-intensity extended anticoagulation. AMPLIFY-EXTEND showed apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily gave the same reduction in recurrence as the 5 mg dose (both under ~2% versus ~8–9% on placebo) while its bleeding curve was indistinguishable from placebo. EINSTEIN-CHOICE similarly showed rivaroxaban 10 mg daily was at least as good as 20 mg, and both were far superior to aspirin. So the honest answer to her concern is not “stop” and not “switch to aspirin” — it is continue at a reduced dose.

Q: Why do both the 2021 CHEST and 2020 American Society of Hematology guidelines use the word “suggest” rather than “recommend” for extended therapy after a first unprovoked event?

Answer

Because the trade-off is genuinely close. Annual fatal recurrence is 0.3–1% and annual fatal bleeding is 0.2–0.6% — overlapping ranges. The CHEST recommendation is explicitly Grade 2B, a weak recommendation on moderate-quality evidence. When the mortality arithmetic does not clearly favour either arm, the deciding input becomes bleeding risk and patient preference, which is why this is framed as a shared decision.

Q: Is aspirin an adequate substitute for anticoagulation in extended secondary prevention of venous thromboembolism?

Answer

No. In EINSTEIN-CHOICE, recurrent fatal or non-fatal venous thromboembolism reached about 4.5% on aspirin 100 mg versus ≤1.5% on rivaroxaban at either 10 mg or 20 mg — roughly a three-fold difference — while major bleeding was similarly low in all three arms. Aspirin is therefore much less effective and not meaningfully safer. It is a fallback only when anticoagulation is unacceptable to the patient or contraindicated.

The evidence trail, in order

  1. CLOT trial — low molecular weight heparin beats warfarinMulticentre, open-label, randomized.   2. Hokusai VTE-Cancer — edoxaban (Raskob et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2018)Low molecular weight heparin for at least 5 days followed by edoxaban, versus low molecular weight heparin. Composite of recurrent venous thromboembolism and major bleeding: 12.8% edoxaban versus 13.5% low molecular weight heparin — equivalent overall. But the components diverged: major bleeding 6.3% with edoxaban versus 3.2% with dalteparin, and 17 of those bleeds (3.2%) were upper gastrointestinal, mostly in patients with upper gastrointestinal cancers. No grade VI or ‘serious’ bleeds in either group.
  3. CARAVAGGIO — apixaban1,170 patients with active cancer and venous thromboembolism, apixaban versus dalteparin. Recurrent venous thromboembolism 5.6% with apixaban versus 7.9% with dalteparin; major bleeding 3.8% versus 4%. Similar efficacy, no bleeding penalty.
  4. CASTA-DIVA / rivaroxaban (CHEST, 2022)Active cancer with venous thromboembolism randomized to rivaroxaban or dalteparin for 3 months. Of 158 patients (74 rivaroxaban, 84 dalteparin), recurrent venous thromboembolism occurred in 4 versus 6 patients — cumulative incidence 6.4% versus 10.1%. Major bleeding in 1 versus 3 patients — 1.4% versus 3.7% (subhazard ratio 0.36; 95% confidence interval 0.04–3.43). Not enough events to reach statistical significance, but similar outcomes.
🔑 Where this leaves practice
  • Low molecular weight heparin followed by edoxaban is an alternative to low molecular weight heparin — but avoid in upper gastrointestinal tumours and avoid in anyone at high bleeding risk.
  • Standard-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban appears to have improved efficacy and safety compared with dalteparin.
  • Apixaban or rivaroxaban is now widely used in cancer — and this was Dr. Winters’ own stated practice in the closing summary.
  • Note that the 2021 CHEST duration table still says “low molecular weight heparin for at least 3 months” for cancer-associated venous thromboembolism. The guideline table and current practice have partially diverged; know both.

The other cancer question: should you go hunting for an occult malignancy?

🧠 SOME trial (Carrier et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2015)

Multicentre, open-label, randomized trial of occult-cancer screening after unprovoked venous thromboembolism in 854 patients.

  • Group 1 — limited screening: basic blood tests, chest radiography, and screening for breast, cervical and prostate cancer.
  • Group 2 — limited screening plus comprehensive computed tomography.
  • Primary outcome: cancer missed by the screening strategy but detected by the end of 1 year.

Results: 3.9% overall had a new diagnosis of occult cancer within a year — 3.2% in the limited-screening group versus 4.5% in the limited-plus-computed-tomography group (p = 0.28). 4 occult cancers (29%) were missed by limited screening and 5 (26%) by limited screening plus computed tomography (p = 1.0). No significant difference in mean time to cancer diagnosis or in cancer-related mortality (1.4% versus 0.9%, p = 0.75).

Conclusion, in the slide’s own bolded words: “Age-appropriate cancer screening” and “Pursue symptoms suggestive of malignancy.” Adding a whole-body computed tomography scan to an unprovoked venous thromboembolism workup finds nothing extra and does not save lives.

Q: A 61-year-old with newly diagnosed gastric adenocarcinoma develops a pulmonary embolism. Which anticoagulant would you avoid, and why?

Answer

Avoid edoxaban. In the Hokusai VTE-Cancer trial, major bleeding was 6.3% with edoxaban versus 3.2% with dalteparin, and the excess was specifically upper gastrointestinal bleeding concentrated in patients with upper gastrointestinal cancers — exactly this patient. Reasonable choices are apixaban or rivaroxaban (both showed similar or better efficacy than dalteparin without a bleeding penalty) or low molecular weight heparin, which remains the guideline-table answer for cancer.

Q: A 58-year-old has a first unprovoked deep vein thrombosis. His family asks for a “full body scan” to look for hidden cancer. What is the evidence-based response?

Answer

Decline the comprehensive computed tomography and do age-appropriate cancer screening plus a careful history and examination, pursuing any symptom suggestive of malignancy. The SOME trial randomized 854 such patients and found no significant difference in occult cancers detected (3.2% versus 4.5%, p = 0.28), in cancers missed (29% versus 26%, p = 1.0), or in cancer-related mortality (1.4% versus 0.9%, p = 0.75). The extra imaging adds cost, radiation and incidental findings without benefit.

Spontaneous

Risk factors: young athletes with “overuse” (the Paget-Schroetter picture), thoracic outlet obstruction, and thrombophilia.

Treatment: anticoagulate initially; rarely long term. The justification is the risk of pulmonary embolism, which is much lower than for proximal leg deep vein thrombosis — 15% versus 50%. Dr. Winters: “I never really anticoagulate people long term after an upper extremity deep vein thrombosis.”

🔌

Catheter-associated

Risks: the type of catheter, driven by the lumen-to-vessel diameter ratio — peripherally inserted central catheter > central line > port — and catheter malposition.

Treatment: it is acceptable to leave the catheter in if it is correctly positioned and still needed. Anticoagulate for 3 months after catheter removal.

(Winters et al., MITH Study, Journal of Thrombosis and Haemostasis, 2015.)

Long-term complications of venous thromboembolism

⚠️ Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension
  • Risk factors: following submassive or massive pulmonary embolism, and right ventricular strain at initial presentation.
  • Presenting symptoms: dyspnea, occasionally edema. Often insidious.
  • Workup: brain natriuretic peptide, echocardiogram, and a ventilation-perfusion scan — note that the ventilation-perfusion scan, not computed tomography angiography, is the screening test of choice here.
  • Outcome if untreated: cor pulmonale → death.
  • Management: indefinite anticoagulation; pulmonary artery thrombotic endarterectomy (potentially curative); double lung transplant for refractory patients.
🩺 Post-thrombotic syndrome — and the trial that overturned compression stockings
  • Epidemiology: 40% of patients after venous thromboembolism. This is extremely common and under-appreciated.
  • Symptoms, waxing and waning: edema, pruritus in the affected limb, pain, erythema.
  • Diagnosis on Doppler ultrasound: a non-dilated, non-occlusive thrombus adherent to the vessel wall — the appearance of old, organized clot rather than fresh thrombus.
  • Prevention: catheter-directed thrombolysis for iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis.
  • Compression stockings do NOT reduce the risk of venous thromboembolism, and — the important negative — the SOX trial (Lancet, 2014) randomized 410 patients to stockings or no stockings after venous thromboembolism and found 14% post-thrombotic syndrome with stockings versus 13% without. They do not prevent it either.
  • They remain the cornerstone of managing active post-thrombotic syndrome — the question becomes simply what is more comfortable for this patient, compression or no compression?

Q: A patient on chemotherapy develops an upper extremity deep vein thrombosis around a correctly positioned peripherally inserted central catheter that is still needed. Must the line come out?

Answer

No. It is acceptable to leave the catheter in place if it is correctly positioned and there is an ongoing need for it. Anticoagulate while the line remains, and continue for 3 months after the catheter is eventually removed. Note also that the peripherally inserted central catheter carries the highest thrombotic risk of the catheter types, because of its lumen-to-vessel diameter ratio — the order is peripherally inserted central catheter > central line > port.

Q: Six months after a large pulmonary embolism, a patient reports progressive exertional dyspnea. What diagnosis must be excluded, and how?

Answer

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension. Risk is highest after submassive or massive pulmonary embolism and where there was right ventricular strain at presentation. Work up with brain natriuretic peptide, echocardiography and a ventilation-perfusion scan. If confirmed, management is indefinite anticoagulation, referral for pulmonary artery thrombotic endarterectomy, and double lung transplantation for refractory disease. Untreated it progresses to cor pulmonale and death.

Q: Should compression stockings be prescribed after a deep vein thrombosis to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome?

Answer

Not for prevention. The SOX trial (Lancet, 2014) randomized 410 patients and found post-thrombotic syndrome in 14% with stockings versus 13% without — no benefit. Stockings also do not reduce the risk of venous thromboembolism itself. They remain the cornerstone of treating established, symptomatic post-thrombotic syndrome, where the decision reduces to patient comfort. The genuine preventive intervention is catheter-directed thrombolysis for iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis.

Glossary and abbreviations

Every abbreviation and technical term used anywhere in this file, spelled out and defined. Grouped by theme rather than alphabetically, so related ideas sit together.

The clot itself
VTE — venous thromboembolism
The umbrella term covering both deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. They are treated as one disease because the clot is the same and it moves.
DVT — deep vein thrombosis
Clot in a deep vein, most often the leg. “Proximal” means at or above the popliteal vein and carries much higher embolic risk than distal (calf) clot.
PE — pulmonary embolism
Clot that has travelled to the pulmonary arteries. “Massive” implies hemodynamic instability; “submassive” implies right ventricular strain without shock.
Provoked / transient
A clot with an identifiable, temporary cause — surgery, trauma, immobilization, acute illness, estrogen, pregnancy, a central line. The risk resolves, so treatment can stop at 3 months.
Unprovoked / idiopathic
No identifiable provoking factor. The two words are used interchangeably in this lecture. Carries roughly double the long-term recurrence risk of a provoked event.
Phlegmasia cerulea dolens
Massive iliofemoral thrombosis obstructing essentially all venous outflow, producing a swollen, blue, intensely painful limb. A vascular emergency.
Splanchnic vein thrombosis
Clot in the portal, mesenteric or splenic veins. One of the few settings that genuinely warrants a workup — including JAK2 and paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria.
Cerebral vein thrombosis
Clot in a dural venous sinus or cerebral vein. Also on the “do test” list.
The coagulation system
Coagulation cascade
The sequence of proteolytic activations ending in fibrin. Split traditionally into intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converging on factor X.
Thrombin (factor IIa)
The central enzyme of the cascade; converts fibrinogen to fibrin. The target of dabigatran and of antithrombin.
Prothrombin (factor II)
The inactive precursor of thrombin. The prothrombin gene mutation raises its circulating level.
Factor Xa
The activated factor at the convergence point of both pathways. The target of apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban and (indirectly, via antithrombin) heparin.
Protein C
A vitamin K-dependent natural anticoagulant. Once activated, it inactivates factors Va and VIIIa. Deficiency raises first-event risk about 5-fold.
Protein S
The cofactor for activated protein C, also vitamin K-dependent. Deficiency likewise raises risk about 5-fold.
Activated protein C (APC)
The active form that cleaves factors Va and VIIIa. “Activated protein C resistance” is the functional consequence of factor V Leiden.
Antithrombin (formerly antithrombin III, AT3)
A natural inhibitor of thrombin and factor Xa, and the protein heparin works through. Deficiency is the rarest and strongest inherited thrombophilia (16-fold). Also causes apparent heparin resistance.
Fibrinogen → fibrin
The final step: soluble fibrinogen is cleaved by thrombin into fibrin, which cross-links into the clot.
The thrombophilias
Thrombophilia
An inherited or acquired tendency to thrombosis. Inherited ones raise the risk of a first event; with the exception of antiphospholipid syndrome, they do not raise the risk of recurrence.
FVL — factor V Leiden
A point mutation making factor Va resistant to cleavage by activated protein C. Present in 5% of the population; heterozygous odds ratio 4.2, homozygous 11.5.
PTG — prothrombin gene mutation
The G20210A variant, raising prothrombin levels. Prevalence 2%; relative risk 3–4 fold. The only gain-of-function inherited thrombophilia.
Compound heterozygote
Carrying one copy each of two different defects — for example factor V Leiden plus the prothrombin gene mutation. Notably, even this does not raise recurrence risk (Lijfering, 2010).
APLS / APS — antiphospholipid antibody syndrome
An acquired autoimmune thrombophilia requiring one clinical plus one persistent laboratory criterion. Causes both arterial and venous thrombosis, genuinely raises recurrence risk, and must be treated with warfarin rather than a direct oral anticoagulant.
LA — lupus anticoagulant
An antibody that prolongs phospholipid-dependent clotting tests in vitro while causing thrombosis in vivo. Misleadingly named on both counts.
ACA — anticardiolipin antibody
One of the three antiphospholipid antibodies. Counts toward diagnosis at high titre (above 40), immunoglobulin G or M.
Anti-β2GPI — anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I antibody
The third antiphospholipid antibody. Immunoglobulin G and M.
Triple positive
Positive for lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I simultaneously. The highest-risk group, and the population of the Pengo rivaroxaban trial.
Mixing study
Mixing patient plasma 1:1 with normal plasma. If the prolonged clotting time corrects, the problem is a factor deficiency; if it does not correct, an inhibitor such as the lupus anticoagulant is present.
MTHFR — methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase
An enzyme in homocysteine metabolism. Its polymorphisms were formerly tested for. The instruction now is never to test it, and never to measure homocysteine.
PNH — paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria
An acquired clonal disorder of blood cells with a striking thrombotic tendency, classically causing splanchnic vein thrombosis.
JAK2
Janus kinase 2; its V617F mutation drives most myeloproliferative neoplasms, another cause of splanchnic vein thrombosis. Tested for in portal or mesenteric vein thrombosis.
The drugs
Anticoagulant
A drug that inhibits clot formation. Distinct from an antiplatelet (aspirin) and from a thrombolytic (which dissolves existing clot).
UFH — unfractionated heparin
Intravenous, monitored with the partial thromboplastin time, short-acting and immediately reversible. Preferred where the situation may change fast — massive clot, possible thrombolysis, pregnancy, renal failure.
LMWH — low molecular weight heparin
Subcutaneous heparin fragments (enoxaparin, dalteparin). No routine monitoring. The historical standard for cancer-associated thrombosis.
VKA — vitamin K antagonist (warfarin, Coumadin)
Blocks the vitamin K-dependent factors II, VII, IX and X, plus proteins C and S. Requires international normalized ratio monitoring; target 2–3 for venous thromboembolism.
DOAC / NOAC — direct oral anticoagulant
Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran. Two names for one class. Preferred over warfarin in the 2021 CHEST guideline for most patients.
Apixaban
Factor Xa inhibitor. 10 mg twice daily for 1 week, then 5 mg twice daily. No heparin lead-in. The direct oral anticoagulant of choice in renal impairment; possibly the lowest bleeding risk of the four.
Rivaroxaban
Factor Xa inhibitor. 15 mg twice daily for 3 weeks, then 20 mg daily. No heparin lead-in. Harmful in high-risk antiphospholipid syndrome.
Edoxaban
Factor Xa inhibitor. Requires 5–10 days of low molecular weight heparin first. Avoid in upper gastrointestinal cancers.
Dabigatran
Direct thrombin inhibitor. Also requires a heparin lead-in. Cleared renally; allow over 36 hours for washout if creatinine clearance is below 30.
Bridging versus switching
Bridging means overlapping heparin with warfarin until the international normalized ratio is therapeutic. Switching means finishing a heparin course and then starting dabigatran or edoxaban.
Andexanet alfa (Andexxa)
Recombinant inactivated factor Xa used as a decoy to reverse apixaban and rivaroxaban in severe bleeding within 18 hours of the dose. Not for edoxaban, dabigatran, warfarin, enoxaparin, fondaparinux, or surgical reversal.
4F-PCC — four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate
A plasma-derived concentrate of factors II, VII, IX and X. Used for warfarin reversal, and in the andexanet pathway as an alternative.
Thrombolytic therapy
Drugs that actively dissolve clot. Considered in massive or submassive pulmonary embolism and in iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (catheter-directed).
Tests and measures
INR — international normalized ratio
A standardized prothrombin time, allowing warfarin dosing to be compared across laboratories. Target 2–3 for venous thromboembolism and for antiphospholipid syndrome.
PTT / aPTT — (activated) partial thromboplastin time
Screening test of the intrinsic pathway. Used to monitor unfractionated heparin, and prolonged by the lupus anticoagulant.
dRVVT — dilute Russell viper venom time
A phospholipid-dependent clotting test especially sensitive to the lupus anticoagulant.
D-dimer
A fibrin degradation product. A persistently raised level after stopping anticoagulation predicts recurrence, and is one input into the extension decision.
CrCl — creatinine clearance
An estimate of kidney filtration rate. Below 30 mL/min, direct oral anticoagulant choice narrows to apixaban or warfarin; 30–60 mL/min scores 1.5 on VTE-BLEED.
Functional (activity) assay
Measures how well a protein works rather than how much is present. Used for protein C, protein S and antithrombin — and therefore distorted by acute clot, heparin or warfarin.
Genetic test
Detects the DNA variant directly. Used for factor V Leiden and the prothrombin gene mutation, which is why these two alone are unaffected by timing.
VTE-BLEED
A bleeding risk score: active cancer 2, anemia 1.5, prior bleeding 1.5, creatinine clearance 30–60 1.5, age over 60 1.5, uncontrolled hypertension in a male 1. At or above 2 points, bleeding risk is 13% versus 2.9% below.
V/Q scan — ventilation-perfusion scan
A nuclear medicine study comparing airflow and blood flow in the lung. The preferred screening test for chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension.
BNP — brain natriuretic peptide
A hormone released by stretched cardiac ventricles. Used with echocardiography when chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension is suspected.
Evidence, guidelines and statistics
ACCP — American College of Chest Physicians
Publisher of the CHEST anticoagulation guidelines. Editions referenced here: 2008, 2012, 2016 and 2021 (11th edition).
ASH — American Society of Hematology
Publisher of the 2020 venous thromboembolism guidelines, which suggest indefinite therapy after unprovoked events.
Choosing Wisely
An initiative of the American Board of Internal Medicine Foundation listing low-value practices. Two of its hematology items appear in this lecture: don’t test for thrombophilia after a major transient risk factor, and don’t anticoagulate beyond 3 months for a first provoked event.
Grade 2B
In the GRADE system, a weak recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence — signalling that reasonable clinicians and patients may choose differently.
Equipoise
Genuine uncertainty about which arm of a trial is better. Both AMPLIFY-EXTEND and EINSTEIN-CHOICE explicitly enrolled only patients in equipoise about continuing.
Non-inferiority trial
A trial designed to show a new treatment is not unacceptably worse than the standard. The Pengo antiphospholipid trial was one — and it failed, in the direction of harm.
OR / RR / HR / SHR
Odds ratio / relative risk / hazard ratio / subhazard ratio — measures of association. A subhazard ratio accounts for competing risks such as death from cancer.
CI — confidence interval
The range compatible with the data. If a 95% confidence interval for a ratio includes 1.0, the result is not statistically significant — as in Christiansen’s hazard ratio of 1.3 (0.8–2.0).
Case fatality rate
The proportion of people who have an event and die from it. Recurrent venous thromboembolism: 4–12%.
Composite outcome
A single endpoint combining several event types — for example recurrent clot plus major bleeding in Hokusai VTE-Cancer. Useful, but the components can move in opposite directions.
Named trials in this lecture
Prandoni, Haematologica, 2007
1,626 patients followed after stopping anticoagulation. Recurrence 10% per year early, 3% per year late; idiopathic events recur far more than provoked.
Lijfering, Circulation, 2010
Heterozygotes, homozygotes and compound heterozygotes for factor V Leiden and the prothrombin gene mutation show no substantial increase in recurrence risk.
Christiansen, JAMA, 2005
474 patients; hazard ratio for recurrence with thrombophilia 1.3 (95% confidence interval 0.8–2.0) — not significant.
Pengo, Blood, 2018
Rivaroxaban versus warfarin in triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome. Terminated early: 19% events on rivaroxaban (12% thrombotic, 7% bleeding) versus 3% on warfarin (0% thrombotic).
CLOT trial
Dalteparin versus a vitamin K antagonist in cancer-associated thrombosis. Recurrence ~10% versus ~17% (p = 0.002).
Hokusai VTE-Cancer (Raskob, NEJM, 2018)
Edoxaban after heparin versus low molecular weight heparin in cancer. Composite 12.8% versus 13.5%; major bleeding 6.3% versus 3.2%, concentrated in upper gastrointestinal cancers.
CARAVAGGIO
Apixaban versus dalteparin in 1,170 cancer patients. Recurrence 5.6% versus 7.9%; major bleeding 3.8% versus 4%.
SOME trial (Carrier, NEJM, 2015)
Occult cancer screening after unprovoked venous thromboembolism, 854 patients. Adding computed tomography found no more cancers and did not reduce mortality.
AMPLIFY-EXTEND (Agnelli, NEJM, 2013)
Apixaban 2.5 mg and 5 mg twice daily versus placebo for extended prevention. Both doses cut recurrence from ~8–9% to under 2%; the 2.5 mg dose bled like placebo.
EINSTEIN-CHOICE (Weitz, NEJM, 2017)
3,396 patients; rivaroxaban 20 mg or 10 mg versus aspirin 100 mg. Both rivaroxaban doses far superior to aspirin, with comparably low major bleeding.
SOX trial (Lancet, 2014)
410 patients randomized to compression stockings or not after venous thromboembolism. Post-thrombotic syndrome 14% versus 13% — no benefit.
MITH study (Winters, JTH, 2015)
Central venous catheters and upper extremity deep vein thrombosis in medical inpatients — the source of the catheter risk hierarchy.

🎯 Hypercoagulable States — High-Yield One-Pager

LO 1–2 · RISK & INITIAL TREATMENT

Threshold model

  • Intrinsic risk (inherited + acquired) + triggers − prophylaxis → cross the thrombosis threshold → clot. No single cause.
  • Transient/provoked: surgery, trauma, acute medical illness, immobilization, estrogen/HRT, pregnancy/postpartum, central venous catheter → 3 months.
  • Persistent: obesity, nephrotic syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, myeloproliferative neoplasm/paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, cancer, paralysis, thrombophilia → drifts to indefinite.
  • Relative risk of first event: FVL 2–3× · PTG 3× · protein C 5× · protein S 5× · antithrombin 10–16×.

Starting anticoagulation

  • Apixaban 10 mg BID ×1 week → 5 mg BID. No lead-in. Best in renal failure.
  • Rivaroxaban 15 mg BID ×3 weeks → 20 mg daily. No lead-in.
  • Dabigatran and edoxaban need 5–10 days of LMWH FIRST.
  • Warfarin route: heparin overlap ≥5 days AND until INR >2 for 1–2 days; target INR 2–3.
  • CHEST 2021: all four DOACs preferred over warfarin. Efficacy equal; less bleeding, especially intracranial; apixaban possibly lowest.

DO NOT use a DOAC

  • Pregnancy/breastfeeding · massive clot burden (massive PE, phlegmasia cerulea dolens, iliofemoral DVT)
  • >300 lb or <110 lb · gastric bypass (apixaban/rivaroxaban absorption)
  • CrCl <30 (use apixaban or warfarin) · heparin-induced thrombocytopenia with thrombosis · splanchnic vein thrombosis
  • Recurrent VTE on therapeutic anticoagulation · ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME · HIV drugs, phenytoin, carbamazepine

Prognosis after stopping

  • Prandoni 2007, n=1,626: 10%/yr early, 3%/yr late; steepest in first 1–2 years.
  • Idiopathic >50% at 10 yr vs provoked ~20%.
  • 3 months = 6 months. Extended therapy cuts recurrence >90%, at the price of bleeding.

Bleeding & reversal

  • VTE-BLEED: cancer 2 · anemia 1.5 · prior bleed 1.5 · CrCl 30–60 1.5 · age >60 1.5 · uncontrolled hypertension (male) 1. ≥2 = 13% · 0–1.5 = 2.9%.
  • Half-lives: rivaroxaban 5–9 h · apixaban 8 h (dose >18 h ago ≈ no effect) · dabigatran >24 h, >36 h if CrCl <30.
  • Andexanet: only apixaban/rivaroxaban, only within 18 h, only severe/life-threatening survivable bleed. NOT for edoxaban, dabigatran, warfarin, enoxaparin, fondaparinux, or surgery.

IVC filters

  • One accepted indication: acute proximal DVT/PE + ABSOLUTE contraindication to anticoagulation.
  • Long-term harm: IVC penetration 20% · DVT 5.4% · occlusion 2.8% · migration 1.3%; insertion-site thrombosis 2%.
  • FDA 2010: 921 events. Always plan retrieval — it is the primary care physician’s job too.
LO 5–7 · THE THROMBOPHILIAS

THE central paradox

  • Inherited thrombophilias raise FIRST-event risk but NOT recurrence risk. Printed under the prevalence table: “does not increase risk of recurrence after initial unprovoked event.”
  • Lijfering 2010: not even homozygous FVL or FVL+PTG compound heterozygotes recur more.
  • Christiansen JAMA 2005 (n=474): HR 1.3 (0.8–2.0) — crosses 1, not significant.
  • CHEST 2008 said thrombophilia is not a major determinant of recurrence; 2012, 2016, 2021 don’t discuss it at all.

Prevalence and risk

  • FVL: 5% general / 12–18% of VTE / 3–4×. Het OR 4.2, homozygous OR 11.5.
  • PTG: 2% / 5–8% / 3–4×.
  • Protein C: 0.2–0.5% / 2–5% / 5×. Protein S: unknown / <1% / 5×.
  • Antithrombin: 0.02–0.2% / <1% / 16×. Rarer = stronger.

Arterial vs venous — one row matters

  • FVL, PTG, protein C, protein S, antithrombin: VENOUS ONLY.
  • Antiphospholipid syndrome: ARTERIAL AND VENOUS. “Always the exception.”

Antiphospholipid syndrome

  • Sapporo 2006: ≥1 clinical (thrombosis · pregnancy morbidity · immune thrombocytopenia + livedo reticularis) AND ≥1 lab (lupus anticoagulant · anticardiolipin IgG/IgM >40 · anti-β2-glycoprotein I), persistent on 2 occasions >12 weeks apart.
  • Paradox: in vitro PROLONGS clotting tests; in vivo CLOTS.
  • Mixing study: corrects = factor deficiency; does not correct = inhibitor.
  • 5% of VTE patients · 20–30% recurrence in year 1 → long-term anticoagulation.
  • Warfarin INR 2–3 is adequate (2 randomized trials).
  • Pengo 2018: rivaroxaban vs warfarin in triple positive — stopped early. 19% events (12% thrombotic, 7% bleed) vs 3% (0% thrombotic).

Acquired deficiency — never test at the wrong time

  • Acute thrombosis lowers protein C, protein S AND antithrombin.
  • Heparin lowers antithrombin. Warfarin lowers protein C and protein S (vitamin K-dependent).
  • Also: pregnancy/estrogen, liver disease, DIC, nephrotic syndrome, major surgery, inflammation.
  • “Don’t draw these tests when the patient has just had a VTE or is on anticoagulants.”
  • Only FVL and PTG are genetic → unaffected by timing.

Homocysteine — dead end

  • Epidemiology said prothrombotic; vitamin B trials lowered homocysteine but not thrombosis; now attributed to unmeasured confounding.
  • NEVER measure homocysteine. NEVER test MTHFR.

Who to test — cost $1,067

  • YES: idiopathic VTE age <50 with positive family history · cerebral vein thrombosis · portal/mesenteric vein thrombosis (add JAK2, PNH).
  • REASONABLE: VTE with OCP / HRT / pregnancy.
  • NO: age >50 first idiopathic · cancer · elderly post-op · retinal vein thrombosis · arterial thrombosis · asymptomatic · women starting an OCP with no history.
  • Exception in every case: antiphospholipid antibody testing.
  • Choosing Wisely #2: don’t test after a major transient risk factor.
LO 8–10 · DURATION, CANCER, SPECIAL

Duration — CHEST 2021

  • Provoked proximal DVT/PE → 3 months.
  • Idiopathic proximal DVT/PE → “at least 3 months”, then STOP or LONG-TERM.
  • Second unprovoked → long-term.
  • Active cancer → LMWH ≥3 months.
  • Choosing Wisely #6: don’t exceed 3 months for a first provoked VTE.

Phases & the extension decision

  • Initial 5–21 days → primary treatment 3–6 months → decision point → secondary prevention (indefinite).
  • CHEST 2021: first unprovoked + low/moderate bleeding risk → extended therapy, no stop date (Grade 2B — weak). ASH 2020 similarly suggests indefinite.
  • Recurrence 5–8%/yr, case fatality 4–12% → 0.3–1% fatal/yr. Bleeding 2–6%/yr → 0.2–0.6% fatal/yr. Ranges overlap — hence shared decision.
  • Consider: recurrence risk (unprovoked, D-dimer) · bleeding risk · patient preference · alternatives.

Low-intensity extended therapy

  • AMPLIFY-EXTEND (2013): apixaban 2.5 or 5 mg BID vs placebo after 6–12 months. Placebo ~8–9% recurrence vs <2% both doses; 2.5 mg bled like placebo.
  • EINSTEIN-CHOICE (2017), n=3,396: rivaroxaban 20 or 10 mg vs aspirin 100 mg. Aspirin ~4.5% vs ≤1.5%; major bleeding low in all three.
  • Aspirin is markedly inferior and not safer.

VTE in cancer

  • 20% of all VTE; more bleeding and more recurrence.
  • CLOT: dalteparin ~10% vs vitamin K antagonist ~17% recurrence (p=0.002).
  • Hokusai (edoxaban): composite 12.8% vs 13.5%; major bleeding 6.3% vs 3.2%, upper GI, in upper GI cancers → AVOID there.
  • CARAVAGGIO (apixaban, n=1,170): VTE 5.6% vs 7.9%; bleeding 3.8% vs 4%.
  • Rivaroxaban (CHEST 2022): 6.4% vs 10.1% VTE; 1.4% vs 3.7% bleeding — underpowered but similar.
  • Apixaban or rivaroxaban is now widely used in cancer.

Occult cancer screening

  • SOME trial (n=854): adding CT found no more cancers (3.2% vs 4.5%, p=0.28), missed the same proportion (29% vs 26%), no mortality difference (1.4% vs 0.9%).
  • Age-appropriate screening. Pursue symptoms suggestive of malignancy. Nothing more.

Special populations

  • Renal failure: UFH + warfarin; apixaban probably OK. Obesity: LMWH/warfarin. APLS: LMWH/heparin → warfarin.
  • Large clot burden: admit, UFH, consider thrombolysis. Isolated distal DVT: anticoagulate or serial ultrasound; never an indication for indefinite therapy.
  • Pregnancy: UFH; LMWH while breastfeeding.

Upper extremity DVT & sequelae

  • Spontaneous: young athletes (overuse), thoracic outlet obstruction, thrombophilia. PE risk 15% vs 50% for proximal leg DVT → anticoagulate initially, rarely long term.
  • Catheter-associated: risk by lumen-to-vessel ratio PICC > central line > port. Leave the line in if positioned and needed; anticoagulate 3 months post removal.
  • CTEPH: after submassive/massive PE, right ventricular strain. Dyspnea → BNP, echo, V/Q scan. Indefinite anticoagulation, pulmonary thromboendarterectomy, lung transplant.
  • Post-thrombotic syndrome: 40% after VTE. SOX trial: stockings 14% vs no stockings 13% — no prevention benefit; still the cornerstone of treating established disease. Prevention = catheter-directed thrombolysis for iliofemoral DVT.
