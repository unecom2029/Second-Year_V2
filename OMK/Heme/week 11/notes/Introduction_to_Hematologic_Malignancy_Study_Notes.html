Introduction to Hematologic Malignancy

An “above the trees” tour of the blood cancers. The organizing idea is a single one: a cell somewhere along the path from pluripotent stem cell to fully differentiated cell acquires a mutation — and where on that path it happens determines which disease you get, and how fast it will kill.

Orientation — what this lecture is and is not

Dr. Winters was explicit about scope: “This is a huge topic to cover in an hour... it’s meant to be kind of an above-the-trees look at hematologic malignancies.” He said twice that the treatment detail is not the examinable part — “You don’t have to remember the drugs” and “there’s no test questions on what therapy you get for this stuff.”

🔑 What to actually learn from this lecture
  1. The organizing construct.Differentiation stage at the moment of transformation determines the disease and its tempo. Less differentiated → aggressive. More differentiated → indolent. This one idea makes the whole taxonomy predictable rather than a list to memorize.
  2. Which lineage: myeloid or lymphoid.Every disease in this lecture sits in one of these two columns. Get the column right and you have halved the differential.
  3. The named translocations and their diseases.These are the highest-density examination facts in the deck — and they are genuinely diagnostic, not trivia.
  4. The presentations and the emergencies.Blast crisis, disseminated intravascular coagulation in acute promyelocytic leukemia, tumour lysis syndrome, hyperviscosity. These are the things that kill people in the first week.
  5. The discriminators between look-alike diseases.Chronic lymphocytic leukemia versus small lymphocytic lymphoma; myelodysplastic syndrome versus acute myeloid leukemia; monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance versus smoldering versus active myeloma.
🧠 The analogy Dr. Winters used

He described the pluripotent stem cell becoming progressively more specialized, and then said: “I’m a highly specialized cell. I can never go back and become a surgeon. You guys are all pluripotent stem cells — you can become anything.” The point of the joke is the biology: differentiation is a one-way street, and a cancer arising early on that street inherits the proliferative capacity of an undifferentiated cell. That is exactly why acute leukemias are aggressive and plasma cell disorders are chronic.

He added the darkest corollary: in the very least differentiated cancers you can get an acute leukemia with both myeloid and lymphoid features — and “that person’s gonna die.” The earlier on the path, the worse the prognosis.

Topic 1 — The basics

The organizing idea

The core construct — differentiation stage determines the disease

🔑 The principal construct, as stated on the slide

A hematopoietic cell in the bone marrow or lymph system acquires a mutation, or a series of mutations, that disrupts the differentiation and maturation of its progeny.

The resulting disease depends on the maturation stage of the cell that undergoes transformation:

  • Less differentiated → AGGRESSIVE
  • More differentiated → INDOLENT

Note the phrase “of its progeny.” The mutated cell itself is not usually the problem — the problem is that everything descended from it is frozen at a stage where it should not be, and keeps dividing.

Figure 1 — The map of hematologic malignancy. Two lineages across, differentiation stage along each. Read left to right and the tempo of the disease changes from weeks (acute leukemia) to years or decades (myeloma, follicular lymphoma).

🧠 Why myeloma is at the far right

The plasma cell is the fully differentiated end-point of the B-cell lineage — an antibody factory and nothing else. A cancer of that cell keeps doing what plasma cells do: it makes a single antibody in enormous quantity (the monoclonal protein). It does not blast the marrow the way an acute leukemia does. That is why multiple myeloma is a chronic disease measured in years, and why its complications — kidney failure from light chains, bone lesions, hypercalcemia — come from the product of the cell rather than from its proliferation.

Q: Two patients transform at different points in the B-cell lineage. One develops Burkitt lymphoma, the other multiple myeloma. Using only the core construct, predict the difference in tempo and explain why.

Answer

Burkitt lymphoma is highly aggressive; multiple myeloma is chronic. Burkitt arises from a less differentiated B cell, which retains enormous proliferative capacity — it is one of the fastest-growing human tumours. Myeloma arises from the fully differentiated plasma cell, whose job is secretion rather than division, so the disease is measured in years and its damage comes from the monoclonal protein and light chains rather than from raw growth.

Q: Why does an acute leukemia with both myeloid and lymphoid features carry such a poor prognosis?

Answer

Because mixed lineage implies transformation at a point before the myeloid/lymphoid branch point — the least differentiated cell of all. By the core construct, the earlier on the differentiation path, the more aggressive the disease. Dr. Winters put it bluntly: that person is going to die.

Topic 2 — Acute leukemia

Myeloid and lymphoid — acute

Acute leukemias — the presentation, and putting out the fire

🩺 The clinical vignette from the lecture

A 57-year-old previously healthy woman with a history of hypertension presents to the emergency room with 3 weeks of progressive malaise and spontaneous bruising.

Vitals: temperature 37.5 °C · blood pressure 123/75 · pulse 110 · respirations 20 · oxygen saturation 98% on room air.
Examination: pallor, tachycardia, scattered ecchymoses.
Labs: white blood cells 87,000 · hemoglobin 6.2 · hematocrit 18 · platelets 45,000.
Differential: neutrophils 0.6 — and 90% immature blasts.

Every element points one way. The white count is enormous but she is functionally neutropenic — the cells are all blasts, which do nothing. She is anemic, thrombocytopenic and neutropenic simultaneously: trilineage marrow failure with a packed marrow, which is the signature of acute leukemia.

Defining features

  • Characterized by unregulated growth of undifferentiated cells in the marrow called BLASTS.
  • Cells rapidly overwhelm the marrow and spill out into the peripheral blood.
  • Diagnosis requires more than 20% blasts in the bone marrow OR peripheral blood. Memorize this number — it is also the line that separates myelodysplastic syndrome and chronic myeloid leukemia blast phase from acute leukemia.
  • May have a high, normal, or LOW white blood cell count at presentation. A normal white count does not exclude acute leukemia.
  • Universally present with bone marrow failure — anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia.
  • Rapidly fatal: untreated, death within 12 weeks in most cases.
⚠️ “Extinguish the fire” — the initial presentation

The stated goal: stabilize the patient and diagnose the leukemia subtype. Two threads run in parallel.

  1. Treat the marrow failure — transfusion supportTransfuse for hemoglobin below 7, platelets below 10,000 — or below 50,000 if actively bleeding. Identify and treat infection, which in a neutropenic patient is the thing most likely to kill them this week.
  2. Buy time — control the unchecked white cell growthTreatment: hydroxyurea. This is cytoreduction, not cure — it holds the count down while the diagnostic workup proceeds and definitive therapy is arranged.

The two metabolic emergencies

🩸

Disseminated intravascular coagulation

Uncontrolled intravascular consumption of blood clotting factors.

  • Low fibrinogen
  • High partial thromboplastin time and prothrombin time
  • Low platelets

Accompanied by thrombotic microangiopathy — hemolysis with schistocytes on the peripheral blood smear. Most strongly associated with acute promyelocytic leukemia, where it is the cause of early death.

💥

Tumour lysis syndrome

Results from rapid cell turnover and death, releasing intracellular contents into the blood. Four derangements, each with its own consequence and treatment:

  • Hyperuricemia → renal failure. Prevention: allopurinol. Severe: rasburicase.
  • Hyperphosphatemia
  • Hypocalcemia — may need calcium replacement
  • Hyperkalemia → arrhythmia if severe. Treatment: sodium polystyrene.

Watch for it in acute leukemias and aggressive lymphomas — particularly Burkitt — either spontaneously or on starting treatment.

🔑 The tumour lysis pattern in one line

Everything goes UP except calcium. Potassium up, phosphate up, uric acid up, calcium down — because the released phosphate binds and precipitates the calcium. If a question lists three highs and one low in a patient starting chemotherapy, this is the diagnosis.

Q: A patient presents with a white cell count of 87,000, of which 90% are blasts, plus anemia and thrombocytopenia. Absolute neutrophil count is 0.6. Why is this patient at high risk of infection despite a white count of 87,000?

Answer

Because blasts are non-functional. The white count measures cells, not function — the patient is functionally neutropenic with an absolute neutrophil count of 0.6 despite a hugely elevated total. Identifying and treating infection is one of the two immediate priorities, alongside transfusion support.

Q: What is the blast percentage threshold that defines acute leukemia, and in which compartments does it apply?

Answer

More than 20% blasts, in the bone marrow OR the peripheral blood. Either compartment suffices. This same 20% line defines blast phase of chronic myeloid leukemia and separates myelodysplastic syndrome from acute myeloid leukemia.

Q: Two days after starting induction chemotherapy, a patient has potassium 6.4, phosphate 7.1, calcium 6.8 and uric acid 14. Name the syndrome and give the treatment for each abnormality.

Answer

Tumour lysis syndrome. Hyperuricemia → allopurinol for prevention, rasburicase if severe (it degrades existing urate rather than merely blocking production); this is the abnormality that causes renal failure. Hyperkalemia → sodium polystyrene, with urgent attention because of arrhythmia risk. Hyperphosphatemia drives the hypocalcemia, which may need calcium replacement.

Topic 3 — Acute myeloid leukemia

Myeloid — acute

Acute myeloid leukemia and its one curable variant

Diagnosis, risk factors and prognosis

Diagnosis

what confirms it

Bone marrow biopsy and aspirate showing more than 20% blasts, with myeloid cell surface antigens on flow cytometry. Flow cytometry is what assigns lineage — the blast morphology alone will not.

Risk factors

who gets it

Prior cytotoxic chemotherapy · a history of myelodysplastic syndrome or a myeloproliferative disorder · Down syndrome. Note that two of the three are iatrogenic or antecedent hematologic disease — this is often a second cancer.

Prognostic features

what determines outcome

Age · performance status · comorbidities · prior cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiation · antecedent myeloid disorders · cytogenetic and molecular findings. The last of these stratifies patients into high, intermediate and low risk, and that stratification decides who gets a transplant.

Treatment — two intents, decided by fitness rather than by age alone

🎯

Curative intent — younger / fit

Induction: “7 + 3” — daunorubicin or idarubicin plus cytarabine. Requires at least a 3–4 week hospitalization. The goal is complete remission.

Consolidation: more intensive chemotherapy. Allogeneic stem cell transplant for intermediate or high risk disease.

🤝

Palliative intent — older / unfit

Hypomethylating agent — decitabine or azacitidine — with or without venetoclax.

Supportive care: transfusions, antibiotics, hospice.

The choice between columns turns on whether the patient can survive a month of marrow aplasia in hospital, not simply on a birthday.

⚠️ Acute promyelocytic leukemia — the one you must not miss

A biologically and genetically distinct variant of acute myeloid leukemia, and the single highest-yield disease in this section.

  • Translocation t(15;17) → the PML-RARA fusion gene.
  • Severe disseminated intravascular coagulation and coagulopathy causes early death from bleeding in untreated or unrecognized cases. The patient does not die of the leukemia — they die of hemorrhage, often within days, often before the diagnosis is confirmed.
  • Treat with all-trans retinoic acid — which does not kill the cells but forces the arrested promyelocytes to differentiate, the purest possible illustration of this lecture’s core construct.
  • 90% overall survival. This transforms the most rapidly lethal leukemia into the most curable one — but only if it is recognized.

The practical rule: in a new acute leukemia with a coagulopathy, start all-trans retinoic acid on suspicion, before the cytogenetics come back. The cost of being wrong is small; the cost of waiting is a fatal bleed.

Q: A 46-year-old presents with acute leukemia, fibrinogen 80, prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time both prolonged, and platelets 22,000. What is the likely subtype, the genetic lesion, and what should be started immediately?

Answer

Acute promyelocytic leukemia. The lesion is t(15;17), producing PML-RARA. Start all-trans retinoic acid immediately, on suspicion, without waiting for confirmatory cytogenetics — because severe disseminated intravascular coagulation causes early death from bleeding in unrecognized cases. Support the coagulopathy aggressively with blood products. Recognized and treated, overall survival is 90%.

Q: How does all-trans retinoic acid work, and why is it a perfect illustration of this lecture’s central idea?

Answer

It induces the malignant promyelocytes to differentiate into mature cells rather than killing them. The core construct of the lecture is that disease arises from arrested differentiation — so a drug that releases the block cures the disease without conventional cytotoxicity. It converts the leukemia most likely to kill in the first week into one with 90% overall survival.

Q: A 78-year-old with heart failure and poor performance status is diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia. Why is “7 + 3” induction generally inappropriate, and what is offered instead?

Answer

Because “7 + 3” requires at least a 3–4 week hospitalization through a period of profound marrow aplasia, which an unfit patient with significant comorbidity is unlikely to survive. The palliative-intent pathway is a hypomethylating agent — decitabine or azacitidine — with or without venetoclax, plus supportive care with transfusions and antibiotics, and hospice where appropriate. The decision is driven by fitness, performance status and comorbidity, not by age alone.

Topic 4 — Acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Lymphoid — acute

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia — the childhood cancer with the age-dependent prognosis

Epidemiology

who gets it

The most common childhood malignancy. Bimodal peak incidence: 2–5 years, and over 50. 85% precursor B-cell; 15% T-cell.

Clinical findings

how it differs from acute myeloid leukemia

Lymphadenopathy · splenomegaly · central nervous system involvement. These three extramedullary features are what distinguish it clinically — acute lymphoblastic leukemia leaves the marrow and colonizes lymphoid tissue and the meninges, which is why treatment includes central nervous system prophylaxis.

BCR-ABL positive B-cell disease

a targetable subset

A subset carries BCR-ABL — the same fusion as chronic myeloid leukemia — and responds to oral tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Historically the worst-prognosis group, transformed by targeted therapy.

Prognosis by age

the striking gradient

Children: over 90% survival. Adolescents and young adults: 50–70%. Older adults: 30%. The same disease name, three very different diseases.

🧠 Why the age gradient is so steep

It is not simply that children tolerate chemotherapy better. The biology differs by age: adult acute lymphoblastic leukemia is enriched for adverse cytogenetics — including BCR-ABL — while childhood disease is enriched for favourable ones. Add reduced tolerance of intensive protocols in older adults and the survival gap widens from over 90% to 30%.

Q: What three clinical findings distinguish acute lymphoblastic leukemia from acute myeloid leukemia at presentation?

Answer

Lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly and central nervous system involvement. Both diseases cause marrow failure, but acute lymphoblastic leukemia characteristically involves extramedullary lymphoid sites and the meninges — which is why central nervous system directed therapy is part of treatment.

Q: A 34-year-old with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia is found to be BCR-ABL positive. What does this add to treatment?

Answer

An oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor, added to chemotherapy. BCR-ABL positive B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia responds to tyrosine kinase inhibitors — the same drug class that transformed chronic myeloid leukemia, because it is the same fusion protein. Historically this was the worst-prognosis subgroup.

Topic 5 — Myelodysplastic syndrome

Myeloid — chronic

Myelodysplastic syndrome — a factory that builds defective cells

Myelodysplastic syndrome occupies the middle box of Figure 1: not undifferentiated like acute myeloid leukemia, not normally differentiated like the myeloproliferative neoplasms, but abnormally differentiated. The marrow is busy and the blood counts are low at the same time.

🔑 The defining paradox

Dysplastic and ineffective blood cell production. The marrow is cellular — often hypercellular — yet the patient is cytopenic, because the cells being produced are structurally abnormal and die before they leave, or shortly after. Production is not reduced; it is wasted.

Clinical presentation

any or all lineages

Anemia · thrombocytopenia · neutropenia. Anemia is usually the first and the most prominent.

Pathophysiology

the mechanism named in lecture

Epigenetic changes — which is precisely why hypomethylating drugs are the disease-directed treatment.

Causes of death

two routes, and the minority is leukemia

30% progress to acute myeloid leukemia. 50% die of bleeding or infection — that is, of the cytopenias themselves. Most patients are killed by the marrow failure, not by transformation.

Treatment

three tiers

Supportive: transfusions, growth factors, antibiotics. Disease-directed: hypomethylating drugs. Curative: allogeneic stem cell transplant — the only one.

🧠 The mechanism-to-drug link worth holding onto

The pathophysiology is epigenetic — genes are silenced by methylation rather than deleted by mutation. A hypomethylating agent (azacitidine, decitabine) removes that silencing and lets normal differentiation resume. This is the same drug class used for unfit acute myeloid leukemia, which makes sense given that myelodysplastic syndrome and acute myeloid leukemia are two points on one continuum separated by the 20% blast line.

Q: A 74-year-old has hemoglobin 8.1, platelets 90,000, neutrophils 1.1, and a hypercellular marrow with dysplastic changes and 8% blasts. What is the diagnosis, and what single number would change it?

Answer

Myelodysplastic syndrome — cytopenias with a cellular, dysplastic marrow: ineffective production. The number that would change the diagnosis is the blast percentage: more than 20% would make this acute myeloid leukemia. At 8% it remains myelodysplastic syndrome, with a 30% lifetime risk of progressing to acute myeloid leukemia.

Q: What kills most patients with myelodysplastic syndrome?

Answer

Bleeding or infection — 50% — that is, complications of the cytopenias. Only 30% die from progression to acute myeloid leukemia. This is counter-intuitive: the feared outcome (leukemic transformation) is the less common one.

Topic 6 — Chronic myeloid leukemia

Myeloid — chronic

Chronic myeloid leukemia — the greatest success story in oncology

Dr. Winters said of this disease: “I’ve never had a patient die from chronic myeloid leukemia.” That statement would have been unthinkable before 2001.

🔑 Definition and the defining lesion

Unregulated growth of myeloid cells in the bone marrow and their accumulation in the blood.

t(9;22) → BCR-ABL → the Philadelphia chromosome. Know all three names for the same thing: the translocation, the fusion gene it creates, and the abnormal chromosome that results. The fusion protein is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase — permanently switched on — which is exactly why a small molecule that blocks it works so well.

Presentation

  • Often asymptomatic — frequently found on a routine blood count.
  • Neutrophilia, basophilia, eosinophilia, thrombocytosis — the whole myeloid series is elevated. Basophilia is the distinctive one; few other conditions raise basophils.
  • Splenomegaly.

The three phases

PhaseDefining features
Chronic phaseUsually asymptomatic. 85% of patients present here.
Accelerated phaseAny of: persistent or increasing high white cell count unresponsive to therapy · persistent or increasing splenomegaly · persistent thrombocytosis above 1,000 × 10⁹/L unresponsive to therapy · persistent thrombocytopenia below 100 × 10⁹/L unrelated to therapy · at least 20% basophils in the peripheral blood · 10–19% blasts · any new clonal chromosomal abnormality in Philadelphia-positive cells arising during therapy
Blast phaseMore than 20% blasts in the bone marrow or blood — i.e. it has become an acute leukemia
🧠 Two numbers, two meanings — do not confuse them

In accelerated phase, 10–19% BLASTS and at least 20% BASOPHILS are two separate criteria that both involve a number near 20. Blasts above 20% means blast phase (acute leukemia). Basophils at or above 20% means accelerated phase. Read the cell type before the number.

Treatment and the transformation of prognosis

First generation

the original

Imatinib. The drug that proved targeted therapy could work.

Second generation

more potent options

Nilotinib, dasatinib, bosutinib.

Resistance

the escape mutation

The T315I gatekeeper mutation blocks binding of the earlier agents. Treat with ponatinib or omacetaxine.

Treatment-free remission

stopping the drug

Treatment-free intervals are now achievable — selected patients in deep, sustained molecular response can discontinue therapy under monitoring.

🔑 The prognosis numbers — memorize the contrast
  • Before tyrosine kinase inhibitors: median survival 2–5 years.
  • After tyrosine kinase inhibitors: 95% alive at 10 years.

A fatal disease converted into a chronic one by a single oral tablet. This is the paradigm case for molecularly targeted therapy in all of oncology.

Q: Give the three names for the genetic lesion of chronic myeloid leukemia and explain why a tyrosine kinase inhibitor works.

Answer

t(9;22) (the translocation), BCR-ABL (the fusion gene and protein), and the Philadelphia chromosome (the resulting derivative chromosome 22). The fusion protein is a constitutively active tyrosine kinase — permanently switched on and driving proliferation. A tyrosine kinase inhibitor such as imatinib blocks its active site, which is why it works so precisely, and why survival went from a median of 2–5 years to 95% alive at 10 years.

Q: A patient on imatinib loses their response. Molecular testing shows a T315I mutation. What now?

Answer

Ponatinib or omacetaxine. T315I is the gatekeeper mutation that confers resistance to imatinib and to the second-generation agents (nilotinib, dasatinib, bosutinib). Switching within those generations will not help — it requires the specific agents active against T315I.

Q: A patient with known chronic myeloid leukemia now has 24% basophils and 14% blasts in the peripheral blood. What phase is this?

Answer

Accelerated phase. Both findings are accelerated-phase criteria: at least 20% basophils and 10–19% blasts. It is not blast phase, which requires more than 20% blasts — at 14% the patient has not crossed that line. The distinction matters because blast phase is treated as an acute leukemia.

Topic 7 — Myeloproliferative neoplasms

Myeloid — chronic

The JAK2 neoplasms — polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, myelofibrosis

Three diseases, one pathway. All are driven by JAK2/STAT pathway mutations, all produce normally differentiated cells in excess (the far-right box of Figure 1), and all can transform into one another or into acute leukemia. Their shared clinical danger is thrombosis, not marrow failure.

🧠 What links the three

The JAK2/STAT pathway normally transmits growth-factor signals into the nucleus. A JAK2 mutation leaves it signalling continuously, so the marrow behaves as though it is being permanently told to produce — but the cells it makes are normal, mature and functional. That is why these patients are not infected or bleeding from failure; they are clotting from excess. It is also why JAK2 testing appears in the thrombophilia workup for splanchnic vein thrombosis — an unexplained portal or mesenteric vein clot can be the first sign of one of these diseases.

Polycythemia vera

🌡️

Presentation

Inflammatory symptoms: night sweats · aquagenic pruritus (itching after a hot shower — highly characteristic) · erythromelalgia (burning red painful extremities) · bone pain · symptomatic splenomegaly · malaise · weight loss.

Thrombosis — arterial and venous. Risk factors: prior thrombosis · age over 60 · white cells above 11,000 · hematocrit above 45 · platelets above 400,000.

Hyperviscosity: headaches, disequilibrium.

💉

Treatment and transformation

Phlebotomy to a target hematocrit: men below 45, women below 42.

Hydroxyurea · aspirin or anticoagulation · ruxolitinib for refractory disease.

Transformation: 10% progress to post-polycythemia vera myelofibrosis; 1% develop acute myeloid leukemia.

🩺 The presentation Dr. Winters warned about

He described 35-year-olds who “never see a doctor and they present with a heart attack — and it’s because they have this disease.” A myocardial infarction or stroke in a young person with a high hematocrit or platelet count should prompt thought about a myeloproliferative neoplasm. These are among the few hematologic conditions causing arterial as well as venous thrombosis.

Essential thrombocythemia

Risk stratification

only two factors

History of thrombosis and age over 60. Simple, and worth memorizing because it is so short.

Treatment

and its actual goal

Hydroxyurea. Goal: reduce thrombosis risk — not to normalize a number for its own sake.

Transformation

low

4% lifetime risk of transformation to myelofibrosis.

Myelofibrosis

⚠️ The one with the worst prognosis of the three

Presentation — two components:

  • Symptom burden: bone pain, malaise, night sweats, weight loss, fevers. The symptom burden itself is a treatment indication.
  • Pancytopenia — as the marrow is replaced by fibrous tissue it stops producing. Unlike polycythemia vera and essential thrombocythemia, this disease ends in marrow failure.

Treatment goals: improve symptoms and improve survival.

  • Ruxolitinib — for intermediate-2 and high risk disease. Improves symptoms AND survival, but does NOT improve the cytopenias. That caveat is the examinable part.
  • Allogeneic stem cell transplant — the only curative-intent treatment.

Q: A 58-year-old reports intense itching after hot showers, burning red hands, and headaches. Hematocrit is 58%. What is the diagnosis, what pathway is involved, and what is the immediate treatment?

Answer

Polycythemia vera. Aquagenic pruritus and erythromelalgia are the two characteristic symptoms; the headaches reflect hyperviscosity. Driven by the JAK2/STAT pathway. Immediate treatment is phlebotomy to a hematocrit below 45 in men (below 42 in women), plus aspirin, with hydroxyurea if higher risk. Long term, 10% progress to post-polycythemia vera myelofibrosis and 1% to acute myeloid leukemia.

Q: What are the two risk-stratification factors in essential thrombocythemia, and what is the goal of treatment?

Answer

A history of thrombosis and age over 60. Treatment is hydroxyurea, and its goal is explicitly to reduce thrombosis risk — not to normalize the platelet count as an end in itself. Lifetime transformation risk to myelofibrosis is 4%.

Q: A patient with high-risk myelofibrosis has debilitating night sweats and pancytopenia. What does ruxolitinib offer, and what will it not do?

Answer

Ruxolitinib is indicated for intermediate-2 and high risk disease and improves both symptoms and survival. Critically, it does NOT improve the cytopenias — the pancytopenia will persist and may worsen. The only curative-intent treatment is allogeneic stem cell transplant.

Topic 8 — Aggressive B-cell lymphoma

Lymphoid — aggressive

Aggressive B-cell lymphomas — diffuse large B-cell and Burkitt

🔑 The defining trade-off of aggressive lymphoma

Often highly symptomatic on presentation. Death in 3–6 months without treatment. Treatment intent: CURE.

This is the central paradox of lymphoma and worth stating alongside its opposite (the indolent lymphomas in the next section): the aggressive diseases are the curable ones, because rapidly dividing cells are exactly what chemotherapy kills. Also: watch for tumour lysis syndrome, for the same reason.

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma

📈

Presentation and risk stratification

A rapidly growing mass. Often with “B symptoms”: night sweats, weight loss, malaise. May have central nervous system involvement.

Risk stratification:

  • Double hit lymphoma — a balanced translocation placing MYC adjacent to BCL2 or BCL6. Approximately 30% overall survival — a dramatically worse subgroup.
  • Activated B-cell type versus non-germinal centre type
  • Stage I versus disseminated
💊

Treatment

First line: R-CHOP — rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin (hydroxydaunorubicin), vincristine (Oncovin) and prednisone.

Relapsed or refractory: chemoimmunotherapy · allogeneic stem cell transplant · CAR-T cell therapy · bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTE).

Q: A 63-year-old with a rapidly enlarging neck mass, night sweats and weight loss is diagnosed with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Lactate dehydrogenase is elevated, ECOG performance status 2, Ann Arbor stage IV, with two extranodal sites. Calculate the International Prognostic Index and give the expected overall survival.

Answer

All five factors are present: age over 60 (1) + raised lactate dehydrogenase (1) + ECOG 2 or more (1) + stage III/IV (1) + more than 1 extranodal site (1) = 5. An index of 4 or 5 corresponds to approximately 50% overall survival. First-line treatment is R-CHOP with curative intent. Test for MYC with BCL2 or BCL6 — a double hit would drop expected survival to around 30%.

Q: Why are aggressive lymphomas curable while indolent lymphomas generally are not?

Answer

Because chemotherapy kills dividing cells. Aggressive lymphomas have a high proliferation fraction, so cytotoxic therapy destroys nearly the whole population — hence treatment intent is cure despite death in 3–6 months without treatment. Indolent lymphomas divide slowly, so most cells are never exposed to the drug’s mechanism; they respond, relapse, and respond again over years. The corollary is the tumour lysis risk: the same rapid turnover that makes aggressive disease curable makes it dangerous to treat.

Q: A patient with HIV presents with a rapidly growing abdominal mass. Biopsy shows a highly aggressive B-cell lymphoma with t(8;14). Diagnosis, and what complication must be anticipated?

Answer

Burkitt lymphoma — highly aggressive, associated with immune deficiency (HIV, allogeneic transplant), and carrying t(8;14). Anticipate tumour lysis syndrome, which can occur spontaneously or on starting treatment: hyperuricemia, hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia. Prevent with allopurinol and hydration; use rasburicase if severe. Survival with chemoimmunotherapy is 60–70% in adults, 90% in children.

Topic 9 — Indolent B-cell lymphoma

Lymphoid — indolent

Indolent B-cell lymphomas — chronic lymphocytic leukemia, follicular, marginal zone, lymphoplasmacytic

The mirror image of the previous section. These diseases are not curable with standard therapy but are compatible with decades of life — and a large fraction of patients never need treatment at all. Dr. Winters on follicular lymphoma: “About a third of patients never get treated.”

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia and small lymphocytic lymphoma

🔑 One disease, two names, decided by where you look

A mature B-cell neoplasm:

  • In the bone marrow and blood → chronic lymphocytic leukemia.
  • In the lymph nodes and spleen → small lymphocytic lymphoma.
  • A patient may have either or both. It is the same disease regardless of presentation.
🔬

Presentation and diagnosis

Often asymptomatic. Lymphadenopathy. Lymphocytosis found on routine blood work — this is how most cases are discovered.

Diagnosis: lymph node biopsy and flow cytometry. The immunophenotype is the answer to know: CD5+, CD10−, CD20+, CD23+.

📊

Risk stratification

Fluorescence in situ hybridization:

  • Favorable: 13q deletion
  • Intermediate: trisomy 12, or negative
  • High risk: 17p deletion, 11q deletion

IgVH mutation status: mutated = favorable; unmutated = unfavorable (counter-intuitive — here mutation is the good result).

Polymerase chain reaction: TP53 = unfavorable.

🧠 The CD5-positive B cell is the diagnostic oddity

CD5 is normally a T-cell marker. A B-cell neoplasm (CD20+) that also expresses CD5 is distinctly unusual, and only two important diseases do it: chronic lymphocytic leukemia / small lymphocytic lymphoma and mantle cell lymphoma. The discriminator between them is CD23: positive in chronic lymphocytic leukemia, negative in mantle cell — and mantle cell carries t(11;14) with cyclin D1 overexpression.

When to treat, and with what

Indications for treatment — note that a rising lymphocyte count on its own is not one of them:

  • Pancytopenia
  • Bulky lymphadenopathy
  • Severe symptoms — malaise, weight loss, night sweats
  • Threatened end organ function

Ibrutinib · acalabrutinib · pirtobrutinib.

Venetoclax.

Use a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor or a BCL-2 inhibitor — this group responds poorly to chemoimmunotherapy.

Richter transformation

the feared event

2–9% transform to large cell lymphoma. Very aggressive. Usually fatal.

⚠️ The paraneoplastic syndromes of chronic lymphocytic leukemia

A disease of dysregulated B cells produces problems of antibody function, quite separately from tumour bulk:

  • Autoimmune hemolytic anemia
  • Immune thrombocytopenia
  • Hypogammaglobulinemia → recurrent or persistent sinopulmonary infections → treat with intravenous immunoglobulin

Note the trap: a falling hemoglobin in chronic lymphocytic leukemia may be autoimmune hemolysis rather than marrow infiltration — a completely different treatment.

Follicular lymphoma

🕰️

Presentation

Usually asymptomatic lymphadenopathy. Usually advanced at diagnosis (Ann Arbor stage III/IV) — and in indolent lymphoma advanced stage does not carry the urgency it does in aggressive disease.

Often months, years — or decades — from diagnosis to first treatment.

Indications for treatment: B symptoms · cytopenias · end organ dysfunction.

💊

Treatment and course

Chemoimmunotherapy · PI3K inhibitor · lenalidomide with rituximab. Allogeneic stem cell transplant is the only curative therapy.

Median progression-free survival after initial chemoimmunotherapy: 5–8 years.

High grade follicular lymphoma is more aggressive and is treated like diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

Transformation to large cell lymphoma: 30% over 10 years.

🧠 The lymphoma you can sometimes treat with an antibiotic

Lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (Waldenström macroglobulinemia)

⚠️ An indolent lymphoma whose complications come from a protein

Presentation: plasmacytic differentiation · MYD88 mutation · advanced diffuse lymphadenopathy with or without splenomegaly · cytopenias · an IgM monoclonal protein.

Every major complication follows from the fact that the paraprotein is IgM — a large pentameric molecule:

  • Hyperviscosity syndrome — vertigo, blurred vision, headache, nystagmus, ataxia; severe: stroke, coma, delirium. Treatment: EMERGENT PLASMAPHERESIS.
  • Neuropathy — classically a slowly progressive, ascending sensorimotor neuropathy.
  • Cold agglutinin autoimmune hemolytic anemia.
  • Cryoglobulinemia — Raynaud phenomenon, urticaria, purpura, acral cyanosis, tissue necrosis.
  • Bleeding and coagulopathy — often with hyperviscosity. Oozing from the nose, gums and gastrointestinal tract, because the IgM paraprotein interferes with fibrin polymerization. A patient who both clots and bleeds.

Treatment: chemoimmunotherapy; plasmapheresis for hyperviscosity. Watch out for the IgM “flare” phenomenon during initial treatment — the IgM can rise transiently when therapy begins, worsening viscosity just as you start to help.

🧠 Why IgM and not IgG causes hyperviscosity

As Dr. Winters noted in passing, IgM forms a pentamer — five antibody units joined together. That makes it a very large molecule that stays in the intravascular space and raises viscosity dramatically at concentrations an IgG paraprotein would tolerate. The same bulk explains the interference with fibrin polymerization that causes bleeding, and the cold agglutinin behaviour. One molecular property explains four clinical syndromes.

Q: A 68-year-old is found on routine bloods to have a lymphocyte count of 24,000. Flow cytometry shows CD5+, CD10−, CD20+, CD23+. What is the diagnosis, and does she need treatment?

Answer

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia — the immunophenotype CD5+, CD10−, CD20+, CD23+ is diagnostic. She does not need treatment on this information alone. Treatment indications are pancytopenia, bulky lymphadenopathy, severe symptoms, or threatened end organ function — a raised lymphocyte count is not among them. Risk-stratify with fluorescence in situ hybridization (13q deletion favorable; 17p or 11q deletion high risk), IgVH status (mutated favorable) and TP53.

Q: How do you distinguish chronic lymphocytic leukemia from mantle cell lymphoma when both are CD5-positive B-cell neoplasms?

Answer

CD23. Chronic lymphocytic leukemia is CD23 positive; mantle cell lymphoma is CD23 negative and carries t(11;14) with overexpression of cyclin D1. The distinction matters enormously: chronic lymphocytic leukemia is indolent and often needs no treatment, while mantle cell is aggressive and everyone relapses.

Q: A patient with gastric MALT lymphoma is found to have Helicobacter pylori. What is the first-line treatment?

Answer

Eradicate the Helicobacter pylori. In gastric MALT lymphoma with proven infection, antibiotic eradication alone can cure the lymphoma, because the malignancy is driven by chronic antigenic stimulation. The parallel case is nodal marginal zone lymphoma with active hepatitis C, where antiviral therapy may also cure the lymphoma.

Q: A patient with an IgM monoclonal protein presents with blurred vision, vertigo, ataxia and nystagmus. What is happening and what is the immediate treatment?

Answer

Hyperviscosity syndrome from lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (Waldenström macroglobulinemia). The immediate treatment is emergent plasmapheresis to remove the pentameric IgM. Untreated it progresses to stroke, coma and delirium. Once stabilized, treat the underlying disease with chemoimmunotherapy — but watch for the IgM flare phenomenon, a transient rise in IgM at the start of therapy.

Topic 10 — Mantle cell lymphoma

Lymphoid — a category of its own

Mantle cell lymphoma — neither comfortably aggressive nor comfortably indolent

Mantle cell sits on its own in the classification for a reason: it combines the worst features of both categories. It behaves aggressively, and it is not cured.

Epidemiology

rare, male, older

Rare — about 3,300 cases per year. Twice as common in men. Median age 68.

Presentation

symptomatic and bulky

Symptomatic · heavy burden of lymphadenopathy · B symptoms · 90% gastrointestinal involvement. That last figure is striking and examinable.

Pathology

the translocation

t(11;14) → overexpression of cyclin D1. Cyclin D1 drives progression through the cell cycle, which is the mechanistic reason for the aggressive behaviour.

The unique feature

stated plainly on the slide

“Aggressive and everyone relapses.”

⬆️

High risk features

  • Symptomatic
  • 17p deletion
  • Blastoid variant
  • Elevated lactate dehydrogenase
  • High proliferation index
⬇️

Low risk features

  • Leukemic phase only — counter-intuitively, disease confined to the blood behaves better than bulky nodal disease
  • SOX11 mutation

Treatment. Aggressive approach: chemoimmunotherapy followed by autologous stem cell transplant. Less intense approach: a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor, a BCL-2 inhibitor, or chemoimmunotherapy.

🩺 The relapse pattern Dr. Winters described

He used mantle cell as his example of a phenomenon he called, half-jokingly, the “Pet Sematary” effect: the disease keeps coming back, and each time it returns it is a little harder to treat than before. That is the practical meaning of “everyone relapses.” The clinical consequence is that treatment strategy is about sequencing therapies over years rather than aiming at a single curative attempt.

Q: A 70-year-old man presents with widespread lymphadenopathy, B symptoms, and biopsy-proven lymphoma with t(11;14). What is the diagnosis, what protein is overexpressed, and what should you tell him about prognosis?

Answer

Mantle cell lymphoma, with overexpression of cyclin D1. It is twice as common in men with a median age of 68, and 90% have gastrointestinal involvement — worth looking for. The prognostic message is that it is aggressive and everyone relapses; the plan is to sequence therapies. Fit patients receive chemoimmunotherapy followed by autologous stem cell transplant; less intensive options are a Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor or venetoclax.

Q: Which is the better prognostic situation in mantle cell lymphoma — leukemic phase only, or bulky symptomatic nodal disease? Why is this counter-intuitive?

Answer

Leukemic phase only is the LOW risk group. This is counter-intuitive because in most malignancies circulating disease implies dissemination and a worse outlook. In mantle cell, the leukemic-only presentation (often with SOX11 mutation) marks a more indolent biological variant, whereas symptomatic bulky disease, 17p deletion, blastoid variant, raised lactate dehydrogenase and a high proliferation index define high risk.

Topic 11 — Hodgkin lymphoma

Lymphoid

Hodgkin lymphoma — bimodal, curable, and full of odd clinical signs

Epidemiology

two peaks

Bimodal: age 15–35, and over 55. 50% are associated with Epstein-Barr virus.

Diagnosis

the biopsy requirement

EXCISIONAL lymph node biopsy. As Dr. Winters emphasized, “you almost always have to do a full excisional biopsy for these” — a needle core is usually insufficient, because the diagnosis depends on seeing the architecture around the rare malignant cells.

Staging

two components

PET/CT and erythrocyte sedimentation rate. The sedimentation rate is unusual as a staging element and is worth remembering.

Prognosis

the headline

Curable. Better in younger adults, and dependent on stage, age and erythrocyte sedimentation rate.

🔑 The presenting features — several are peculiar to Hodgkin
  • Local or diffuse lymphadenopathy
  • Pruritus
  • Splenomegaly
  • Night sweats, weight loss, malaise (B symptoms)
  • Alcohol intolerance — pain in involved nodes after drinking alcohol. Rare, but essentially specific to Hodgkin lymphoma when present.
  • Peripheral eosinophilia
  • Nonspecific back pain

The combination of itching, alcohol-induced nodal pain and eosinophilia in a young adult with lymphadenopathy is a classic examination stem.

Treatment. Chemotherapy: ABVD — doxorubicin (Adriamycin), bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine. For relapsed disease: autologous or allogeneic stem cell transplant, brentuximab vedotin (an anti-CD30 monoclonal antibody conjugate), and PD-1 antibodies.

🧠 Why CD30 and PD-1 both work here

The malignant Reed-Sternberg cells express CD30, which makes them an ideal target for the antibody-drug conjugate brentuximab vedotin. Separately, Hodgkin lymphoma is unusually dependent on evading the immune cells that surround it — the tumour is mostly reactive inflammatory infiltrate — which is why PD-1 blockade is exceptionally effective in this disease compared with most cancers.

Q: A 24-year-old has painless cervical lymphadenopathy, itching, and reports that the nodes ache after drinking beer. Bloods show eosinophilia. What is the likely diagnosis and what biopsy will you request?

Answer

Hodgkin lymphoma. Alcohol intolerance (nodal pain after alcohol), pruritus and peripheral eosinophilia together are highly characteristic, and the age fits the first peak of the bimodal 15–35 / over 55 distribution. Request an excisional lymph node biopsy, not a core needle — the diagnosis depends on nodal architecture. Stage with PET/CT and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate. The disease is curable, and treatment is ABVD.

Q: Why is an excisional biopsy required rather than a fine needle aspirate in suspected Hodgkin lymphoma?

Answer

Because the malignant Reed-Sternberg cells are rare within the node and the diagnosis rests on seeing them in context — the surrounding reactive inflammatory architecture. A fine needle aspirate or small core may sample only reactive cells and miss the diagnosis entirely. The same principle applies to most lymphoma diagnoses.

Topic 12 — Monoclonal gammopathies

Plasma cell disorders

Multiple myeloma, MGUS and smoldering myeloma

The far right of Figure 1 — a cancer of the fully differentiated plasma cell. Its damage comes from what the cell secretes. And uniquely in this lecture, it has two well-defined precursor states that are managed by surveillance rather than treatment.

Workup

  • Serum protein electrophoresis — detects and quantifies the monoclonal protein
  • Serum light chains
  • Comprehensive metabolic panel — for calcium and renal function
  • Complete blood count
  • Skeletal survey
  • Equivocal findings: confirm with PET/CT or magnetic resonance imaging
🧠 Why light chains matter so much

Dr. Winters explained it directly: antibodies are made of a heavy chain and a light chain. A myeloma clone can produce light chains in very high concentration, and because they are small they are filtered by the glomerulus and precipitate in the tubules — causing kidney failure. This is why serum light chains are measured separately from the serum protein electrophoresis: a patient can have significant light chain disease with an unimpressive monoclonal spike, and the kidney is the organ that pays.

⚠️

High risk features

  • Severe anemia
  • Chronic renal failure
  • Very high monoclonal protein concentration
  • Severe hypercalcemia
  • High beta-2 microglobulin
  • Fluorescence in situ hybridization: 17p = bad
📆

Highly dependent on age, fitness, stage and fluorescence in situ hybridization. Many patients will live 7–10 years or beyond.

The counselling point is real reassurance combined with genuine commitment to surveillance, because the 1% per year does not stop.

Q: A 66-year-old has an IgG monoclonal protein of 1.8 g/dL, 6% plasma cells on marrow biopsy, normal calcium, creatinine and hemoglobin, and no bone lesions. Diagnosis, risk, and plan?

Answer

MGUS — under 10% plasma cells and under 3 g/dL monoclonal protein, with no end organ damage. Risk of progression to multiple myeloma is 1% per year. No treatment. Surveillance with serum protein electrophoresis, serum light chains, complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel every 6 months, plus a skeletal survey at least annually.

Q: How does the progression risk of smoldering myeloma differ from MGUS, and how does that change surveillance?

Answer

MGUS progresses at a flat 1% per year. Smoldering myeloma is front-loaded: 10% per year for the first 5 years, then 3% per year for 5 years, then 1% per year — roughly ten times the early risk. Accordingly surveillance is intensified: labs every 4 months rather than every 6, and consider an annual PET scan.

Q: Why can a myeloma patient develop renal failure even when the serum protein electrophoresis spike is modest?

Answer

Because of free light chains. Antibodies comprise heavy and light chains, and a clone may secrete light chains in very high concentration. Being small, they are filtered at the glomerulus and precipitate in the tubules, causing kidney failure — while contributing little to the serum protein electrophoresis peak. This is precisely why serum light chains are measured separately in the workup.

Glossary and abbreviations

Every abbreviation and technical term used anywhere in this file, spelled out and defined, grouped by theme.

Foundational concepts
Hematopoiesis
Blood cell formation in the bone marrow, beginning with the pluripotent stem cell.
Pluripotent stem cell
The earliest marrow cell, capable of becoming any blood cell. Transformation at this stage produces the most aggressive disease.
Differentiation
The process by which a cell becomes progressively more specialized. A one-way street — the central organizing idea of the lecture.
Myeloid lineage
The branch producing red cells, platelets, granulocytes and monocytes. Diseases: AML, MDS, CML, PV, ET, MF.
Lymphoid lineage
The branch producing B cells, T cells and natural killer cells. Diseases: ALL, the lymphomas, CLL, myeloma.
Blast
An immature precursor cell. More than 20% blasts in marrow or blood defines acute leukemia.
Indolent
Slow-growing. Compatible with years or decades of life, but generally not curable.
Aggressive
Rapidly progressive — fatal in months untreated, but usually treated with curative intent.
B symptoms
Night sweats, unexplained weight loss and fever. An adverse prognostic feature across the lymphomas.
Paraneoplastic syndrome
A disease manifestation caused by the tumour's products or immune effects rather than by its bulk — for example autoimmune hemolytic anemia in chronic lymphocytic leukemia.
Extramedullary
Outside the bone marrow.
Leukemic phase
Lymphoma cells circulating in the peripheral blood.
The diseases
AML — acute myeloid leukemia
Over 20% myeloid blasts. Risk factors: prior chemotherapy, antecedent MDS or MPN, Down syndrome. Fit patients get "7 + 3" induction; unfit patients get a hypomethylating agent ± venetoclax.
APL — acute promyelocytic leukemia
A distinct AML variant with t(15;17) / PML-RARA. Causes severe DIC and early death from bleeding if unrecognized; treated with all-trans retinoic acid for 90% overall survival.
ALL — acute lymphoblastic leukemia
The commonest childhood malignancy; bimodal peaks at 2–5 and over 50. 85% precursor B-cell. Lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly and CNS involvement. Survival: children over 90%, adolescents/young adults 50–70%, older adults 30%.
MDS — myelodysplastic syndrome
Dysplastic, ineffective production: a cellular marrow with cytopenic blood. Epigenetic pathophysiology, hence hypomethylating drugs. 30% progress to AML; 50% die of bleeding or infection.
CML — chronic myeloid leukemia
t(9;22) / BCR-ABL / Philadelphia chromosome. Chronic (85%), accelerated, and blast phase. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors changed median survival from 2–5 years to 95% alive at 10 years.
MPN — myeloproliferative neoplasm
The JAK2/STAT-driven group: polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia and myelofibrosis. Normally differentiated cells in excess; the danger is thrombosis.
PV — polycythemia vera
Red cell excess with aquagenic pruritus, erythromelalgia, hyperviscosity and thrombosis. Phlebotomy to hematocrit below 45 (men) or 42 (women), plus aspirin ± hydroxyurea. 10% to myelofibrosis, 1% to AML.
ET — essential thrombocythemia
Platelet excess. Risk-stratified by only two factors: prior thrombosis and age over 60. Hydroxyurea, to reduce thrombosis. 4% transform to myelofibrosis.
MF — myelofibrosis
Marrow replaced by fibrous tissue, giving symptom burden and pancytopenia. Ruxolitinib improves symptoms and survival but not counts; allogeneic transplant is the only cure.
DLBCL — diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
The commonest aggressive lymphoma. R-CHOP first line, curative intent. Prognosis by International Prognostic Index; double hit disease is far worse.
Burkitt lymphoma
Highly aggressive, t(8;14), associated with HIV and allogeneic transplant. Children 90%, adults 60–70% survival. High tumour lysis risk.
CLL / SLL
One mature B-cell neoplasm with two names: CLL in blood and marrow, SLL in nodes and spleen. CD5+, CD10−, CD20+, CD23+.
FL — follicular lymphoma
Indolent, usually asymptomatic and usually advanced at diagnosis. Often years to first treatment; about a third are never treated. Median progression-free survival 5–8 years; 30% transform over 10 years.
MZL — marginal zone lymphoma
Very indolent, in nodal, extranodal (MALT) and splenic forms, arising in chronic inflammation. Gastric MALT may be cured by eradicating Helicobacter pylori; nodal disease by treating hepatitis C.
LPL — lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (Waldenström macroglobulinemia)
Indolent lymphoma with MYD88 mutation and an IgM paraprotein, causing hyperviscosity, neuropathy, cold agglutinin hemolysis, cryoglobulinemia and bleeding.
MCL — mantle cell lymphoma
Rare (~3,300/year), twice as common in men, median age 68, 90% gastrointestinal involvement. t(11;14) / cyclin D1. Aggressive, and everyone relapses.
HL — Hodgkin lymphoma
Bimodal (15–35 and over 55), 50% Epstein-Barr virus associated. Pruritus, alcohol intolerance, eosinophilia. Excisional biopsy; stage with PET/CT and ESR. Curable; ABVD.
MM — multiple myeloma
A malignancy of the fully differentiated plasma cell. Damage from the monoclonal protein and free light chains. Many patients live 7–10 years or beyond.
MGUS
Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance: under 10% plasma cells and under 3 g/dL paraprotein. 1% per year progression. Watched, not treated.
Smoldering myeloma
10–60% plasma cells or 3 g/dL or more paraprotein, without end organ damage. Progression 10%/year for 5 years, then 3%/year for 5, then 1%/year.
Genetics and laboratory
Translocation, written t(a;b)
Exchange of material between two chromosomes, often creating a fusion gene with new, pathological activity.
BCR-ABL / Philadelphia chromosome
The product of t(9;22): a constitutively active tyrosine kinase. Defines CML and a subset of B-cell ALL.
PML-RARA
The fusion of t(15;17) defining acute promyelocytic leukemia; the target of all-trans retinoic acid.
Cyclin D1
A cell-cycle protein overexpressed in mantle cell lymphoma via t(11;14).
MYC
A proliferation-driving oncogene; translocated in Burkitt lymphoma (t(8;14)) and in double hit DLBCL.
Double hit lymphoma
MYC translocated adjacent to BCL2 or BCL6 — approximately 30% overall survival, worse than the highest clinical risk score.
JAK2 / STAT
Janus kinase 2 and its downstream signal transducer; mutated in the myeloproliferative neoplasms, leaving growth signalling permanently on.
MYD88
The gene mutated in most lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma.
SOX11
A transcription factor marking lower-risk mantle cell lymphoma.
T315I
The BCR-ABL gatekeeper mutation causing resistance to most tyrosine kinase inhibitors; needs ponatinib or omacetaxine.
TP53 / 17p deletion
Loss or mutation of the major tumour suppressor. Adverse in CLL, mantle cell and myeloma alike.
IgVH mutation status
Immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region. In CLL, mutated is favorable and unmutated unfavorable — the reverse of the usual intuition.
FISH — fluorescence in situ hybridization
A technique using fluorescent probes to detect specific chromosomal deletions or rearrangements.
Flow cytometry
Laser-based identification of cell surface antigens; how lineage and immunophenotype are assigned.
CD5, CD10, CD20, CD23, CD30
Cell surface markers. CD20 marks B cells (target of rituximab); CD5 is normally a T-cell marker but is aberrantly present in CLL and mantle cell; CD23 separates the two; CD30 marks Reed-Sternberg cells (target of brentuximab).
Immunophenotype
The pattern of surface markers that identifies a malignancy — for CLL, CD5+, CD10−, CD20+, CD23+.
SPEP — serum protein electrophoresis
Separates serum proteins by charge, revealing a monoclonal spike.
Free light chains
Unpaired immunoglobulin light chains. Small enough to be filtered by the glomerulus, where they precipitate and cause renal failure.
Beta-2 microglobulin
A serum protein reflecting tumour burden; high levels are adverse in myeloma.
LDH — lactate dehydrogenase
A marker of cell turnover; elevated levels are adverse in lymphoma.
ESR — erythrocyte sedimentation rate
A nonspecific inflammatory marker, used unusually as part of Hodgkin lymphoma staging and prognosis.
Excisional biopsy
Removal of an entire lymph node, preserving architecture. Required for most lymphoma diagnoses, especially Hodgkin.
Complications and emergencies
Bone marrow failure
Anemia, thrombocytopenia and neutropenia together. Universal at presentation in acute leukemia.
DIC — disseminated intravascular coagulation
Uncontrolled intravascular consumption of clotting factors: low fibrinogen, prolonged PT and PTT, low platelets, with schistocytes. Classically APL.
Thrombotic microangiopathy
Mechanical destruction of red cells in small vessels, producing schistocytes on the smear.
Schistocyte
A fragmented red cell — the smear hallmark of microangiopathic hemolysis.
TLS — tumour lysis syndrome
Massive cell death releasing intracellular contents: hyperuricemia (renal failure), hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia and hyperkalemia (arrhythmia). Everything up except calcium.
Allopurinol / rasburicase
Allopurinol blocks uric acid production (prevention); rasburicase enzymatically degrades existing uric acid (severe disease).
Sodium polystyrene
A potassium-binding resin, used for the hyperkalemia of tumour lysis syndrome.
Hyperviscosity syndrome
Sludging of blood from a very high paraprotein level, classically pentameric IgM. Vertigo, blurred vision, headache, nystagmus, ataxia; severe cases stroke, coma, delirium. Treated with emergent plasmapheresis.
Plasmapheresis
Removal and replacement of plasma to strip out a pathological protein.
IgM flare
A transient rise in IgM at the start of treatment for lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma, which can worsen hyperviscosity.
Cryoglobulinemia
Proteins precipitating in the cold, causing Raynaud phenomenon, urticaria, purpura, acral cyanosis and tissue necrosis.
Cold agglutinin disease
Cold-reactive antibody causing autoimmune hemolysis; associated with IgM paraproteins.
Richter transformation
Transformation of CLL into aggressive large cell lymphoma. 2–9%; very aggressive and usually fatal.
Hypogammaglobulinemia
Low antibody levels, causing recurrent sinopulmonary infection in CLL. Treated with intravenous immunoglobulin.
Treatments
Induction / consolidation
Induction aims to achieve complete remission; consolidation aims to eliminate residual disease afterwards.
"7 + 3"
AML induction: 7 days of cytarabine with 3 days of daunorubicin or idarubicin. Requires 3–4 weeks in hospital.
CR — complete remission
No detectable disease by standard assessment. The goal of induction.
Hypomethylating agent
Azacitidine or decitabine — epigenetic drugs used in MDS and in unfit AML.
Hydroxyurea
An oral cytoreductive agent: buys time in hyperleukocytosis, and controls counts in PV and ET.
ATRA — all-trans retinoic acid
A vitamin A derivative that forces malignant promyelocytes to differentiate. Curative in APL.
TKI — tyrosine kinase inhibitor
Imatinib (first generation); nilotinib, dasatinib, bosutinib (second); ponatinib and omacetaxine for T315I.
Ruxolitinib
A JAK inhibitor. In myelofibrosis it improves symptoms and survival but not the cytopenias; also used in refractory PV.
R-CHOP
Rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin (hydroxydaunorubicin), vincristine (Oncovin), prednisone. First-line for DLBCL.
ABVD
Doxorubicin (Adriamycin), bleomycin, vinblastine, dacarbazine. Standard for Hodgkin lymphoma.
Rituximab / obinutuzumab
Anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies.
Brentuximab vedotin
An anti-CD30 antibody-drug conjugate used in Hodgkin lymphoma.
BTK inhibitors
Ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, pirtobrutinib — Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors, central to CLL treatment.
Venetoclax
A BCL-2 inhibitor, restoring apoptosis. Used in CLL, mantle cell and unfit AML.
PI3K inhibitors
Idelalisib, duvelisib, copanlisib, alpelisib — phosphoinositide 3-kinase inhibitors.
Proteasome inhibitors
Drugs such as bortezomib blocking protein degradation, to which antibody-secreting plasma cells are exquisitely sensitive.
Thalidomide derivatives ("-amides")
Immunomodulatory drugs such as lenalidomide and pomalidomide, used in myeloma and (with rituximab) in follicular lymphoma.
CAR-T — chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy
The patient's own T cells re-engineered to recognize a tumour antigen.
BiTE — bispecific T-cell engager
An antibody binding both the tumour cell and a T cell, bringing them together.
PD-1 antibody
An immune checkpoint inhibitor, unusually effective in Hodgkin lymphoma.
Allogeneic stem cell transplant (allo-SCT)
Transplant from a donor. The only curative option in MDS, myelofibrosis and follicular lymphoma, and used for higher-risk AML.
Autologous stem cell transplant (auto-SCT)
Transplant using the patient's own harvested stem cells. Used in myeloma and mantle cell lymphoma.
Chemoimmunotherapy
Cytotoxic chemotherapy combined with a monoclonal antibody, such as R-CHOP.
Splenectomy
Removal of the spleen — the specific treatment for splenic marginal zone lymphoma.

CONSTRUCT & ACUTE LEUKEMIA

The core construct

  • A hematopoietic cell acquires a mutation disrupting differentiation and maturation of its progeny.
  • The disease depends on the maturation stage of the transformed cell.
  • Less differentiated → AGGRESSIVE. More differentiated → INDOLENT.
  • Mixed myeloid + lymphoid features = transformation before the branch point = worst prognosis.

Acute leukemia — the definition

  • >20% BLASTS in marrow OR peripheral blood. The single most reusable number in this lecture.
  • White count may be high, normal OR low. Universally bone marrow failure (anemia + thrombocytopenia + neutropenia).
  • Untreated: death within 12 weeks.
  • Vignette: WBC 87K with 90% blasts but neutrophils 0.6 → functionally neutropenic despite a huge count.

“Extinguish the fire”

  • Transfuse: hemoglobin <7 · platelets <10,000, or <50,000 if actively bleeding.
  • Identify and treat infection.
  • Hydroxyurea to buy time by cytoreducing the white count.

Two emergencies

  • DIC: low fibrinogen, high PT and PTT, low platelets, schistocytes. Classically acute promyelocytic leukemia.
  • Tumour lysis: EVERYTHING UP EXCEPT CALCIUM. Urate ↑ → renal failure (allopurinol to prevent, rasburicase if severe) · phosphate ↑ · calcium ↓ (replace) · potassium ↑ → arrhythmia (sodium polystyrene).

AML

  • Diagnosis: marrow biopsy, >20% blasts, myeloid antigens on flow cytometry.
  • Risk factors: prior cytotoxic chemotherapy · prior MDS or MPN · Down syndrome.
  • Fit: “7 + 3” = daunorubicin or idarubicin + cytarabine, 3–4 week hospitalization, goal complete remission → consolidation, allo-SCT for intermediate/high risk.
  • Unfit: hypomethylating agent (decitabine, azacitidine) ± venetoclax, supportive care, hospice.

APL — do not miss

  • t(15;17) → PML-RARA.
  • Severe DIC causes early death from BLEEDING in unrecognized cases.
  • All-trans retinoic acid → 90% overall survival. Forces differentiation — the construct made into a drug.
  • Start ATRA on suspicion, before cytogenetics return.

ALL

  • Commonest childhood malignancy. Bimodal 2–5 and >50. 85% precursor B, 15% T.
  • Distinguishing findings: lymphadenopathy · splenomegaly · CNS involvement.
  • BCR-ABL positive B-ALL responds to oral tyrosine kinase inhibitors.
  • Survival: children >90% · adolescents/young adults 50–70% · older adults 30%.
CHRONIC MYELOID DISEASE

MDS

  • Dysplastic and INEFFECTIVE production — cellular marrow, cytopenic blood.
  • Presentation: anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia. Pathophysiology: epigenetic.
  • 30% progress to AML; 50% die of bleeding or infection — the cytopenias kill more than transformation does.
  • Treatment: supportive (transfusion, growth factors, antibiotics) · hypomethylating drugs · allo-SCT the only cure.

CML

  • t(9;22) = BCR-ABL = Philadelphia chromosome. Three names, one lesion.
  • Often asymptomatic; neutrophilia, BASOPHILIA, eosinophilia, thrombocytosis, splenomegaly.
  • Chronic phase = 85% of patients.
  • Accelerated: refractory high white count · growing spleen · platelets >1,000 × 10⁹/L refractory · platelets <100 × 10⁹/L unrelated to therapy · basophils ≥20% · blasts 10–19% · new clonal abnormality.
  • Blast phase: >20% blasts.
  • Imatinib (1st gen) · nilotinib, dasatinib, bosutinib (2nd) · T315I → ponatinib or omacetaxine. Treatment-free intervals possible.
  • Median survival 2–5 years → 95% alive at 10 years.

The JAK2 neoplasms

  • All driven by JAK2/STAT; produce normally differentiated cells; the danger is THROMBOSIS, not failure.
  • PV: aquagenic pruritus (itch after hot shower), erythromelalgia, hyperviscosity, arterial + venous clot. Risks: prior thrombosis, age >60, WBC >11K, hematocrit >45, platelets >400K. Phlebotomy: men <45, women <42 + aspirin ± hydroxyurea; ruxolitinib if refractory. 10% → myelofibrosis, 1% → AML.
  • ET: risk = prior thrombosis + age >60 only. Hydroxyurea to reduce thrombosis. 4% → myelofibrosis.
  • MF: symptom burden + PANCYTOPENIA. Ruxolitinib for intermediate-2/high risk — improves symptoms AND survival but NOT the cytopenias. Allo-SCT the only curative option.
LYMPHOID & PLASMA CELL

Aggressive B-cell lymphoma

  • Death in 3–6 months untreated — but treatment intent is CURE. Watch for tumour lysis.
  • DLBCL: rapidly growing mass, B symptoms, may involve CNS. R-CHOP first line; relapsed → CAR-T, BiTE, allo-SCT.
  • International Prognostic Index (1 point each): age >60 · raised LDH · ECOG ≥2 · Ann Arbor III/IV · >1 extranodal site. Survival 0–1: 81% · 2: 69% · 3: 53% · 4–5: 50%.
  • DOUBLE HIT (MYC next to BCL2 or BCL6): ~30% — worse than IPI 5.
  • Burkitt: t(8;14), any age, associated with HIV and allogeneic transplant. Children 90%, adults 60–70%.

Indolent B-cell lymphoma

  • CLL and SLL are ONE disease: blood/marrow = CLL, nodes/spleen = SLL.
  • CLL immunophenotype: CD5+, CD10−, CD20+, CD23+.
  • FISH: 13q del favorable · trisomy 12 or negative intermediate · 17p or 11q del HIGH RISK. IgVH MUTATED = favorable (counter-intuitive). TP53 unfavorable.
  • Treat only for: pancytopenia · bulky lymphadenopathy · severe symptoms · threatened end organ function. Not for a rising lymphocyte count.
  • Drugs: BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib, acalabrutinib, pirtobrutinib) · venetoclax · PI3K inhibitors · anti-CD20. 17p → BTK or BCL-2 inhibitor.
  • Paraneoplastic: autoimmune hemolytic anemia · immune thrombocytopenia · hypogammaglobulinemia → sinopulmonary infection → IVIG.
  • Richter transformation 2–9% — very aggressive, usually fatal.
  • Follicular: asymptomatic, usually stage III/IV, often years to first treatment; ~1/3 never treated. Median PFS 5–8 years. 30% transform over 10 years. Allo-SCT the only cure.
  • Marginal zone: arises in chronic inflammation. Gastric MALT + H. pylori → eradication can CURE. Nodal + hepatitis C → antivirals may cure. Splenic → splenectomy. MALT sites: stomach, intestine, salivary, lacrimal, thyroid, breast.

Waldenström (LPL)

  • MYD88 mutation + IgM monoclonal protein. IgM is a pentamer — that one fact explains everything below.
  • Hyperviscosity: vertigo, blurred vision, headache, nystagmus, ataxia → stroke, coma. EMERGENT PLASMAPHERESIS.
  • Ascending sensorimotor neuropathy · cold agglutinin hemolysis · cryoglobulinemia (Raynaud, purpura, acral necrosis).
  • Bleeding: IgM interferes with fibrin polymerization → oozing from nose, gums, gut. Watch the IgM flare on starting treatment.

Mantle cell

  • t(11;14) → cyclin D1. Rare (~3,300/yr), 2× in men, median age 68, 90% GI involvement.
  • “Aggressive and everyone relapses.”
  • High risk: symptomatic · 17p del · blastoid variant · raised LDH · high proliferation index. Low risk: leukemic phase only, SOX11.
  • CD23 NEGATIVE — that is what separates it from CLL.

Hodgkin lymphoma

  • Bimodal 15–35 and >55. 50% EBV-associated.
  • Pruritus · ALCOHOL INTOLERANCE · peripheral eosinophilia · B symptoms · splenomegaly · nonspecific back pain.
  • EXCISIONAL lymph node biopsy — architecture is required for diagnosis.
  • Stage with PET/CT and ESR. Prognosis by stage, age, ESR. Curable.
  • ABVD; relapsed → auto/allo SCT, brentuximab (anti-CD30), PD-1 antibody.

Plasma cell disorders

  • Workup: SPEP · serum light chains · CMP · CBC · skeletal survey; equivocal → PET/CT or MRI.
  • Free light chains are filtered and precipitate in tubules → RENAL FAILURE, even with a modest spike.
  • High risk: severe anemia · chronic renal failure · very high paraprotein · severe hypercalcemia · high beta-2 microglobulin · 17p on FISH.
  • Many patients live 7–10 years or beyond. Treatment: “-amides” · proteasome inhibitors · monoclonal antibodies · auto-SCT · CAR-T · BiTE · palliative radiotherapy.
  • MGUS: <10% plasma cells AND <3 g/dL → 1%/year. Labs every 6 months, skeletal survey annually.
  • Smoldering: 10–60% plasma cells OR ≥3 g/dL → 10%/yr ×5, then 3%/yr ×5, then 1%/yr. Labs every 4 months, consider annual PET.
  • Neither is treated. End organ damage is what makes it active myeloma.
