Lymph Node: Normal Architecture & Reactive Lymphadenopathy

Before you can call a lymph node abnormal you have to know exactly what a normal one looks like — which zone holds which cell, and why a reactive node can look alarming and still be benign. This lecture builds that baseline, then walks a pattern-based approach: look at the low-power architecture first, let the pattern narrow the differential to two or three entities, and only then reach for stains.

Orientation — what this lecture is asking of you

Dr. Sirotnikov is a pediatric pathologist and hematopathologist. This lecture and the companion Lymphoproliferative Disorders lecture are two halves of one argument: this one teaches you the normal node and the benign mimics, the other teaches you the malignancies those mimics get confused with. 🔑 The five things this lecture actually wants you to be able to do
  1. Name the three zones of a normal lymph node and the cell type that lives in each.Cortex is B cells, paracortex is T cells, medulla is vessels and cords. Nearly every pattern in the pattern-based approach is named after whichever zone has expanded.
  2. Recognize a reactive germinal center and list the features that make it reactive rather than neoplastic.Polarization, tingible body macrophages, an intact mantle zone, follicles that are not back-to-back, and a negative BCL2 stain.
  3. Use the low-power pattern to generate a short differential.Follicular, paracortical/interfollicular, diffuse, sinus, histiocytic/granulomatous. The pattern comes first; the stains confirm.
  4. Know the named reactive entities cold.Infectious mononucleosis, Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, Castleman disease, Rosai–Dorfman disease — each has one or two buzzwords that give it away.
  5. Separate infectious mononucleosis from classic Hodgkin lymphoma.This is the single most testable discrimination in the lecture, because mononucleosis contains Reed–Sternberg-like cells and the consequences of getting it wrong are enormous.
🧠 The organizing habit: blue versus pink at low power

Dr. Sirotnikov returns to this in every case. Before you put the slide on high power, ask one question: where is it blue and where is it pink? The distribution of blue and pink IS the pattern, and the pattern is what generates your differential. A pathologist who starts at 40× has already lost the architecture, which is the most informative thing on the slide.

Topic 1 — Normal

The baseline

Normal lymph node architecture

A lymph node is a filter with a fixed floor plan. Lymph enters through afferent lymphatics into the subcapsular sinus, percolates through the cortex and paracortex, and leaves through the efferent lymphatic at the hilum. Every zone in that path houses a specific cell population — and every reactive or neoplastic pattern you will learn is simply one of those zones expanding.

The floor plan — afferent lymphatics enter at the capsule, efferent lymphatic leaves at the hilum

The three zones and what lives in each

🔵

Cortex — the B-cell zone

The outer (superficial) rim, packed with follicles.

  • Primary follicles have no germinal center — a uniform ball of small naive B cells that has not yet met antigen.
  • Secondary follicles contain a reactive germinal center — the B cell has met antigen and the follicle has switched on.
  • Finding both primary and secondary follicles side by side in one node is normal, not a red flag. Different lobules drain different tissue fields, so they see different antigen loads and react to different degrees.
🔴

Paracortex — the T-cell zone

Also called the interfollicular or deep cortical area — the space between and beneath the follicles.

  • Predominantly small T lymphocytes.
  • Also normally contains histiocytes (tissue macrophages), dendritic cells, plasma cells and scattered activated immunoblasts.
  • Dendritic cells are recognizable by pale chromatin, irregular nuclear contours and abundant cytoplasm — when they accumulate, the paracortex looks pale at low power.
  • Contains the high endothelial venules, the postcapillary venules through which circulating lymphocytes enter the node.
🟩

Medulla — cords and vessels

The inner zone, toward the hilum.

  • Medullary cords (plasma cells and lymphocytes) alternating with medullary sinuses.
  • This is where the vascular structures that drain the node sit.
  • Neoplasms can arise from any of these normal structures — which is why the map of the node doubles as a map of the lymphomas.
🔑 Always look for the capsule

When a pathologist receives lymphoid tissue, the first structural question is: is there a capsule? A capsule with a subcapsular sinus underneath it confirms that you are actually looking at a lymph node rather than at some other lymphoid aggregate (tonsil, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue, spleen, thymus). It sounds trivial; it changes the entire differential.

🩺 The subcapsular sinus is where you hunt for metastasis

Lymph arrives at the node through the afferent lymphatics and empties first into the subcapsular sinus. That makes the sinus the first place tumor cells land. When a node is being examined to rule out metastatic carcinoma or melanoma, the sinuses under the capsule are where you look first — a small cohesive cluster sitting in a sinus is the earliest finding.

Q: A lymph node biopsy is described as having “expansion of the interfollicular area with preserved follicles.” Which cell population has expanded, and which broad category of disease does that point toward first?

Answer

The interfollicular area is the paracortex, which is the T-cell zone. Paracortical expansion therefore points to a T-cell process — benign paracortical hyperplasia (as in infectious mononucleosis or dermatopathic lymphadenopathy) or, on the malignant side, a peripheral T-cell lymphoma. Note the important trap: mixed cellularity classic Hodgkin lymphoma also expands the interfollicular area, which is exactly why that subtype is the hardest one to separate from a reactive paracortical process.

Q: Why is it not alarming to find primary follicles (no germinal center) and secondary follicles (large reactive germinal centers) in the same lymph node?

Answer

Because different lobules of a node drain different fields of tissue and therefore see different antigen exposures. Variable reactivity across lobules produces follicles at different stages within one node. Variation in follicle size and shape is a feature of a reactive node; the monotony of uniform, crowded, back-to-back follicles is what should make you think follicular lymphoma.

Topic 2 — Normal

The most examinable structure in the node

The germinal center — and how to tell reactive from neoplastic

A reactive germinal center at high power is, in Dr. Sirotnikov’s words, “a scary image” — large cells, prominent nucleoli, brisk mitoses, apoptotic debris. It looks like a high-grade lymphoma and it is entirely normal. Knowing the five features that certify it as reactive is what keeps you from over-calling.

The germinal center reaction, schematically — dark zone to light zone to plasma cell or memory cell

Reactive germinal center at high power — centroblasts and tingible body macrophages

The two zones of a germinal center

Dark zone

Centroblast-rich

Centroblasts are the large activated cells: round nuclei, open (vesicular) chromatin, prominent nucleoli, and brisk mitotic activity. This is where somatic hypermutation happens, so it is also where cells fail and die — hence the macrophages.

Light zone

Centrocyte-rich

Centrocytes are the smaller cleaved cells being selected on affinity. The light zone appears paler because the cells are smaller and less densely packed, and it holds the follicular dendritic cell meshwork that presents antigen.

Tingible body macrophages

The pale holes

Macrophages engulfing the apoptotic bodies of B cells that failed selection. They create the “starry sky” look within the germinal center. Their presence is strong evidence of a reactive process.

Mantle zone

The rim

A cuff of small naive B cells around the germinal center. Well-defined in reactive follicles; attenuated or absent in follicular lymphoma.

Marginal zone

Beyond the mantle

A paler outer rim of memory B cells with more abundant cytoplasm. This is the normal counterpart of marginal zone lymphoma.

Polarization

The architectural clue

A reactive germinal center is polarized — a distinct dark zone at one pole and a light zone at the other. Follicular lymphoma loses polarization and looks uniform end to end.

🧠 Why BCL2 is negative in a normal germinal center — the logic worth remembering

BCL2 is an anti-apoptotic protein. The whole purpose of the germinal center is somatic hypermutation followed by ruthless selection: B cells randomly mutate their immunoglobulin variable regions, and the ones whose receptors got worse must die. To make that possible, normal germinal center B cells switch BCL2 OFF — they are deliberately kept easy to kill. That is why the tingible body macrophages have so much to eat.

Follicular lymphoma arises when the t(14;18) translocation puts BCL2 (chromosome 18) under the control of the immunoglobulin heavy chain promoter (chromosome 14), forcing BCL2 back on. The cells become unkillable and accumulate. So a BCL2-positive germinal center is the single most useful stain for separating follicular hyperplasia from follicular lymphoma, and the reason is mechanistic, not arbitrary.

Working through the follicular pattern with stains — note the BCL2-negative germinal center in a reactive node

🩺 Ki-67 across the lymphoid differential

Ki-67 is the proliferation marker — it labels every cell not in G0. Its value here is comparative:

  • Reactive germinal center: brisk, and polarized — visibly higher in the centroblast-rich dark zone than in the light zone. If you get a well-oriented section, that gradient is itself reassuring.
  • Mantle cell lymphoma and most indolent B-cell lymphomas (including marginal zone lymphoma): low Ki-67.
  • Burkitt lymphoma (covered in the lymphoproliferative lecture): over 95% — essentially every cell cycling.

Q: A lymph node shows numerous follicles. The germinal centers contain many macrophages full of apoptotic debris, show a clear dark zone and light zone, and are surrounded by crisp mantle cuffs. BCL2 stains the mantle and paracortical lymphocytes but leaves the germinal centers blank. Diagnosis?

Answer

Reactive follicular hyperplasia. Four independent features all point the same way: tingible body macrophages present, polarization preserved, mantle zones intact, and BCL2 negative within the germinal centers. The BCL2 pattern is the clincher — a positive germinal center would be the abnormal result, because normal germinal center B cells suppress BCL2 so that failed clones can undergo apoptosis.

Q: Why does the tingible body macrophage “starry sky” of a reactive germinal center not mean the same thing as the “starry sky” of Burkitt lymphoma?

Answer

The cell biology is identical — macrophages clearing apoptotic debris — but the context is opposite. In a reactive germinal center the apoptosis is programmed selection, confined within a follicle that still has a mantle zone and a light/dark polarity. In Burkitt lymphoma the starry sky is spread through a diffuse sheet of monotonous medium-sized cells with no follicular architecture at all, driven by an enormous proliferation rate (Ki-67 over 95%) with matching apoptosis. The starry sky is never diagnostic on its own — the architecture around it is what carries the meaning.

Topic 3 — Normal

Where each lymphocyte comes from

Lymphoid cell development — and why it predicts the lymphomas

The reason to learn normal lymphocyte maturation is not immunology for its own sake. It is that every lymphoid neoplasm recapitulates the phenotype of the normal cell it came from. If you know where a normal cell lives and which markers it carries at that stage, you can predict where its tumor will present and what the flow cytometry will show.

🔑 The single organizing split: antigen-independent versus antigen-dependent

Antigen-independent development

Happens largely during the fetal, neonatal and childhood periods for both B and T cells, and does not require ever having seen a foreign antigen.

  • B cells: in the bone marrow. Stem cell → pro-B → pre-B → immature B → mature naive (“virgin”) B cell.
  • T cells: in the thymus. Marrow stem cell migrates to the thymus → T-cell receptor gene rearrangement → selection → mature naive T cell.
  • This is where the precursor (lymphoblastic) neoplasms arise — which is why B-lymphoblastic leukemia fills the marrow and T-lymphoblastic lymphoma presents as a thymic (mediastinal) mass.

Antigen-dependent development

Happens in secondary lymphoid tissue — the lymph node — after the naive cell meets its antigen.

  • Naive B cells migrate to the node and undergo the germinal center reaction.
  • Naive T cells undergo blast transformation to become CD4-positive helper or CD8-positive cytotoxic effector cells, plus memory cells.
  • This is where the peripheral (mature) lymphomas arise — follicular, Burkitt, diffuse large B-cell, marginal zone, peripheral T-cell.

The B-cell pathway in detail

  1. Bone marrow: build a receptor without ever seeing an antigen.The B-cell receptor consists of a heavy chain and a light chain, generated by VDJ recombination — randomly assembling one V (variable), one D (diversity) and one J (joining) gene segment with a constant region for the heavy chain. Light chains use only V and J segments, with either a kappa or a lambda constant region. Randomness is the point: it generates the repertoire.
  2. Every stage carries its own set of surface markers.Pro-B, pre-B, immature B and mature B each have a characteristic marker combination. This is the whole basis of flow cytometry in B-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma — the neoplastic blasts recapitulate the phenotype of their normal counterpart, so an abnormal combination that does not match any normal stage is itself evidence of a clone.
  3. Migrate to the lymph node and meet antigen.On antigen encounter an activated B cell takes one of two routes: a rapid extrafollicular response producing short-lived plasmablasts, or entry into the germinal center reaction in secondary lymphoid tissue.
  4. The germinal center reaction is T-cell assisted.It permits class switch recombination (changing the antibody isotype, e.g. IgM to IgG) and somatic hypermutation (refining affinity). Both processes deliberately break and re-ligate DNA — which is precisely why the germinal center is where most oncogenic translocations happen.
  5. Exit as a plasma cell or a memory B cell.Plasma cells migrate back to the bone marrow, take up residence alongside the marrow vessels, and secrete immunoglobulin from there. Memory B cells are generated in parallel and persist.

The T-cell pathway in detail

T-cell development in the thymus — double negative to double positive to single positive

  1. Origin in the marrow, maturation in the thymus.T cells originate from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow and then migrate to the thymus. Everything that follows happens there.
  2. T-cell receptor gene rearrangement.Thymocytes build a unique T-cell receptor by rearranging DNA segments — the same combinatorial logic as VDJ recombination in B cells. Detecting a single rearranged pattern shared by a whole population is how T-cell clonality studies prove a lymphoma.
  3. Positive selection.Thymocytes are exposed to major histocompatibility complex molecules on thymic stromal cells. Those that weakly recognize self-MHC receive survival signals and continue to mature; those that recognize nothing undergo apoptosis. The test is: can this receptor see anything at all?
  4. Negative selection.The survivors are then tested against self-antigens presented on MHC. Those that bind self too strongly are eliminated by apoptosis. The test is: is this receptor dangerous to its owner?
  5. Lineage commitment.Only after both selections does the thymocyte commit to the CD4 helper or the CD8 cytotoxic lineage.
⚠️ The double-positive stage — a fact with a direct diagnostic payoff

During thymic development, thymocytes normally express BOTH CD4 and CD8 at the same time (the “double positive” stage) before committing to one lineage. No mature peripheral T cell does this. Therefore, when flow cytometry on a mediastinal mass shows a population co-expressing CD4 and CD8, you are looking at cells frozen at a thymic stage — a strong pointer to T-lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma. This is a favorite examination detail precisely because it is a normal-development fact that becomes a diagnostic one.

Q: A 14-year-old presents with a large anterior mediastinal mass and respiratory compromise. Flow cytometry of the biopsy shows an immature population co-expressing CD4 and CD8. What is the diagnosis, and what normal developmental fact explains the phenotype?

Answer

T-lymphoblastic lymphoma. The mass is in the mediastinum because that is where the thymus sits, and the thymus is where T cells undergo antigen-independent maturation. The phenotype is explained by the fact that normal developing thymocytes pass through a double-positive stage expressing both CD4 and CD8 before committing to a single lineage — the neoplasm is frozen at that stage. Remember the naming logic too: “-oma” means mass, so the lymphoma form presents as the thymic mass while the leukemic form fills the marrow.

Q: Why do so many oncogenic translocations in B-cell lymphomas involve the immunoglobulin heavy chain locus on chromosome 14?

Answer

Because the germinal center B cell deliberately breaks and re-joins the DNA at the immunoglobulin loci during class switch recombination and somatic hypermutation (and earlier, during VDJ recombination in the marrow). These are controlled double-strand breaks — but errors during them can join an oncogene to the transcriptionally hyperactive immunoglobulin heavy chain promoter, which then drives it permanently. That single mechanism explains t(14;18) BCL2 in follicular lymphoma, t(8;14) MYC in Burkitt lymphoma, and t(11;14) cyclin D1 in mantle cell lymphoma. Potentially oncogenic mutations occur most frequently in germinal center B cells for exactly this reason.

Topic 4 — Approach

What happens to the specimen, and what the site already tells you

Specimen handling and site-based clues

The lymph node / lymphoma protocol

Lymph node biopsies are handled differently from most surgical specimens because the most informative studies require fresh, unfixed tissue. Once tissue goes into formalin, flow cytometry and conventional cytogenetics are no longer possible.

The processing protocol — fresh tissue is triaged before anything is fixed

  1. Receive fresh tissue from the operating room and serially section it.Sectioning both exposes the cut surface for imprints and lets the pathologist see gross architecture — matted nodes, capsular fibrosis, necrosis, fish-flesh cut surface.
  2. Make touch preparations (touch imprints).Take an unstained slide and either gently smear a portion of tissue on it or simply tap the cut surface against it. Because lymphocytes are discohesive, they readily transfer to the slide. Air dry, then stain. Extra unstained slides are saved in case ancillary studies are needed later.
  3. Triage on the touch prep — this drives everything else.A rapid morphologic read tells you which ancillary studies to order while the tissue is still fresh.
  4. Allocate tissue to ancillary studies.Flow cytometry (needs a cell suspension from fresh tissue), conventional cytogenetics (needs living, dividing cells), molecular studies.
  5. Submit the remainder for routine processing.Fix, embed in paraffin, cut sections, stain — this yields the morphology you actually diagnose from, plus the blocks for immunohistochemistry and FISH.

The MIAMI mnemonic — Malignancies, Infections, Autoimmune, Miscellaneous, Iatrogenic

Historical clues mapped to suggested diagnoses and initial tests

🩺 MIAMI — a workable way to think broadly without a list

When a patient presents with lymphadenopathy, the differential is genuinely enormous. The mnemonic keeps it structured:

  • M — Malignancies: lymphomas, leukemias, metastases, Kaposi sarcoma, skin neoplasms.
  • I — Infections: bacterial (cat-scratch disease, tularemia, syphilis), granulomatous (tuberculosis, histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis), viral (Epstein–Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, HIV, adenovirus, rubella), and other (fungal, Lyme, toxoplasmosis).
  • A — Autoimmune disorders: rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren syndrome, dermatomyositis, Still disease.
  • M — Miscellaneous / unusual: Castleman disease, Kikuchi lymphadenitis, Kimura disease, sarcoidosis, histiocytoses, Kawasaki disease.
  • I — Iatrogenic: medications, serum sickness.

Then narrow using site of involvement, exposures, and clinical history — the fever, sore throat and splenomegaly combination in a teenager, for instance, immediately reorders the whole list toward viral pharyngitis.

Q: A lymph node arrives fresh in the pathology laboratory at 4 p.m. The resident, wanting to be efficient, drops the entire specimen into formalin. Which studies has this made impossible, and why?

Answer

Flow cytometry and conventional cytogenetics (karyotype). Flow cytometry requires a viable single-cell suspension, and conventional cytogenetics requires living cells that can be cultured and driven into metaphase. Formalin cross-links proteins and kills the cells, so both are lost. Immunohistochemistry, FISH and most molecular studies (PCR, next-generation sequencing) still work on fixed paraffin-embedded tissue — but flow and karyotype are one-way doors. This is exactly why the protocol triages fresh tissue before fixation.

Q: A 62-year-old smoker has a single hard, fixed left supraclavicular node. How does the location alone change your differential compared with a soft, mobile, tender anterior cervical node in a 17-year-old?

Answer

The hard, fixed supraclavicular node is worrying for malignancy — Hodgkin lymphoma, or metastatic carcinoma from an intrathoracic or intra-abdominal primary. Fixation implies extracapsular extension. In the 17-year-old, a soft, mobile, tender anterior cervical node points to a reactive/infectious cause — most often viral pharyngitis or infectious mononucleosis. Tenderness generally favors an inflammatory process (rapid capsular stretch); painless, hard, matted nodes favor neoplasm.

Topic 5 — Approach

The core method of the lecture

The pattern-based approach to a lymph node

Dr. Sirotnikov’s reason for teaching this is explicit: it stops you being overwhelmed. Rather than trying to recall every entity that can involve a node, you read the low-power architecture, decide which pattern you are in, and inherit a differential of two or three — which stains can then settle. The same method appears in the lymphoproliferative lecture, applied to the malignant side.

The full pattern reference (Tzankov & Dirnhofer) — a lookup table, not a memorization target

🔑 The pattern is the question; the stains are the answer

For each pattern below, hold one idea: every benign pattern has a malignant counterpart that produces the same low-power look. The pattern tells you which pair you are choosing between. That is what makes the method worth learning — it is not a classification scheme, it is a way of framing the actual decision.

Pattern 1 — Follicular

Architecture is distorted but the interfollicular / paracortical space is preserved; the predominant process is follicles.

✅ Favors reactive follicular hyperplasia

  • Germinal centers of varying size and shape
  • Follicles not back-to-back — paracortex still visible between them
  • Tingible body macrophages present; polarization preserved
  • BCL2 negative within germinal centers

⚠️ Favors follicular lymphoma

  • Monotonous follicles, back-to-back, effacing the paracortex
  • No tingible body macrophages; polarization lost
  • Attenuated or absent mantle zones
  • BCL2 positive within the follicles; t(14;18) on molecular testing

Regardless of how benign it looks, you still stain and still correlate with the clinical history. A reactive-appearing follicular pattern in a 68-year-old with widespread painless lymphadenopathy is a different proposition from the same picture in a 6-year-old with a sore throat.

Follicular pattern variants — note the concentric onion-skinning and expanded follicular dendritic cell meshwork of Castleman disease in panels C and E

Pattern 2 — Interfollicular / paracortical / mixed

🧠 What expands the paracortex
  • Paracortical T-cell hyperplasia with increased dendritic cells → think dermatopathic lymphadenopathy, the reactive node draining chronically inflamed skin. This matters enormously in mycosis fungoides, where a dermatopathic node must be distinguished from true nodal involvement — because that distinction changes the stage. (Covered in detail in the lymphoproliferative lecture.)
  • Numerous eosinophils in this pattern → Kimura disease (a lower-yield entity, worth recognizing by name only).
  • Malignant counterparts: peripheral T-cell lymphoma, angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma, and — the trap — mixed cellularity classic Hodgkin lymphoma, which characteristically expands the interfollicular zone while leaving reactive follicles standing.

Interfollicular and diffuse patterns — expansion between preserved follicles

Pattern 3 — Diffuse

  • Paracortical hyperplasia with numerous immunoblasts. Benign consideration: infectious mononucleosis. This is the pattern that produces the mottled low-power appearance described in the next section.
  • The neoplastic counterpart is Hodgkin lymphoma — so this is always the differential to be careful with. Stains resolve it, and the logic is in the mononucleosis section below.
  • Sheets of large cells throughout → diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

Pattern 4 — Sinus

The histiocytic family of patterns, side by side

Sinus histiocytosis

Normal reactive

Mild expansion of the subcapsular and medullary sinuses by bland-looking histiocytes. A normal reactive phenomenon, not a disease.

Rosai–Dorfman disease

Sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy

Same compartment, but the sinuses are massively expanded and the nodes are enlarged. This one is neoplastic, with its own immunophenotype (S100 positive) and its own buzzword (emperipolesis). Full section below.

Hemophagocytosis

Debris inside histiocytes

Histiocytes containing engulfed red cells, apoptotic bodies and degenerated cells. Distinct from emperipolesis — see the contrast box in the Rosai–Dorfman section.

Metastatic carcinoma

The one you must exclude

Tumor arrives via afferent lymphatics into the subcapsular sinus. Cohesive clusters in a sinus are the earliest metastatic deposit.

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma

The mimic

Kept separate because its follicles look nothing like a hyperplastic follicle: small atretic follicles composed mainly of dendritic cells, onion-skinning of concentric mantle lymphocytes, and a penetrating vessel (“lollipop sign”). Full section below.

Q: A lymph node from a 30-year-old shows expansion of the sinuses by large pale cells. What is the single most important question to answer, and how do you answer it?

Answer

The question is: are these histiocytes, or are they metastatic tumor cells? The sinus is where metastases land first, so a sinus-pattern process must always be interrogated for carcinoma or melanoma. Answer it with immunohistochemistry: histiocytes are CD68 positive and keratin negative; carcinoma is keratin positive; melanoma is S100 and SOX10 positive. Note the trap — Rosai–Dorfman histiocytes are also S100 positive, so S100 alone cannot separate them from melanoma; you need the full panel plus the morphology (emperipolesis). Anaplastic large cell lymphoma is the third sinusoidal mimic and needs CD30.

Q: Why is the diffuse/paracortical pattern the one that most often causes serious diagnostic trouble?

Answer

Because its benign entity and its malignant entity look genuinely alike. Infectious mononucleosis produces paracortical hyperplasia with numerous immunoblasts, some of which become so large and nucleolated that they are called Reed–Sternberg-like cells. Classic Hodgkin lymphoma — particularly the mixed cellularity subtype — also expands the interfollicular zone and contains scattered large cells in a mixed background. The resolution is immunophenotypic: the large cells of mononucleosis retain their B-cell program (CD20 and OCT2 positive), while Reed–Sternberg cells have lost it (CD20 and OCT2 negative).

Topic 6 — Named entity
Highest yield in the lecture

Diffuse / paracortical pattern

Infectious mononucleosis — and separating it from Hodgkin lymphoma

Infectious mononucleosis is Epstein–Barr virus infection of B cells with a massive reactive T-cell response. It is benign and self-limited — and it is the single most dangerous benign mimic of Hodgkin lymphoma, because it contains large atypical cells that can be CD30 positive with weak PAX5, exactly the Hodgkin profile.

Clinical

🔑 The triad, present in more than 50% of patients
  • Fever
  • Pharyngitis
  • Cervical or generalized lymphadenopathy

Add splenomegaly and profound fatigue and the clinical picture is essentially made. Blood count classically shows lymphocytosis with more than 10% atypical lymphocytes.

🧩 Duncan syndrome — the genetic predisposition to remember

Duncan syndrome (X-linked lymphoproliferative disease) is caused by an X-linked mutation in the SAP gene. Affected boys cannot control Epstein–Barr virus infection, and a first exposure that would be trivial in anyone else can cause fulminant infectious mononucleosis, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, or lymphoma. This is the link between this section and the HLH section below.

Pathology

  • Gross: moderately enlarged lymph node.
  • Architecture: distorted but intact or only partially effaced — this is the key architectural point that separates it from lymphoma.
  • Three changes together: paracortical hyperplasia (the most prominent), follicular hyperplasia, and sinus histiocytosis.
  • The paracortex contains a polymorphous mixture: small and larger lymphocytes, immunoblasts (activated B and T cells), histiocytes, plasma cells.
  • Patchy pale areas in the expanded paracortex give the classic “mottled” low-power appearance.
  • Focal necrosis can be present.
  • Occasional very large mono- or polynucleated cells with prominent nucleoli — the Reed–Sternberg-like cells.

Q: A 16-year-old has an excised cervical node showing an expanded, mottled paracortex containing scattered very large binucleate cells with prominent nucleoli. Immunostains show these cells are CD30 positive, PAX5 positive at normal intensity, CD20 strongly positive, OCT2 positive, and Epstein–Barr virus positive. Diagnosis?

Answer

Infectious mononucleosis — not Hodgkin lymphoma. CD30 positivity and Epstein–Barr virus positivity are both compatible with either and are therefore uninformative here. What settles it is the retained B-cell program: strong CD20, positive OCT2 and normal-intensity PAX5. In classic Hodgkin lymphoma the Reed–Sternberg cells would be CD20 negative, OCT2 negative, and PAX5 weak relative to background B cells. The intact-to-partially-effaced architecture with follicular hyperplasia supports the reactive interpretation.

Q: Which single immunohistochemical stain, if you could order only one, best separates the large cells of infectious mononucleosis from Reed–Sternberg cells, and why is CD30 the wrong choice?

Answer

CD20 (or equivalently OCT2). CD30 is an activation marker, not a lineage or malignancy marker — it is expressed by normal activated lymphocytes, so the immunoblasts of infectious mononucleosis are frequently CD30 positive. Using CD30 to call Hodgkin lymphoma in a mononucleosis node is one of the classic ways to make a catastrophic error. CD20 asks the question that actually differs between the two: is the B-cell program intact?

Topic 7 — Named entity

Histiocytic / necrotizing pattern

Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease (histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis)

A benign, self-limited necrotizing lymphadenitis that Dr. Sirotnikov calls “a scary differential” — because at high power it can look like a high-grade lymphoma or even histiocytic sarcoma. The two facts that save you are the absence of neutrophils and the myeloperoxidase-positive, crescent-shaped histiocytes.

🔑 The clinical profile that appears in question stems
  • Young adults; women more often than men; more common in Asian populations — the stem is usually a young Asian woman.
  • Cervical lymph nodes, typically unilateral, with mild enlargement and often tenderness.
  • Fever, weight loss, night sweats, skin rash, sore throat, arthromyalgias. Note that the B-symptom-like picture is exactly what makes lymphoma the clinical worry.
  • Self-limited with spontaneous remission — which is why the diagnosis matters so much: the alternative diagnoses lead to chemotherapy.
  • Does not respond to antibiotics — a common feature of the presenting history.

The complete clinical and pathologic checklist

Pathology

  • Paracortical process with partially preserved architecture.
  • Necrosis and single-cell apoptosis are prominent, with abundant nuclear debris. The necrosis is eosinophilic — hence the low-power pink zones against blue preserved node.
  • Crescent-shaped (C-shaped) histiocyte nuclei — the morphologic signature. Note they are described as non-phagocytic histiocytes.
  • Plasmacytoid dendritic cells, small lymphocytes and numerous immunoblasts in some cases.
  • ABSENCE OF NEUTROPHILS (and of eosinophils). This is the finding that separates it from infectious/suppurative causes such as cat-scratch disease, which is full of neutrophils.
  • Immunophenotype: histiocytes are CD68 positive AND myeloperoxidase positive; CD8-positive T cells predominate; very few B cells.
  • Clonality studies (T-cell or B-cell gene rearrangement) show no monoclonality — used when there is genuine doubt.

  • Proliferative phase (about 5% of cases): proliferation of plasmacytoid dendritic cells, histiocytes and lymphocytes with minimal necrosis; single-cell apoptosis present.
  • Necrotizing phase — the one to be able to recognize: focal or extensive eosinophilic necrosis with numerous C-shaped histiocytes.
  • Xanthomatous phase (about one third): lipid-laden macrophages, appearing first at the margins of the lesion.

Q: A 26-year-old woman of Korean descent has a tender left cervical node, fever and night sweats that did not respond to antibiotics. The node shows patchy eosinophilic necrosis with abundant nuclear debris, crescent-shaped histiocytes, and no neutrophils. What is the diagnosis, what is the one serology you must send, and what is the treatment?

Answer

Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease (histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis). The absence of neutrophils despite necrosis is the discriminating morphologic finding. The serology to send is for systemic lupus erythematosus — lupus lymphadenitis is the closest histologic mimic, and some patients later develop lupus. Treatment is supportive only: the disease is self-limited with spontaneous remission. Confirmatory immunohistochemistry: histiocytes CD68 positive and myeloperoxidase positive, with predominant CD8 T cells and very few B cells.

Q: Why is myeloperoxidase a surprising but useful stain in a lymph node?

Answer

Myeloperoxidase is normally thought of as a granulocyte/myeloid marker — it is what you reach for in acute myeloid leukemia. In the lymph node it is useful because the histiocytes of Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease co-express MPO along with CD68. That combination, in the right morphologic and clinical setting, is what allowed Case 1 in this lecture to be signed out as Kikuchi rather than lupus.

Topic 8 — Named entity

Histiocytic pattern — a systemic emergency

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)

Unlike everything else in this lecture, HLH is not primarily a lymph node diagnosis — it is a systemic inflammatory catastrophe in which the pathologist supplies one criterion out of eight. It belongs here because the bone marrow, spleen or lymph node finding (hemophagocytic histiocytes) is what a pathologist is asked to look for, and because it links back to Epstein–Barr virus and forward to aggressive lymphomas.

🔑 What it is

A sudden-onset systemic inflammatory response syndrome secondary to macrophage activation. Macrophages become uncontrolled, consume blood cells, and flood the body with cytokines. Diagnosis requires 5 out of 8 criteria.

Primary (familial) HLH

Occurs in infants, caused by a variety of inherited mutations in the granule-mediated cytotoxicity pathway.

Secondary (acquired) HLH

Triggered by infection — particularly Epstein–Barr virus and cytomegalovirus. Also seen in the setting of aggressive lymphomas and autoimmune disease (where it is called macrophage activation syndrome).

Dr. Sirotnikov is explicit: hemophagocytic histiocytes are seen in many settings and are not specific on their own. Q: A 4-month-old presents with fever, hepatosplenomegaly and pancytopenia. Ferritin is 12,000 ng/mL, triglycerides are elevated and fibrinogen is low. What is the diagnosis, what will the marrow show, and what should be sought as a trigger?

Answer

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. The marrow will show hemophagocytic histiocytes containing engulfed red cells, nucleated red cells and cellular debris. Topic 9 — Named entity

Follicular pattern, but not hyperplasia

Castleman disease

Uncommon — roughly 6,500–7,700 new cases per year in the United States — and worth learning mainly for two buzzwords and one classification split. It is a disease of dendritic cells, which explains why its follicles look small, pale and atretic rather than hyperplastic.

Classification — two axes

Axis 1: how many nodes?

  • Unicentric: a single node or a group of closely related nodes. Usually curable by excision.
  • Multicentric (20–25%): multiple groups of nodes, systemic disease. Subdivided into HHV-8-associated (human herpesvirus 8, particularly in HIV), idiopathic multicentric, and syndrome-associated — POEMS and TAFRO.

Axis 2: what does it look like?

  • Hyaline vascular variant — a benign neoplasm derived from lymph node stromal cells. This is the one with the classic morphology below.
  • Plasma cell variant — sheets of plasma cells between follicles; more often associated with the systemic/multicentric presentations.

Multicentric Castleman disease — the three-way split

🔑 The morphologic buzzwords — hyaline vascular variant
  • Hyalinization: very dense pink (hyalinized) material around the vessels — the feature the variant is named for.
  • Atretic follicles: unlike the large hyperplastic germinal centers seen earlier, these follicles are small and composed mainly of pale dendritic cells — a normal constituent of germinal centers that here becomes the dominant population.
  • “Lollipop sign”: a penetrating blood vessel running into the atretic follicle — the vessel is the stick, the follicle is the candy.
  • Onion-skinning: mantle-zone lymphocytes arranged in concentric rings around the atretic germinal center.
  • “Twinning”: two germinal centers within one follicle.

  • Complication to know: the hyaline vascular variant is prone to developing follicular dendritic cell sarcoma — which follows logically once you accept that this is a disease of dendritic cells.

Q: A lymph node contains numerous small follicles with regressed germinal centers made largely of pale dendritic cells, surrounded by concentric rings of mantle lymphocytes, with hyalinized vessels running into them. What is the diagnosis, and what malignancy is this variant at risk of developing?

Answer

Hyaline vascular Castleman disease. Three buzzwords are present: atretic follicles composed of dendritic cells, onion-skinning of the mantle, and the lollipop sign (penetrating hyalinized vessel). Because the underlying lesion is a benign neoplasm of lymph node stromal / dendritic cells, the variant is prone to developing follicular dendritic cell sarcoma. Also know the other axis: unicentric versus multicentric, and that multicentric disease may be HHV-8-associated (especially in HIV), idiopathic, or syndrome-associated (POEMS, TAFRO).

Topic 10 — Named entity

Sinus pattern

Rosai–Dorfman disease

Historically called sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy, which is a perfect description of what it looks like: the same compartment as ordinary reactive sinus histiocytosis, but expanded to the point of producing bulky nodes. It is now recognized as a histiocytic neoplasm, with MAP kinase pathway mutations in a third of cases.

🔑 Distribution and associations
  • Lymph node involvement in 50–60% of patients.
  • Extranodal sites in 30–40% — including skin and central nervous system, which is why the case in this lecture recurred in the spinal cord.
  • 10% have an associated immunologic disorder.
  • Can be associated with IgG4-related disease, can be familial, and can arise with or after other histiocytic diseases, lymphoma or leukemia. The main takeaway is simply that it has many presentations.
  • Gross: large, matted nodes with fibrosis of the capsule and pericapsular tissue.

The full classification (the “R group” histiocytoses) — reference only; the takeaway is the breadth of presentations

Morphology and phenotype

  • Sinuses are expanded by a pale-looking population that is obvious at low power.
  • The cells are neoplastic histiocytes with round to oval nuclei, prominent nucleoli and ample cytoplasm.
  • EMPERIPOLESIS — the buzzword. Intact lymphocytes and plasma cells engulfed within the histiocyte cytoplasm, passing through rather than being digested.
  • Numerous plasma cells in the background.
Phenotype S100 +CD68 +CD163 +cyclin D1 +OCT2 +CD1a −CD207 (langerin) −
🧠 S100 as its own negative control — the elegant bit

Dr. Sirotnikov calls this “a helpful thing” and it is worth internalizing. S100 stains the nucleus and cytoplasm of the neoplastic histiocytes but is negative in the lymphocytes and plasma cells they have engulfed. The result is that the engulfed cells appear as clear, unstained holes inside a brown histiocyte — the stain highlights emperipolesis while simultaneously providing a built-in internal negative control. When emperipolesis is hard to see on H&E, the S100 makes it obvious.

⚠️ CD1a and CD207 negativity is what excludes Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Langerhans cell histiocytosis is also S100 positive, so S100 alone cannot separate the two. Langerhans cells are CD1a positive and CD207 (langerin) positive; Rosai–Dorfman histiocytes are negative for both. Add that Langerhans cell histiocytosis shows grooved “coffee-bean” nuclei and eosinophils rather than emperipolesis.

Genetics and treatment

  • MAP kinase / ERK pathway alterations in about one third of cases — MAP2K1, NRAS, KRAS. This is shared with the other histiocytic neoplasms and is what makes the disease targetable.
  • Spontaneous regression occurs and observation is often appropriate.
  • For relapsed or recurrent disease: combinations of steroids, chemotherapy and radiation, or targeted therapy with MEK inhibitors (which follows directly from the MAP kinase pathway biology).

Q: A lymph node shows markedly expanded sinuses filled with large histiocytes containing intact lymphocytes within their cytoplasm. The histiocytes are S100 positive, CD68 positive, CD1a negative and CD207 negative. Name the finding, the diagnosis, and the two entities the immunophenotype excludes.

Answer

The finding is emperipolesis; the diagnosis is Rosai–Dorfman disease (sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy). The phenotype excludes Langerhans cell histiocytosis (which would be CD1a and CD207/langerin positive) and, in combination with CD68 positivity and the morphology, metastatic melanoma (also S100 positive but SOX10 positive, CD68 negative, and without emperipolesis). Remember also that S100 is negative in the engulfed cells, which is what makes the emperipolesis so conspicuous.

Q: Why does a MEK inhibitor make biological sense in refractory Rosai–Dorfman disease?

Answer

Because about one third of cases carry activating alterations in the MAP kinase / ERK pathway — MAP2K1, NRAS or KRAS. MEK sits directly downstream in that pathway, so MEK inhibition blocks the driving signal. This is the same logic that links the histiocytic neoplasms as a family, and the same logic that makes BRAF inhibitors effective in the BRAF-mutated histiocytoses and in hairy cell leukemia.

Q: In Case 3, which single finding most strongly argues against classic Hodgkin lymphoma, and which finding is the most misleading?

Answer

The strongest argument against Hodgkin lymphoma is the strong CD20 positivity of the large cells — the B-cell program is retained, which Reed–Sternberg cells cannot do. (The preserved architecture with normal CD3/CD20 zoning supports it.) The most misleading finding is CD30 positivity: CD30 is an activation marker expressed by normal immunoblasts, so it is entirely expected in mononucleosis. Epstein–Barr virus positivity is equally unhelpful, since classic Hodgkin lymphoma is also frequently EBV positive.

Q: All three cases in this lecture were solved the same way. State the method in four steps.

Answer

(1) Low power first — blue versus pink, and ask whether the architecture is preserved, distorted or effaced. (2) Name the pattern — follicular, paracortical/interfollicular, diffuse, sinus, or histiocytic/necrotizing — which yields a differential of two or three entities. (3) Go to high power for the one discriminating morphologic feature — presence or absence of neutrophils, emperipolesis, the exact character of the large cells. (4) Confirm with a short, targeted immunostain panel, and correlate with the clinical history, which in every one of these cases carried real diagnostic weight.

Q: Rank these three stains by how much they contribute to distinguishing infectious mononucleosis from classic Hodgkin lymphoma: CD30, EBER, CD20.

Answer

CD20 is decisive; CD30 and EBER contribute essentially nothing. CD20 is positive in mononucleosis immunoblasts and negative in Reed–Sternberg cells, so it directly answers the question. CD30 is an activation marker positive in both. EBER is positive in mononucleosis by definition and in a large fraction of classic Hodgkin lymphomas, so it also fails to discriminate. The general principle: ask which stain differs between the two options, not which stain is positive in the disease you are considering.

Glossary & abbreviations

Anatomic and morphologic terms

Cortex (superficial cortex)
The outer zone of a lymph node, containing the follicles. The B-cell zone.
Paracortex
Also called the interfollicular or deep cortical zone. The space between and beneath follicles. The T-cell zone, also containing histiocytes, dendritic cells, plasma cells and immunoblasts.
Medulla
The inner zone toward the hilum, containing medullary cords and sinuses and the draining vessels.
Subcapsular sinus
The space immediately beneath the capsule into which afferent lymph empties. Where metastatic tumor lands first.
Primary follicle
A follicle with no germinal center — naive B cells that have not met antigen.
Secondary follicle
A follicle containing a reactive germinal center.
Germinal center
The reaction center within a secondary follicle where B cells undergo somatic hypermutation and selection.
Centroblast
The large activated germinal center B cell of the dark zone — round nucleus, open chromatin, prominent nucleoli, mitotically active.
Centrocyte
The smaller cleaved germinal center B cell of the light zone, undergoing affinity selection.
Tingible body macrophage
A macrophage containing engulfed apoptotic bodies. Produces the starry sky look; strongly favors a reactive germinal center.
Mantle zone
The cuff of small naive B cells around a germinal center. Attenuated in follicular lymphoma.
Marginal zone
The paler outer rim of memory B cells beyond the mantle. Normal counterpart of marginal zone lymphoma.
Polarization
The presence of a distinct dark zone at one pole and light zone at the other within a germinal center. A reactive feature.
Effaced architecture
The normal zonal floor plan of the node has been obliterated by the process. Favors lymphoma.
Immunoblast
A large activated lymphocyte (B or T) with prominent nucleoli. Normal in the paracortex; numerous in infectious mononucleosis.
Histiocyte
A tissue macrophage.
Emperipolesis
Intact, viable lymphocytes or plasma cells within a histiocyte’s cytoplasm, passing through undigested. The hallmark of Rosai–Dorfman disease.
Hemophagocytosis
Histiocytes containing engulfed and degenerating blood cells and debris. Seen in hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis.
Onion-skinning
Concentric rings of mantle lymphocytes around an atretic germinal center. Castleman disease.
Lollipop sign
A penetrating hyalinized blood vessel entering an atretic follicle. Castleman disease.
Twinning
Two germinal centers within a single follicle. Castleman disease.
Asteroid body
A star-shaped inclusion within a giant cell in a granuloma. Classically sarcoidosis.
Suppurative (stellate) granuloma
A star-shaped granuloma with a neutrophil-rich center. Classically cat-scratch disease.
Karyorrhectic debris
Fragmented nuclear material from dying cells. Abundant in Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease.
Dermatopathic lymphadenopathy
Reactive paracortical expansion by histiocytes and dendritic cells with pigment-laden macrophages, in a node draining chronically inflamed skin.
Touch preparation (touch imprint)
An unstained slide touched or gently smeared against fresh cut tissue. Works in lymph nodes because lymphocytes are discohesive.
Reverse nodularity
The CD3 staining pattern of a normal node — T cells fill the paracortex, leaving the B-cell follicles as pale holes.
Reed–Sternberg cell
The binucleate neoplastic cell of classic Hodgkin lymphoma, with cherry-red macronucleoli and a perinuclear halo. Its look-alike in mononucleosis is a Reed–Sternberg-like cell, which retains B-cell markers.

1 · NORMAL NODE

Three zones

  • Cortex = B cells — primary follicles (no germinal center), secondary follicles (with germinal center)
  • Paracortex / interfollicular = T cells + histiocytes, dendritic cells, plasma cells, immunoblasts, high endothelial venules
  • Medulla = cords + sinuses + draining vessels
  • Capsule present = it really is a lymph node
  • Subcapsular sinus = where metastases land first
  • Primary + secondary follicles together is normal — lobules drain different fields

Germinal center anatomy

  • Dark zone = centroblasts (large, open chromatin, nucleoli, mitoses)
  • Light zone = centrocytes (small, cleaved)
  • Tingible body macrophages = eaten apoptotic debris = reactive
  • Mantle zone then marginal zone outside

Reactive GC vs follicular lymphoma

  • Polarized vs not polarized
  • TBM present vs absent
  • Mantle intact vs attenuated
  • Follicles varied & separated vs monotonous & back-to-back
  • BCL2 NEGATIVE vs BCL2 POSITIVE — the decisive stain
  • Why: normal GC B cells switch BCL2 off so failed clones can die; t(14;18) forces it back on

Ki-67 quick ranks

  • Reactive GC: brisk, polarized (higher in dark zone)
  • Mantle cell & marginal zone: low
  • Burkitt: >95%
2 · DEVELOPMENT

Antigen-independent vs dependent

  • Independent (fetal/childhood): B in bone marrow, T in thymus → precursor / lymphoblastic neoplasms
  • Dependent (lymph node): germinal center reaction → mature / peripheral lymphomas
  • “-oma” = mass: T-lymphoblastic lymphoma = mediastinal (thymic) mass

B cell path

  • Marrow: VDJ recombination builds heavy chain (V+D+J); light chain uses V+J with kappa or lambda
  • Node: germinal center reaction, T-cell assisted → class switch recombination + somatic hypermutation
  • Exit as plasma cell (returns to marrow, sits by vessels, secretes Ig) or memory B cell
  • Alternative: extrafollicular route → short-lived plasmablasts
  • DNA breaking during these steps = why translocations happen in GC B cells

T cell path

  • Marrow stem cell → thymus → TCR gene rearrangement
  • Positive selection: weakly recognize self-MHC → survive; no recognition → apoptosis
  • Negative selection: bind self too strongly → apoptosis
  • Then commit to CD4 helper or CD8 cytotoxic
  • Thymocytes are transiently CD4+CD8+ (double positive) → explains T-lymphoblastic lymphoma phenotype
3 · APPROACH

Specimen handling

  • Fresh from OR → serially section → touch preps (lymphocytes discohesive) → save unstained slides
  • Fresh tissue needed for flow cytometry and conventional cytogenetics — both lost after formalin
  • Fixed tissue still allows IHC, FISH, PCR, NGS

Touch prep triage

  • Sheets of blasts → lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma
  • Scattered large atypical cells → Hodgkin
  • Sheets of large cells → DLBCL
  • Bizarre cohesive cells → ALCL / metastasis

Site → differential

  • Anterior cervical → mononucleosis
  • Posterior cervical → toxoplasmosis, mononucleosis, Kikuchi
  • Supraclavicular, hard & fixed → Hodgkin; metastatic carcinoma
  • Axillary + mesenteric → non-Hodgkin lymphoma
  • Mediastinal → Hodgkin lymphoma
  • Mnemonic MIAMI: Malignancy, Infection, Autoimmune, Miscellaneous, Iatrogenic

Patterns → benign vs malignant pair

  • Follicular: reactive hyperplasia vs follicular lymphoma → BCL2
  • Paracortical: dermatopathic LAD vs peripheral T-cell lymphoma / mixed cellularity Hodgkin
  • Diffuse + immunoblasts: mononucleosis vs Hodgkin / DLBCL → CD20, OCT2
  • Sinus: sinus histiocytosis vs Rosai–Dorfman / metastasis / ALCL → S100, keratin, CD30
  • Necrotizing, no neutrophils: Kikuchi vs lymphoma → CD68+MPO
  • Necrotizing, neutrophils present: cat-scratch (stellate suppurative granulomas)
  • Granulomas non-necrotizing + asteroid bodies: sarcoid
  • Granulomas necrotizing: mycobacteria / fungi → AFB + GMS
  • Necrosis in a node never excludes Hodgkin — still hunt Reed–Sternberg cells
4 · THE BIG DISCRIMINATION

Infectious mononucleosis vs classic Hodgkin

  • Triad (>50%): fever, pharyngitis, cervical/generalized LAD; add splenomegaly, >10% atypical lymphocytes
  • Duncan syndrome = X-linked SAP gene mutation → cannot control EBV
  • Histology: paracortical hyperplasia (dominant) + follicular hyperplasia + sinus histiocytosis, mottled low power, architecture intact/partially effaced
  • IM large cells: CD20 +, OCT2 +, PAX5 normal intensity
  • CHL Reed–Sternberg: CD20 −, OCT2 −, PAX5 WEAK
  • CD30 positive in BOTH (activation marker) — useless alone
  • EBV positive in BOTH — also useless alone
  • Nucleoli: IM basophilic; CHL cherry-red + perinuclear halo
5 · NAMED ENTITIES

Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease

  • Young Asian woman, unilateral cervical node, fever/night sweats/rash, no antibiotic response, self-limited
  • Necrosis + abundant nuclear debris + single-cell apoptosis, paracortical, architecture partly preserved
  • CRESCENT / C-SHAPED histiocytes; plasmacytoid dendritic cells
  • ABSENCE OF NEUTROPHILS (and eosinophils) — the key negative
  • CD68 + and MPO +; CD8 T cells predominant, very few B cells; no monoclonality
  • Phases: proliferative (5%) · necrotizing · xanthomatous (1/3)
  • Main mimic = lupus lymphadenitis — always check serology

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis

  • Macrophage activation → systemic inflammatory response; 5 of 8 criteria
  • Clinical: fever, splenomegaly, cytopenias
  • Labs: high ferritin, high triglycerides, LOW fibrinogen
  • Path: hemophagocytic histiocytes in marrow / spleen / node — not specific alone
  • Primary (infants, inherited) vs secondary (EBV, CMV, lymphoma)

Castleman disease

  • Unicentric vs multicentric (20–25%); multicentric = HHV-8-associated, idiopathic, or syndrome-associated (POEMS, TAFRO)
  • Variants: hyaline vascular (neoplasm of LN stromal cells) · plasma cell
  • Buzzwords: onion-skinning · lollipop vessel · twinning · atretic follicles of dendritic cells
  • Gross: large firm node, median 7 cm
  • Hyaline vascular variant → risk of follicular dendritic cell sarcoma

Rosai–Dorfman disease

  • = sinus histiocytosis with massive lymphadenopathy; a neoplasm
  • Node 50–60%; extranodal 30–40% (skin, CNS); 10% immunologic disorder
  • Gross: large, matted, capsular fibrosis
  • EMPERIPOLESIS = intact lymphocytes/plasma cells inside histiocytes
  • S100 + , CD68 + , CD163 + , cyclin D1 + , OCT2 +; CD1a − , CD207 − (excludes Langerhans cell histiocytosis)
  • S100 negative in engulfed cells = built-in negative control
  • MAP kinase mutations (MAP2K1, NRAS, KRAS) in 1/3 → MEK inhibitors

Hemophagocytosis vs emperipolesis

  • Hemophagocytosis: debris & degenerating cells, being destroyed → HLH
  • Emperipolesis: intact viable cells passing through → Rosai–Dorfman

The three cases

  • 28F, posterior cervical, fever/night sweats, no antibiotic response, cytopenias: necrosis + debris + C-shaped histiocytes + no neutrophils, CD68+/MPO+ → Kikuchi–Fujimoto (ruled out lupus)
  • 9F with RDD since age 1, CNS recurrences: sinus histiocytes with emperipolesis plus a separate confluent markedly atypical area → RDD progressed to histiocytic sarcoma
  • 15M, tonsillar exudate, palatal petechiae, splenomegaly, >10% atypical lymphocytes: paracortical hyperplasia, large cells CD30+ / PAX5 weak-moderate / EBV+ BUT strongly CD20+, architecture preserved → infectious mononucleosis, not Hodgkin
