Viral Hemorrhagic Fevers

Five virus families, one shared mechanism. Every agent in this lecture damages small blood vessels, cripples clotting, and ends in multi-organ failure by the same four-step pathway — so the exam-relevant work is not re-learning the syndrome each time, but learning the handful of facts that separate one virus from the next: which animal, which vector, which organ fails first.

Orientation — how to read this lecture

Start here

One mechanism, four viruses, a small list of discriminators

A viral hemorrhagic fever (VHF) is a febrile illness in which a virus damages small blood vessels and simultaneously reduces the blood’s ability to clot, with the capacity to involve multiple organ systems. The lecturer makes one structural promise at the outset and keeps it: the pathogenesis and the signs and symptoms are essentially the same for every agent in this lecture, so he explains them once, up front, and then never repeats them.

🔑 How this file is organized (and why)

Sections 1–3 are the shared blueprint — the mechanism, the symptom list, and the family tree. Sections 4–8 are the individual agents, and each one is written to answer only four questions, because those four are what actually distinguish them on an exam:

  1. What is the reservoir?A mosquito, a tick, a field mouse, or a multimammate rat — the animal is usually the single most identifying detail in a question stem.
  2. Is there a vector, and does the virus replicate in it?Dengue and Crimean–Congo have true vectors. Hantavirus and Lassa have none — you inhale or touch rodent excreta instead.
  3. Where in the world?Central/South America and the Caribbean, versus the Far East, versus Africa and southeastern Europe, versus West Africa.
  4. Is there an organ that fails out of proportion to the rest?Only one agent here has that: Hantavirus HFRS destroys kidneys. Everything else fails as a set.
🧠 The single sentence to carry into the exam

Antigen-presenting cells are disabled early, so the virus replicates unchecked; macrophages then overreact with a cytokine storm; infected phagocytes carry live virus to distant organs; and the sustained inflammation plus direct endothelial infection opens the vasculature. That is all four panels of the pathogenesis figure, and it is nearly identical to what happens in sepsis — a comparison the lecturer draws explicitly.

⚠️ What this file does not cover (deliberately, per the lecturer)
  • Yellow fever — a genuine Flaviviridae hemorrhagic fever with a high fatality rate, but its defining symptoms are hepatic, so it is taught in the gastrointestinal (GI) system block.
  • Chikungunya — a Togaviridae agent with occasional hemorrhagic manifestations, but fever plus polyarthralgia dominates, so it is taught in the musculoskeletal (MSK / MST) system block.
  • Ebola and Marburg (Filoviridae) — named in this lecture’s family table but deferred to Part 2.
  • Hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS), the New World hantavirus disease — deferred to the respiratory system block. Only the Old World renal disease is in scope here.

Q: A question stem describes a returning traveler with fever, headache, severe bone and joint pain, a faint rash, and a falling platelet count. Before you know anything else, what two facts should you be hunting for in the stem?

Answer

Where they travelled and what they were exposed to — a mosquito bite, a tick bite, a rodent-infested building, or a sick contact. The syndrome itself is nearly identical across all viral hemorrhagic fevers, so the geography plus the exposure is what names the virus. The lecturer’s own version of this: a patient in northern Wisconsin who has not left the state does not get dengue on the differential.

LO 1 — Pathogenesis of hemorrhagic fever–causing viruses

Mechanism

Four panels: immunosuppression → cytokine storm → dissemination → vascular collapse

This one figure is the whole mechanistic content of the lecture. Learn it once and it applies to dengue, hantavirus, Crimean–Congo and Lassa without modification. Read it as a sequence, not four independent facts — each panel is caused by the one before it.

Slide 2 — General pathogenesis of the viral hemorrhagic fever viruses. Panels A→B→C→D are a causal chain, not a list.

Panel A — the virus disables the immune system before it does anything else

Virus crosses a mucosal barrier and is picked up by dendritic cells and macrophages. These antigen-presenting cells (APCs) then present an immature phenotype, defined by two deficits:

  • Very low expression of co-stimulatory molecules — the second signal a T cell needs to be activated.
  • Very low production of type I interferon (IFN) — the cytokine family that would normally shut down viral replication in neighbouring cells.

The consequence is poor natural killer (NK) cell activity and poor T cell activation — a poor cytotoxic response and a delayed adaptive response. That is a state of functional immunosuppression, and under it the virus faces almost no restraint: viral replication control is lost and viral burden climbs.

🧠 Why panel A has to come first

Every downstream disaster is proportional to viral burden. The virus does not cause hemorrhage by being cytopathic in some clever way — it causes hemorrhage by reaching a burden the innate system was never allowed to cap, and then provoking a response scaled to that burden. Blunting the interferon response is the founding event.

Panel B — the cytokine storm

Infected monocytes and macrophages now release very large quantities of pro-inflammatory mediators. The lecturer uses the term that became household vocabulary during COVID-19: a cytokine storm. These mediators are meant to be defensive; at storm concentrations they do four destructive things instead:

Endothelial activation

Vessel wall

The endothelium switches to an activated, adhesive, permeable phenotype — the first step toward the leak in panel D.

Platelet activation

Clotting

Platelets are consumed rather than held in reserve, which is one half of why a hemorrhagic fever patient bleeds.

Coagulation cascade activation

Clotting

Clotting factors are consumed in widespread microthrombosis — the substrate for disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

Bystander activation & T cell apoptosis

Immunity

T cells that were never specific for the virus are activated and then killed by apoptosis, deepening the immunosuppression from panel A.

⚠️ The counter-intuitive part

Panel B worsens panel A. The inflammatory response does not rescue the immunosuppression — the bystander activation and apoptosis of T cells destroys T cells that were not even virus-specific. The patient ends up simultaneously over-inflamed and under-defended.

Panel C — infected phagocytes become the delivery system

Macrophages and other APCs take up the virus but do not kill it, for the reasons in panel A. They then migrate normally — to lymph nodes and on to distant organs — carrying still-infectious virus with them. This is what mediates systemic dissemination. 🩺 Two independent reasons the patient bleeds

It is worth separating them, because a question can test either. (1) Consumption — panel B burns through platelets and clotting factors. (2) Failure of production — panel C’s liver injury stops new clotting factors being made. Add the leaky vessel of panel D and you have all three legs of the bleeding.

Panel D — sustained inflammation plus endothelial infection = vascular collapse

If the pro-inflammatory state is sustained and the virus continues to infect endothelial cells directly, the vessel wall stops functioning as a barrier or as a regulator of tone:

  • Increased vascular permeability → vascular leakage and organ effusion
  • Abnormal vascular regulation — vasodilatation and hypotension
  • Platelet and clotting factor consumption → disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
  • Hemorrhage — internal and external
  • Multi-organ failure and death
💡 The comparison the lecturer wants you to make

“If a lot of this sounds somewhat familiar” — it is because panels B and D are sepsis. Dysregulated host response, endothelial activation, capillary leak, DIC, distributive shock, multi-organ failure. The viral hemorrhagic fevers are a viral route into the same final common pathway you already learned. Use that: you do not need a new mental model, only a new trigger.

Q: Why does the immunosuppression in panel A make the later cytokine storm worse rather than milder?

Answer

Because the mediator surge in panel B is a response to viral burden, and panel A is precisely what let the viral burden climb unchecked. Low type I interferon and low co-stimulatory molecule expression mean poor NK cell and T cell activity, so there is no early cytotoxic control of replication. Macrophages then encounter an enormous antigen load and respond with an enormous mediator release. The weak specific response and the excessive non-specific response are two faces of one failure.

Q: A patient with a viral hemorrhagic fever has AST and ALT in the thousands, a prolonged prothrombin time, hypotension and hyponatremia. Map each finding onto the pathogenesis figure.

Answer

All four are panel C — systemic dissemination by infected phagocytes. Raised AST/ALT and prolonged clotting times are liver infection (hepatocyte injury plus decreased synthesis of clotting factors). Hypotension and sodium loss are adrenal infection with decreased steroid-synthesizing enzymes, producing a hypovolemic state. Note that the hypotension here has a second, later contributor in panel D (vasodilatation from abnormal vascular regulation).

LO 2 — Typical clinical manifestation of a viral hemorrhagic fever

Clinical syndrome

One symptom list, taught once, that applies to all of them

The lecturer gives the full list at the start explicitly so he does not have to repeat it for each virus. Individual agents emphasize different parts of it, but this is the blueprint. Notice that it is a staged illness: a non-specific febrile prodrome first, coagulation failure second, shock and organ failure third.

Slide 3 — The complete signs and symptoms list for the viral hemorrhagic fevers.

STAGE 1

Febrile prodrome

Fever of at least 100.4 °F (38 °C), typically climbing to 101–103 °F. With it: general weakness, headache, bone pain, joint pain, myalgia, dizziness.

STAGE 1

Non-specific systemic features

Vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, chest pain, rash. Also photophobia, hyperesthesia, periorbital edema, retro-orbital pain, facial flushing, conjunctival redness.

STAGE 2

Coagulation disorders

Petechiae and bleeding under the skin; bleeding from gums, mouth, eyes and ears; bleeding into internal organs. Driven by vascular damage largely caused by the immune reaction, not by the virus lysing vessels directly.

STAGE 3

Shock and organ failure

Low blood pressure → shock. Delirium, seizures, coma. Kidney failure, liver failure, proteinuria. Endpoint is multi-organ failure.

🔑 The line the lecturer repeats twice

The bleeding is caused “a lot by the reaction of the immune system.” This is not a rhetorical flourish — it is the therapeutic principle. There is no antiviral for any agent in this lecture, so every intervention is aimed at supporting the patient through their own inflammatory response: fluids, blood pressure support, oxygenation, and correcting what the coagulopathy has consumed.

⚠️ The one prescribing rule that spans the whole lecture

Use acetaminophen (paracetamol) for fever and pain. Do not use NSAIDs — non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen, naproxen or aspirin. NSAIDs inhibit platelet function and increase bleeding risk, and the patient already has a major bleeding problem in skin, mucosa and internal organs. The lecturer states this rule for dengue and repeats it verbatim for Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever — treat it as a class rule for every viral hemorrhagic fever.

See this in a stem → think this Fever + retro-orbital pain + severe bone pain → dengue Fever + thrombocytopenia + acute renal failure → hantavirus HFRS Fever + petechiae + tick bite in Turkey or the Balkans → Crimean–Congo Fever + West Africa + new sensorineural hearing loss → Lassa Cytokine storm + DIC + capillary leak → the shared panel B/D endpoint

Q: A patient in the second week of a viral hemorrhagic fever develops bleeding gums, petechiae, and a prolonged prothrombin time with a falling platelet count and a falling fibrinogen. Name the process and its two upstream drivers.

Answer

Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Upstream driver one is the cytokine storm (panel B), which activates the coagulation cascade and platelets so both are consumed in widespread microthrombosis. Upstream driver two is hepatic infection (panel C), which decreases synthesis of clotting factors. The falling fibrinogen with a falling platelet count is the consumption signature; the raised transaminases, if present, point to the hepatic contribution.

LO 3 — The virus families that cause hemorrhagic fever

Taxonomy

Five families, and the four properties they all share

The hemorrhagic fever agents are distributed across five virus families. Not every member of these families causes hemorrhagic disease — the Flaviviridae in particular contain a long list of encephalitis viruses — but every hemorrhagic fever agent you will be asked about lives in one of these five.

🔑 Four properties true of every family in this list
  1. All are enveloped.Which is also why they are fragile outside a host — envelopes are lipid, and lipid does not survive drying, detergent or heat.
  2. All are RNA viruses.Specifically single-stranded RNA (ssRNA). Segmentation varies: Bunyavirales have three segments, Arenaviridae have two, Flaviviridae are non-segmented.
  3. All have an animal or insect reservoir.None of them is a purely human pathogen. There is always something else maintaining the virus in nature.
  4. Humans acquire them one of two ways.Either by direct contact with an infected animal (or its excreta), or by transmission from a vector such as a mosquito or tick. Once a human is infected, human-to-human spread requires contact with blood or body fluids.

🩺 Reading the table as a decision tree

The four in-depth agents split cleanly on one question: is there an arthropod vector?

🦟

Vector-borne — an arthropod bite

The virus replicates inside the arthropod, which is what makes it a true vector rather than a passive needle.

  • Dengue — Aedes mosquito. Reservoir is humans themselves.
  • Crimean–Congo — Hyalomma tick. Reservoir is livestock and birds, which stay well.
🐁

Rodent-borne — no vector at all

You acquire it by inhaling or touching rodent urine, feces or saliva. There is no insect in the story.

  • Hantavirus (HFRS) — field mice, rats, voles. Aerosol is the classic route.
  • Lassa — Mastomys natalensis, the multimammate rat. Contact, aerosol, or eating the rodent.

Q: Why is a mosquito called a vector for dengue rather than simply a mechanical carrier?

Answer

Because the virus actually replicates inside the mosquito. As the lecturer puts it, that replication is “what makes the mosquito the vector — otherwise the chance of transmission would be almost nil.” The virus infects the midgut, amplifies, and travels to the salivary glands over roughly 8–10 days (the extrinsic incubation period), after which that single mosquito can infect several humans. Mechanical carriage of the tiny volume on mouthparts would not deliver an infectious dose.

LO 4 — Flaviviridae family properties

Family: Flaviviridae

“Flavus” means yellow — a large, fragile, arthropod-borne family

The family is named for the first virus found in it, yellow fever virus — Latin flavus, yellow. It contains more than 70 distinct types, most of which cause something other than hemorrhagic fever, which is exactly why the family matters as an organizing idea rather than as a disease.

🧠 Extreme environmental lability — and what it rules out clinically

Flaviviruses are extremely unstable in the environment. They are labile to heat, ultraviolet (UV) light, disinfectants, acid pH and even alkaline pH. They are stable in only two places: inside their host and inside their vector.

Two clinical consequences follow directly, and both are testable:

  • They are not fomite-transmitted. You do not pick dengue up from a doorknob, a bench or shared equipment.
  • They do not persist in aerosols. Airborne transmission over any meaningful distance or time does not occur.

They do grow in a wide range of hosts — mosquitoes especially, some ticks, and a variety of vertebrate animals that may act as carriers.

⚠️ Yellow fever and chikungunya — where they actually get taught

Yellow fever is a Flaviviridae hemorrhagic fever with a very high fatality rate, but because its central symptoms are hepatic, the lecturer defers it to the GI system block. Remember one fact about it here, because it comes back in the dengue section: the yellow fever vaccine is a live attenuated virus vaccine, and that same attenuated virus is the backbone of the dengue vaccine.

Chikungunya is a Togaviridae agent, not a flavivirus. It can produce hemorrhagic manifestations, but fever plus polyarthralgia so dominates the picture that it is taught in the MSK / MST block. The name is worth recognizing because it appears every year in reports about mosquito range moving north.

Q: A hospital asks whether a dengue patient needs airborne precautions and terminal fomite disinfection of their room. What do you tell them, and on what virologic basis?

Answer

No airborne precautions are needed on the basis of the virus. Flaviviruses are enveloped and extremely labile in the environment — sensitive to heat, UV light, disinfectants, acid and alkaline pH — and are stable only inside their host or their vector. They therefore do not survive on fomites and do not persist in aerosols. The residual risk is blood and body-fluid exposure (dengue can be transmitted through contaminated blood products, though at low risk), so standard blood-borne precautions are the appropriate answer.

LO 5 — Dengue: epidemiology, transmission, clinical stages, diagnosis, treatment, prevention

Flaviviridae · Agent 1

Dengue — “breakbone fever”

Dengue is the largest agent in this lecture by case volume: roughly 400 million infections and 40,000 deaths worldwide every year. It is also the one with a genuine United States footprint — not just imported cases, but locally acquired ones — and the only one in Part 1 with a licensed vaccine.

🩺 United States distribution — imported versus locally acquired
  • Most United States cases are in Puerto Rico, not the continental United States.
  • Within the continental United States, cases cluster in Florida, California, Texas and New York.
  • Many of these are travellers returning from highly endemic areas — and note that a New York case may simply reflect travel to Puerto Rico.
  • But a subset are locally acquired: cases in Hawaii, Florida and Texas in patients with no travel history and no known contact with anyone who had travelled.
  • Timing matters diagnostically: locally acquired United States cases appear in late summer and fall, when mosquito populations peak.

Reservoir — the answer is people

Non-human primates, some bats and some domestic animals can carry dengue virus. But they typically are not infected in a meaningful sense — they do not develop symptoms and they are not efficient sources. The outline states it flatly: an animal reservoir has not been identified in the Western Hemisphere.

🧠 Why “humans are the reservoir” changes how you think about dengue

Humans are the primary infectious source and the primary transmission element to other humans. The cycle is human → mosquito → human, with no animal amplifier in between — unlike Crimean–Congo (livestock reservoir) or hantavirus and Lassa (rodent reservoirs). Two consequences:

  • You are unlikely to acquire dengue from handling animals, even animals that carry it.
  • Because ~76% of infections are asymptomatic but still transmissible, the human reservoir is largely invisible — which is why vector control, not case isolation, is the prevention strategy.

Slide 9 — The dengue transmission cycle. Human→mosquito→human, with an incubation period on each side.

🦟

Extrinsic incubation — in the mosquito

Usually 8–10 days. After the blood meal the virus infects the midgut, replicates, and travels to the salivary glands. Only then is the mosquito infectious — and it then remains so, able to infect several humans.

🤵

Intrinsic incubation — in the human

Usually 4–7 days from bite to symptom onset. Short enough that a returning traveller is typically symptomatic within the first week home, which is a useful anchor when a stem gives you travel dates.

The vector — two Aedes mosquitoes with different habits

Both vectors are female Aedes mosquitoes (only females take blood meals). They are not interchangeable, and the difference between them is the basis of the geographic-spread question.

⚠️ The climate-change prediction the lecturer wants you to notice

Global warming models built specifically for these two mosquitoes predict an expanding range for Aedes aegypti as average temperature rises — its dark band on the map creeping up into New England. The clinical point is not just dengue: Aedes aegypti transmits yellow fever, chikungunya and Zika as well, so an expanding aegypti range brings the whole set of diseases with it. And because aegypti is the mosquito that actually prefers to bite humans, its expansion matters far more than albopictus already being present.

🩺 Transmission routes other than a mosquito bite
  • Vertical transmission — mother to fetus. Documented and testable.
  • Contaminated blood products. Possible, but the lecturer is explicit that this is a low-risk route and “nothing like what one sees in Ebola.”
  • No animal→mosquito→human cycle has been observed in the Western Hemisphere — another way of saying humans are the reservoir.

Clinical disease — four serotypes, three severity tiers

There are four dengue viruses, named DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3 and DENV-4. Disease from any of them ranges from completely asymptomatic — while still transmissible onward via a mosquito — to severe dengue with shock.

Slide 11 — The dengue rash. Non-specific on its own; more common in children than adults.

⚠️ The rash is not diagnostic

The lecturer is blunt about this: the rash is not particularly notable and certainly not diagnostic on its own. To use it you need the rest of the syndrome plus an exposure history — mosquito contact, blood exposure, endemic-area travel. It is also more commonly seen in children than in adults, so its absence in an adult argues nothing.

TIER 1

Common / acute dengue

Fever and chills, headache, rash, musculoskeletal and lumbar (low back) pain, eye pain (retro-orbital). The musculoskeletal pain is the “breakbone” component that named the disease.

TIER 2

Moderate infection

All of tier 1, plus any two of: abdominal tenderness; persistent anorexia, nausea or vomiting; mucosal bleeding. The outline adds hemoptysis and liver symptoms to this tier.

TIER 3

Severe dengue

Dengue plus any one of: hypovolemic shock; severe bleeding; organ failure. This is the panel D endpoint. Requires hospitalization.

🔑 How the severity tiers are actually defined

Notice the structure, because it is how the criteria are written and how they are tested: tier 2 is tier 1 plus any two warning features; tier 3 is dengue plus any one of three severe features. A patient with fever, headache, rash and bone pain who then develops abdominal tenderness and persistent vomiting has moderate dengue. The same patient who instead becomes hypotensive and hypoperfused has severe dengue, on that one finding alone.

Diagnosis

  1. Clinical suspicion, anchored by exposure.A febrile patient with typical dengue manifestations and a history of recent exposure in an endemic area. Without the exposure history, dengue does not belong on the differential.
  2. Multiplex reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR).Commercial kits exist that test for dengue and several other hemorrhagic fever viruses simultaneously — the top five or six agents on one multiplex plate. Expensive, but it resolves the whole differential in one run.
  3. Dengue virus antigen detection.By ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) on blood, after an appropriate interval — you have to let antigen concentration rise first.
  4. Antibody testing — usually not useful acutely.It would take weeks to become positive, which does not help a patient in front of you now.

Treatment — supportive only

⚠️ There is no antiviral for dengue

Despite the disease being nearly two thousand years old, no specific antiviral agent is available. Management is entirely supportive:

  • Fluid intake to prevent dehydration — the single most important intervention.
  • Acetaminophen for fever and muscle pain.
  • Avoid NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs). They increase bleeding risk, and the patient already has bleeding into organs and under the skin.
  • Hospitalization for severe cases — those patients are in genuine trouble.

Prevention — vector control, plus one unusual vaccine

Prevention rests primarily on control of the Aedes mosquito in endemic areas. There is, however, a licensed vaccine, and its design is worth understanding because it is genuinely clever and easy to write a question about.

🧠 Dengvaxia — a live attenuated tetravalent recombinant vaccine

FDA-approved in 2019. Take each word of the description apart:

  • Live attenuated — the backbone is the attenuated yellow fever vaccine virus. (The yellow fever vaccine is itself a live attenuated virus vaccine, which is the fact that makes this possible.)
  • Recombinant — that yellow fever genome has been augmented, with genes inserted into it.
  • Tetravalent — the inserted genes encode the surface proteins of all four dengue serotypes, DENV-1 through DENV-4. That is where “tetravalent” comes from.
⚠️ The prescribing restriction — high yield

Do not give the dengue vaccine to someone who has not previously had dengue. In the dengue-naive it “can be kind of rough on them.” The vaccine is indicated only for people with documented prior dengue infection. This is the single most examinable fact about Dengvaxia — a vaccine with a prior infection prerequisite is unusual enough to be worth a question on its own.

Q: A 30-year-old returns from two weeks in Puerto Rico and, five days later, develops fever to 102 °F, severe headache, retro-orbital pain, and pain in the lower back and long bones. On day 3 of illness a faint macular rash appears and the platelet count is 88,000. What is the diagnosis, what test do you order, and what analgesic do you specifically avoid?

Answer

Dengue fever. The tell-tale combination is endemic-area travel (Puerto Rico accounts for most United States dengue cases), an incubation of 4–7 days matching the intrinsic incubation period, and the classic tier-1 features — fever, headache, retro-orbital eye pain, musculoskeletal and lumbar pain (“breakbone”), rash — with thrombocytopenia. Order a multiplex RT-PCR, which covers dengue plus the other main hemorrhagic fever viruses in one run; ELISA for dengue antigen is the alternative. Avoid NSAIDs — they increase bleeding risk on top of an existing coagulopathy. Use acetaminophen instead, and give fluids.

Q: Which mosquito is the principal dengue vector, and why does the other Aedes species matter for the future of dengue in the northern United States?

Answer

Aedes aegypti is the principal vector — it is domestic and strongly prefers to bite humans, but its United States range currently thins out around New Jersey. Aedes albopictus, the tiger mosquito, is more cold tolerant and already extends well into New England and the Great Lakes — but it is less inclined to bite humans, preferring animals, so its northern presence has not translated into northern dengue. The concerning projection is that global warming models predict aegypti’s range expanding northward, which would bring the human-biting vector — and with it yellow fever, chikungunya and Zika as well as dengue — into that territory.

Q: A healthy 22-year-old with no history of dengue is moving to a highly endemic region and asks for the dengue vaccine. What do you tell them?

Answer

They should not receive it. Dengvaxia is only given to people with documented previous dengue infection; in the dengue-naive it can produce a rougher course. Counsel vector avoidance instead — that is the mainstay of prevention in endemic areas. Worth knowing for the same question: the vaccine is a live attenuated tetravalent recombinant built on the attenuated yellow fever vaccine virus, whose genome carries added genes for the surface proteins of all four dengue serotypes.

LO 6 — Hantavirus hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS)

Bunyaviridae · Agent 2

Hantavirus — the one where the kidney fails

Every other agent in this lecture kills through generalized multi-organ failure. Hantavirus hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) is the exception: the kidney is damaged by a specific, immunopathologic mechanism, and acute kidney injury is the usual cause of death. If a stem gives you a hemorrhagic fever with renal failure out of proportion to everything else, this is the answer.

Family and structure — Bunyaviridae, three genome segments

The Bunyaviridae (order Bunyavirales) are 80–120 nanometre, enveloped, single-stranded RNA viruses whose genome comes in three segments. There are 30 species of hantavirus, of which 12 are known to cause human disease.

Two distinct hantavirus diseases — and only one is in this lecture

🩸

HFRS — hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome

The “Old World” hantaviruses. Far East (especially China and Korea) extending into Western Europe. This is the hemorrhagic disease, and the subject of this section.

  • Hantaan virus — first found in Korea; also China
  • Seoul virus — also Korea
  • Dobrava virus — central and western Europe
  • Puumala virus — Scandinavia
🫁

HCPS — hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome

The “New World” hantaviruses, of the Americas — for example Andes virus. Taught in the respiratory system block, not here.

The lecturer’s topical example: the recent contamination of a marine vessel, probably from rodents, with Andes hantavirus — a New World virus, hence cardiopulmonary rather than renal.

⚠️ Old World versus New World is the whole distinction

Old World hantaviruses → kidneys (HFRS). New World hantaviruses → heart and lungs (HCPS). Get the hemisphere right and the syndrome follows. This also means HFRS is genuinely rare in the United States — a United States case would be someone who has been to an endemic area in the Far East or parts of Western Europe, or who picked it up from a visitor.

Reservoir and transmission — rodents, and no vector at all

Slide 15 — Field mice and rats are the usual hantavirus reservoir.

Slide 15 — The vole — the Scandinavian reservoir for Puumala virus.

  • Reservoir: rodents — in particular field mice and rats. In Scandinavia, Puumala virus is carried by voles.
  • Virus is shed in rodent urine, feces and saliva.
  • Transmission to humans is by aerosol. The classic scenario: someone working in a building contaminated with rodent urine, feces or saliva generates aerosols in the course of the work and inhales them.
  • There is no vector. No mosquito, no tick. This is a pure reservoir-contact disease.
  • Person-to-person transmission via aerosol is possible but very rare — it probably does not happen often.
  • Burden: an estimated 60,000–120,000 cases per year worldwide.
🩺 The historical case that named the disease in the West

Before the early 1950s, this illness was seen almost exclusively in parts of Asia and Southeast Asia. The first Western experience came during the Korean War: over a two-year period, roughly 2,000–2,500 United Nations troops serving in Korea acquired what we now recognize as hantavirus HFRS, with about a 5% fatality rate. That outbreak is why the prototype agent is called Hantaan virus, after the Hantan River.

Clinical course

🔑 The incubation period is unusually long — and diagnostically useful

3 to 9 weeks from inhalation to symptom onset. Compare dengue’s 4–7 days. A patient who cleaned out a rodent-infested cabin a month or two ago and is now febrile is still within the window — do not dismiss the exposure because it was not recent.

  • Usual course: flu-like — high fever, headache, vomiting, then the general viral hemorrhagic fever picture from Section 2.
  • Severe course, and what makes this virus distinctive: signs of shock; signs of hemorrhage, which usually appears first as petechiae and may include conjunctival hemorrhage; hypotension; and acute renal failure.

Slide 16 — Petechiae and confluent purpura — the usual first hemorrhagic manifestation of HFRS.

⚠️ Kidney damage here is not just part of multi-organ failure

This is the point the lecturer stresses hardest. Other hemorrhagic fevers may produce renal failure as one component of multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). Hantavirus HFRS is different: kidney damage is common and mechanistically specific. The lesion is an acute tubulointerstitial nephritis that is immunopathologic in origin, and acute kidney injury (AKI) is the most common cause of mortality in this infection.

How the kidney is actually injured

Slide 17 — Immunopathologic mechanism of hantavirus kidney injury.

Read this figure as the general pathogenesis of Section 1, aimed at the renal vascular bed:

  1. Infected dendritic cells and macrophages start the cascade.They release IL-12, TNF-α (tumor necrosis factor alpha), IL-1 and a set of chemokines — RANTES, IP-10, IL-6, MCP-1, IL-8 — along with VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor). This is the cytokine storm of panel B.

🧠 Why “immunopathologic” is the operative word

Almost nothing in that cascade is the virus destroying kidney tissue directly. It is cytokines, cytotoxic T cells, antibody-dependent cytotoxicity, complement and bradykinin attacking an endothelium that has been marked as infected. The kidney is collateral damage from the host’s own response — which is exactly why there is no antiviral to give and why the treatment is supportive.

Diagnosis

  • Suspect it in a patient with fever of unknown origin, thrombocytopenia and renal failure — particularly if preceded by respiratory distress.
  • Exposure history is essential: living in, or recent travel to, an endemic area — which for HFRS means the Far East or parts of Western Europe, not the United States.
  • Serology is the mainstay: ELISA for anti-hantavirus IgM and IgG. The limitation is that antibodies take a couple of weeks to appear.
  • RT-PCR is used when a patient may have a fulminant infection and you cannot wait two weeks for antibodies.

Treatment

⚠️ Supportive only — and no United States vaccine
  • No specific antiviral therapy.
  • No vaccine is currently available in the United States.
  • Supportive care: oxygen therapy, intubation if needed, and above all fluid management.
  • Because the kidneys are failing, anything that addresses that problem — careful volume and electrolyte management, and renal replacement therapy where indicated — is the appropriate treatment.

Q: A 40-year-old who spent last month renovating an old barn in rural South Korea presents with fever, headache, vomiting, petechiae, conjunctival hemorrhage, hypotension, a platelet count of 40,000 and a creatinine of 4.2 mg/dL. What is the agent, how did they get it, and what specifically will kill them?

Answer

Hantavirus hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) — an Old World hantavirus, most likely Hantaan or Seoul virus given Korea. They acquired it by inhaling aerosolized rodent urine, feces or saliva generated while working in a rodent-contaminated building; there is no vector. The exposure a month ago fits the 3–9 week incubation period. The likely cause of death is acute kidney injury — an immunopathologic acute tubulointerstitial nephritis, which is the most common cause of mortality in this infection. Management is supportive: there is no antiviral and no vaccine available in the United States.

Q: A hantavirus is isolated from a patient in New Mexico who presents with rapidly progressive respiratory failure and pulmonary edema, with normal renal function. Is this HFRS, and what does the presentation tell you about the virus?

Answer

No — this is hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS), caused by a New World hantavirus of the Americas (the class of which Andes virus is the lecture’s named example). Old World hantaviruses cause the renal disease (HFRS); New World hantaviruses cause the cardiopulmonary disease. HCPS is taught in the respiratory system block. The shared features remain: rodent reservoir, aerosol transmission, no vector, supportive treatment.

Q: Which hantavirus genome segment would you target with a vaccine intended to generate neutralizing antibody, and why?

Answer

The M (medium) segment, because it encodes the envelope glycoproteins Gn and Gc — the outer surface spike complex, and therefore the only viral proteins accessible to antibody on an intact virion. The S segment encodes the internal nucleocapsid (N) protein that coats the RNA, and the L segment encodes the internal RNA-dependent RNA polymerase; neither is exposed on the particle surface.

LO 7 — Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF)

Bunyaviridae · genus Nairovirus · Agent 3

Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever — the tick-borne one

💡 Why this agent gets its own section despite being uncommon

The lecturer’s stated reason for including it: “it appears frequently on the USMLE test, especially Step 1.” Roughly 1,000 cases are reported per year worldwide — a small disease with a disproportionate examination footprint. Learn it as a pattern: tick bite, livestock contact, southeastern Europe or the Middle East, and a 30% mortality.

A number to be aware of: the course outline gives ~1,000 cases/year; in the recorded lecture the figure was quoted as ~10,000/year. Where the two disagree, the written outline is the safer number to carry into an exam.

Where the name comes from

The disease was first described in Russian soldiers stationed in the Crimea in 1944. About ten years later the same virus was isolated from a child living in the Congo. Two geographically unrelated discoveries of one agent produced the compound name Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever. The virus group is sometimes called the Nairovirus group, and its morphology is similar to hantavirus because both are Bunyaviridae.

🔑 Endemic geography — the four regions

Africa · the Middle East · Asia · southeastern Europe. The dense cluster of isolation points across Turkey, the Balkans, the Caucasus, Iran and Central Asia is the visual signature to remember — that band is where the Hyalomma tick and its livestock hosts overlap with people.

Reservoir and transmission

Slide 19 — Hyalomma ticks — the CCHF vector. Note the banded legs.

Slide 19 — Extensive bleeding into the skin in severe CCHF.

  • The virus infects livestock and birds — and they stay well. Viral titers may rise, but there are no symptoms in either group. As the lecturer puts it, the animals appear completely adapted to it; only humans get sick.
  • Principal transmission to humans is the bite of a Hyalomma tick, which acquires the virus by feeding on livestock. Hyalomma ticks are slow feeders — like the Ixodes ticks you already know — and normally feed on livestock, but will feed on humans.
  • Direct contact with infected animal or human blood is the second route. Because the virus does grow in animals despite not sickening them, copious exposure to infected animal blood — a slaughterhouse or veterinary setting — can transmit it. But most of the time it is the tick.
⚠️ One naming point to keep straight

In the recording, the Hyalomma tick is described as “related to the deer tick, but it’s not the deer tick.” That is the right idea — both are hard ticks (family Ixodidae) and both are slow feeders — but the vector of CCHF is the genus Hyalomma, not Ixodes. If a stem names Ixodes scapularis (the deer tick), it is pointing at Lyme disease, babesiosis or anaplasmosis, not CCHF.

Q: A 45-year-old sheep farmer in eastern Turkey presents with abrupt fever, severe headache, myalgia, conjunctivitis, and abdominal pain, and on day 4 develops bleeding gums, widespread petechiae, and hemoptysis. What is the diagnosis, what is the vector, and what is the approximate mortality?

Answer

Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF), a Nairovirus within the Bunyaviridae. The vector is the Hyalomma tick, which acquires the virus by feeding on livestock — animals that carry the virus without becoming ill. This farmer also has the alternative route available to him: direct contact with infected animal blood. Mortality is approximately 30%. There is no antiviral; give supportive care and use acetaminophen rather than NSAIDs. Note that about 88% of infections are subclinical — this patient is in the symptomatic minority.

Q: How does the reservoir logic of CCHF differ from that of dengue, and why does it matter for prevention?

Answer

In dengue, humans are the reservoir — the cycle is human→mosquito→human, so controlling the vector also removes the only bridge between infected and uninfected people. In CCHF, the reservoir is livestock and birds that never become ill, so the virus is maintained in an animal population you cannot detect by looking for sick animals and cannot eliminate. Prevention therefore falls entirely on the human end of the chain: avoiding tick bites and avoiding blood exposure during animal handling and slaughter.

On electron micrographs the virions contain small granules that should not be there. They are 30-nanometre ribosomes belonging to the host cell, apparently internalized during virus assembly. There is no known function — a ribosome inside a virion has nothing to make. But the granular, speckled look gave the family its name: Latin arena, sand. Arenavirus = “sandy virus.”

Lassa fever — epidemiology

  • First identified in 1969, in the village of Lassa, Nigeria — which is where the name comes from.
  • Roughly 300,000–350,000 cases per year with about 5,000 deaths, giving an overall mortality of about 1%.
  • Primarily West Africa — Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia and Guinea.
  • The 1969 event that made it famous in the United States: the first notable outbreak here occurred at Yale University, among a research group working with the virus (and with monkeys that had picked it up). It was highly fatal in that group, and made headlines that a “weird African disease” otherwise would not have.

Reservoir and transmission

Slide 21 — Mastomys natalensis, the multimammate rat — the Lassa reservoir.

  • Reservoir: the multimammate rat, Mastomys natalensis — a very common rodent distributed throughout Nigeria and neighbouring countries.
  • Human acquisition — three routes, all involving the rodent directly:
    • Direct contact with rodent urine, feces or bodily fluids
    • Inhalation of aerosolized rodent excretions
    • Consumption of infected rodents as a food source
  • There is no insect vector. As with hantavirus, you must contact the animal or its products.
  • Human-to-human transmission occurs by exposure to blood, tissues, secretions or excretions of an infected person.
🔑 The two no-vector rodent diseases, side by side

Hantavirus HFRS and Lassa are the two agents in this lecture with no arthropod vector at all. They differ on three points that a question can hinge on: geography (Far East / Western Europe versus West Africa), rodent (field mice, rats and voles versus Mastomys natalensis specifically), and the extra route — Lassa adds eating the rodent, which hantavirus does not have.

🩺 The clinical shape of Lassa in one paragraph

About 80% of infected people have subclinical or mild disease — a little fever, a little headache, a little myalgia — and it goes away. That is stage 1. The minority who progress get the full hemorrhagic picture: bleeding from gums, eyes, gastrointestinal tract and nose; respiratory distress; vomiting and diarrhea; chest, back and abdominal pain; then shock and multi-organ failure. When death occurs it is usually a couple of weeks after symptom onset, and the cause is multi-organ failure.

⚠️ The sequela that identifies Lassa — high yield

Survivors characteristically complain of hearing loss — sensorineural, and usually bilateral — along with tremors, balance difficulties, hair loss and difficulty speaking. Cases of encephalitis following Lassa fever have been described. New bilateral sensorineural hearing loss after a febrile illness acquired in West Africa is Lassa until proven otherwise — no other agent in this lecture has that sequela.

Diagnosis

  • History first: travel to an endemic region — Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea — plus direct or indirect exposure to Mastomys rodents or to a symptomatic person.
  • ELISA for anti-Lassa IgM and IgG.
  • ELISA for Lassa antigen in blood, when you need a faster answer than antibody kinetics allow.
  • RT-PCR is preferred if available — the quickest route to identifying the virus in blood.

Treatment

🔑 Lassa is the one agent in Part 1 with a drug — and it is a weak one
  • Supportive care first: maintain oxygen levels, maintain blood pressure, replace fluids.
  • Ribavirin — a guanosine analog that inhibits viral RNA synthesis. Studies have shown mixed results. The lecturer’s honest summary: “it’s not a great drug, but it is used and it can slow things down.”

Contrast this with dengue, hantavirus HFRS and CCHF, for which no antiviral exists at all.

Prevention

🧠 No licensed vaccine — but the candidates are instructive

No vaccine is licensed for human use. The most advanced candidates entered human testing about a year and a half ago. They are viral vector vaccines, and the vectors are worth knowing because the same platform logic recurs across modern vaccinology:

  • An attenuated measles virus — the same kind of attenuated virus used in the MMR (measles, mumps and rubella) vaccine; or
  • An attenuated vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV).

In both, Lassa genes have been added to the vector genome — specifically genes for the synthesis of Lassa external (surface) viral protein. The recipient is inoculated with the harmless attenuated vector, which then expresses Lassa surface protein and raises an immune response against it. Early results appear to be working, but it is not ready yet.

⚠️ One correction worth carrying

In the recording, the VSV vector is described as “vesicular dermatitis virus … which is a pox virus.” The intended agent is vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), and it is a rhabdovirus, not a poxvirus — the same family as rabies virus. It is a very widely used vaccine vector (the licensed Ebola vaccine uses it). Learn the name and family correctly; the vaccine concept described in the lecture is otherwise exactly right.

🩺 The other half of prevention

Avoid the rodents — particularly the large Mastomys multimammate rats — and use appropriate precautions when caring for infected patients. The lecturer closes the lecture on this point and generalizes it: with all of these hemorrhagic fever viruses, if you are really sloppy handling an infected person you can acquire it. Ebola is the obvious example, but it is true of most of them.

Q: A 29-year-old aid worker returns from rural Nigeria with three weeks of fever, sore throat, retrosternal chest pain, vomiting and diarrhea, followed by facial and neck swelling, conjunctival injection and proteinuria. On recovery she reports she cannot hear properly in either ear. What was the infection, what was the reservoir, and what is the one drug with any evidence?

Answer

Lassa fever, an Arenaviridae infection. The reservoir is the multimammate rat, Mastomys natalensis, acquired by contact with or inhalation of its urine and feces, or by eating the rodent — there is no insect vector. The described course maps precisely onto the staged natural history: stage II (sore throat, retrosternal chest pain, vomiting, diarrhea) progressing to stage III (head and neck edema, conjunctival injection, proteinuria). The bilateral sensorineural hearing loss is the classic Lassa sequela and effectively clinches it. The one drug is ribavirin, a guanosine analog that inhibits viral RNA synthesis — with mixed results, used alongside supportive care.

Q: An electron micrograph of a virus recovered from a hemorrhagic fever patient shows pleomorphic enveloped particles of widely varying size containing scattered internal electron-dense granules. What are the granules, and what family is this?

Answer

The granules are host-cell ribosomes, about 30 nm, apparently internalized during virus assembly in the host cell. They have no known function for the virus. This sandy, granular appearance is what named the family: Arenaviridae, from Latin arena, sand. The other consistent features here are pleomorphism, an envelope, and the wide 50–300 nm size range; the genome is two single strands of RNA.

Q: Explain how the leading Lassa vaccine candidates work, and why using an attenuated measles virus as the vector is not the same as giving someone measles.

Answer

They are recombinant viral vector vaccines. A harmless attenuated vector virus — either the attenuated measles virus used in MMR, or an attenuated vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) — has Lassa genes added to its genome, specifically genes encoding Lassa external surface proteins. The vector replicates in a limited, non-pathogenic way and expresses Lassa surface protein, against which the recipient mounts an immune response. The vector is attenuated, meaning it has been rendered unable to cause measles — it is the same virus already given to essentially every vaccinated child. The candidates are in early human trials; no Lassa vaccine is licensed.

Viral hemorrhagic fever (VHF)
A febrile viral illness that damages small blood vessels, diminishes the ability to clot, and can affect multiple organ systems.
Antigen-presenting cell (APC)
A cell — dendritic cell, macrophage, B cell — that displays processed antigen to T cells. In VHF these adopt an immature phenotype with low co-stimulation and low type I interferon.
Co-stimulatory molecule
The “second signal” surface protein an APC must provide, alongside antigen presentation, for a T cell to be activated rather than rendered anergic.
Type I interferon (IFN)
The antiviral cytokine family (IFN-α, IFN-β) that puts neighbouring cells into an antiviral state. Suppressing it is the founding event of VHF pathogenesis.
Natural killer (NK) cell
An innate lymphocyte that kills virus-infected cells without prior sensitization. Poorly activated in VHF.
Cytokine storm
Hyperproduction of pro-inflammatory mediators by monocytes and macrophages, at concentrations that damage the host — endothelial and platelet activation, coagulation cascade activation, bystander T cell apoptosis.
Bystander activation
Activation of lymphocytes that are not specific for the infecting virus, driven by the cytokine environment. In VHF these cells then undergo apoptosis, deepening immunosuppression.
Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
Widespread activation of clotting throughout the microvasculature that consumes platelets and clotting factors, so the patient thromboses and bleeds simultaneously.
Multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS)
Progressive failure of two or more organ systems. The common endpoint of every agent in this lecture.
Vector
An arthropod that transmits a pathogen and in which the pathogen replicates. The replication requirement is what separates a true vector from a mechanical carrier.
Extrinsic incubation period
Time from a vector’s infectious blood meal to the vector becoming able to transmit — 8–10 days for dengue in Aedes, during which virus travels midgut → salivary glands.
Intrinsic incubation period
Time from an infectious bite to symptom onset in the human host — 4–7 days for dengue.
Zoonotic
Transmitted from animals to humans. Every family in this lecture is zoonotic, arthropod-borne, or both.
Reservoir
The population that maintains a pathogen in nature between human cases. Humans for dengue; rodents for hantavirus and Lassa; livestock and birds for CCHF.
Pleomorphic
Having no fixed shape or size. Characteristic of Arenaviridae, whose particles range from 50 to 300 nm.
“Sandy” / arenavirus appearance
The granular look of arenavirus particles on electron micrograph, caused by host ribosomes (~30 nm) internalized during assembly. Latin arena = sand.
Acute tubulointerstitial nephritis
Inflammation of the renal tubules and interstitium. In hantavirus HFRS it is immunopathologic — caused by the host response to infected endothelium rather than by direct viral cytotoxicity.
Petechiae
Pinpoint, non-blanching hemorrhages into the skin. Typically the first visible hemorrhagic manifestation in HFRS and CCHF.
“Breakbone fever”
The 1780 Philadelphia name for dengue, from the extreme musculoskeletal and joint pain. Described then as a bilious remittent fever with extreme joint stiffness.
Bilious
An older clinical term meaning queasy or nauseated, used in the 1780 description of dengue.
Retro-orbital pain
Pain behind the eyes. A characteristic and high-yield dengue feature.
Tetravalent
Directed against four antigenic types. The dengue vaccine carries surface protein genes for all four dengue serotypes.
Live attenuated vaccine
A vaccine containing a weakened but replicating virus. The yellow fever vaccine is one, and it is the backbone of the dengue vaccine.
Viral vector vaccine
A vaccine in which a harmless attenuated virus carries added genes for the target pathogen’s antigens. The Lassa candidates use attenuated measles virus or VSV as vectors.
Guanosine analog
A nucleoside mimic. Ribavirin is one; it inhibits viral RNA synthesis.
Sensorineural hearing loss
Hearing loss originating in the cochlea or auditory nerve rather than the conducting apparatus. Usually bilateral after Lassa fever, and the signature sequela of that infection.

Q: What does the term “arenavirus” literally mean, and what physical observation produced the name?

Answer

“Sandy virus,” from the Latin arena, meaning sand. The observation was the granular, speckled interior of the virions on electron micrograph — caused by host-cell ribosomes of about 30 nm that are internalized during virus assembly. They serve no known viral function: a virus makes no proteins of its own inside the particle, so a captured ribosome does nothing there.

1–2 · MECHANISM & SYNDROME

Pathogenesis — four panels, one chain

  • A. Immunosuppression: APCs show an immature phenotype — low co-stimulatory molecules, low type I interferon → poor NK cell + T cell activation → loss of replication control, ↑ viral burden.
  • B. Cytokine storm: monocytes/macrophages release massive pro-inflammatory mediators → endothelial activation, platelet activation, coagulation cascade activation, bystander T cell apoptosis.
  • C. Dissemination: infected phagocytes carry live virus to lymph nodes and organs — brain, adrenal, liver, spleen.
  • D. Vascular collapse: sustained inflammation + direct endothelial infection → ↑ permeability, leak, DIC, vasodilatation, multi-organ failure and death.
  • This is sepsis physiology by a viral route.

Panel C organ effects (labs)

  • Liver: ↑↑ AST/ALT; ↓ clotting factor synthesis → ↑ coagulation times.
  • Adrenal: ↓ steroid-synthesizing enzymes → hypotension, sodium loss, hypovolemia.

Why the patient bleeds — three legs

  • Consumption of platelets and factors (cytokine storm).
  • Failure of production (hepatic infection).
  • Leaky, damaged vessel (endothelial infection).

Shared syndrome — 3 stages

  • 1. Prodrome: fever ≥100.4 °F (usually 101–103), weakness, headache, bone and joint pain, dizziness, vomiting, diarrhea, rash, retro-orbital pain, photophobia.
  • 2. Coagulation disorder: petechiae, bleeding under skin, from gums/mouth/eyes/ears, into internal organs.
  • 3. Shock: hypotension, delirium, seizures, coma, kidney and liver failure → multi-organ failure.

⚠ Class treatment rule

  • Supportive care only for every agent except Lassa (ribavirin).
  • Acetaminophen for fever/pain. NEVER NSAIDs — they increase bleeding.
  • Fluids, blood pressure support, oxygenation.
3–5 · FAMILIES & DENGUE

Five families — four shared properties

  • Arenaviridae · Bunyaviridae · Filoviridae · Flaviviridae · Togaviridae
  • All enveloped; all ssRNA; all have an animal or insect reservoir.
  • Humans acquire by direct animal contact or vector transmission; human→human needs blood or body fluids.
  • Deferred: yellow fever → GI; chikungunya → MSK; Ebola/Marburg → Part 2; hantavirus HCPS → respiratory.

Flaviviridae — family facts

  • Flavus = yellow, for yellow fever. >70 types.
  • ~50 nm, enveloped, ssRNA, arthropod-borne or zoonotic.
  • Extremely labile: heat, UV, disinfectants, acid, alkali. Stable only in host or vector → no fomite spread, no durable aerosols.
  • Also contains West Nile, St. Louis encephalitis, Powassan, Zika — none hemorrhagic.

Dengue — core

  • “Breakbone fever” — 1780 Philadelphia. DENV-1 to DENV-4.
  • Reservoir = humans. No Western-Hemisphere animal reservoir identified.
  • Vector: female Aedes aegypti (principal; domestic, prefers humans; range thins ~New Jersey) and Ae. albopictus (tiger mosquito; more cold tolerant, into New England/Great Lakes; prefers animals).
  • Also vertical (mother→fetus) and blood products (low risk).
  • Extrinsic incubation 8–10 d (midgut→salivary glands); intrinsic 4–7 d.
  • ~400M cases/yr, 40,000 deaths. Americas ~13M (2024). US Jan–Aug 2025: 2,725, mostly Puerto Rico; continental FL, CA, TX, NY; locally acquired in HI, FL, TX in late summer/fall.
  • ~24% clinically apparent (76% inapparent but transmissible).

Dengue — severity tiers

  • Common/acute: fever/chills, headache, rash, musculoskeletal + lumbar pain, eye pain.
  • Moderate: above + any TWO of abdominal tenderness, persistent anorexia/nausea/vomiting, mucosal bleeding (± hemoptysis, liver symptoms).
  • Severe: dengue + any ONE of hypovolemic shock, severe bleeding, organ failure.
  • Rash not diagnostic; more common in children.

Dengue — Dx / Tx / vaccine

  • Dx: febrile + symptoms + endemic exposure; multiplex RT-PCR; ELISA antigen. Antibody takes weeks.
  • Tx: supportive; fluids; acetaminophen, no NSAIDs; no antiviral; hospitalize severe.
  • Dengvaxia (FDA 2019): live attenuated tetravalent recombinant — attenuated yellow fever vaccine virus backbone + genes for the 4 dengue surface proteins.
  • ⚠ Only give to people with PRIOR dengue infection.
6–8 · HANTA · CCHF · LASSA

Hantavirus HFRS — the kidney one

  • Bunyaviridae, 80–120 nm, enveloped, ssRNA in 3 segments. 30 species, 12 cause human disease.
  • S → nucleocapsid (N) protein; M → envelope glycoproteins Gn/Gc; L → RdRp.
  • Old World → HFRS (renal). Hantaan (Korea/China), Seoul (Korea), Dobrava (C/W Europe), Puumala (Scandinavia). New World → HCPS (Andes) → respiratory block.
  • Reservoir: field mice, rats; voles for Puumala. Shed in urine, feces, saliva.
  • Transmission: AEROSOL inhalation. NO VECTOR. Person→person very rare.
  • Incubation 3–9 WEEKS. ~60,000–120,000 cases/yr.
  • Korean War 1950s: ~2,000–2,500 UN troops, ~5% fatality.
  • Severe: shock, petechiae, conjunctival hemorrhage, hypotension, acute renal failure.
  • ⚠ Kidney damage is common and specific — immunopathologic acute tubulointerstitial nephritis; AKI is the most common cause of death.
  • Dx: fever + thrombocytopenia + renal failure + endemic exposure; ELISA IgM/IgG (mainstay, ~2 wks); RT-PCR if fulminant.
  • Tx: supportive — oxygen, intubation, fluid management. No antiviral, no US vaccine.

Crimean–Congo (CCHF) — the tick one

  • Bunyaviridae, genus Nairovirus. Frequent on USMLE Step 1.
  • Crimea 1944 (Russian soldiers) + Congo ~10 yrs later (a child) → the compound name.
  • Endemic: Africa, Middle East, Asia, southeastern Europe. ~1,000 cases/yr (outline; lecture said ~10,000).
  • Reservoir: livestock and birds — ASYMPTOMATIC.
  • Vector: Hyalomma tick (slow feeder; acquires virus from livestock). Also direct contact with infected animal or human blood.
  • ~88% subclinical. Acute: sudden fever, headache, myalgia, conjunctivitis, photophobia, abdominal pain, GI upset. Severe: pulmonary hemorrhage, gum bleeding, petechiae.
  • ⚠ ~30% mortality — the highest in this lecture.
  • Dx: RT-PCR; IgM/IgG kits. Tx: no antiviral; fluids, electrolytes, ventilation, hemodialysis, vasopressors/inotropes; acetaminophen not NSAIDs. Prevent tick bites.

Lassa fever — the West Africa one

  • Arenaviridae: pleomorphic, enveloped, 50–300 nm, 2 single RNA strands. Also Machupo (Bolivian HF), Junin (Argentine HF), LCMV (neuro).
  • “Sandy” granules on EM = host 30 nm ribosomes internalized during assembly, no known function. Latin arena = sand.
  • 1969, village of Lassa, Nigeria. First US outbreak at Yale University, highly fatal in the research group.
  • ~300–350K cases/yr, ~5,000 deaths, ~1% mortality. West Africa: Nigeria, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Guinea.
  • Reservoir: multimammate rat, Mastomys natalensis. NO VECTOR.
  • Acquisition: contact with rodent urine/feces/fluids; inhaled aerosols; eating the rodent. Human→human via blood, tissue, secretions, excretions.
  • Incubation 6–21 d. ~80% mild (stage I). Stages II–IV: sore throat/retrosternal pain/vomiting → head-neck edema, conjunctival injection, proteinuria, hypotension, anemia → altered mental status, convulsions, bleeding, coma.
  • ⚠ Survivor sequela: BILATERAL SENSORINEURAL HEARING LOSS, tremors, balance and speech difficulty, hair loss. Death ~2 weeks after onset, from multi-organ failure.
  • Dx: travel + Mastomys/patient exposure; ELISA IgM/IgG and antigen; RT-PCR preferred.
  • Tx: supportive + RIBAVIRIN (guanosine analog, inhibits viral RNA synthesis; mixed results) — the only antiviral in Part 1.
  • Vaccine: none licensed; candidates in early human trials use attenuated measles virus or attenuated VSV (vesicular stomatitis virus, a rhabdovirus) vectors carrying Lassa surface protein genes.
