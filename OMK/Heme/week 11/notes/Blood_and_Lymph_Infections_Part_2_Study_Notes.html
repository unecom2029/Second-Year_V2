Six Organisms, One Grid

If microbiology feels like an infinite list of names, it is because it is usually taught as one. This lecture is not that. It is six organisms, and every single one of them answers the same seven questions — what kind of bug, which animal, which bug bites you, where in the world, what it does to the patient, how you prove it, what you give. Fill in the grid six times and you are done.

How to not hate this lecture

Micro is miserable when you try to memorize it as prose. It gets much easier when you notice that every infectious disease question is built from the same small number of slots, and that the exam almost always hands you the animal or the place before it hands you anything else. So: do not read this file front to back trying to absorb it. Read it as six copies of one table.

🔑 The seven slots. Every organism in this lecture fills all seven.
  1. What kind of bug is it?Virus, bacterium, or parasite. Three of each family here, and knowing the category already halves your differential.
  2. What is the reservoir — which animal keeps it alive in nature?Fruit bat. Cat. Rat. White-footed mouse. Human. Learn the animals and you have learned most of this lecture.
  3. Is there a vector, and which one?None (filoviruses) · cat flea · rat flea · deer tick · Anopheles mosquito.
  4. Where in the world?Central/West Africa · anywhere there are cats · the western United States · the American northeast · the tropics.
  5. What does it actually do to the patient?The one clinical picture that is only this organism — the bubo, the swollen node after a scratch, the ring inside the red cell.
  6. How do you prove it?Usually one named stain or one named test.
  7. What do you give?Usually one or two named drugs.
💡 The lecturer told you exactly how he writes these questions

In his closing remarks he gives the game away, and it is the single most useful sentence in the lecture:

“A lot of the questions that you see about hemorrhagic fever virus are going to — by definition, they're going to have to kind of point you towards some area, some geographic area where that organism is endemic.”

He cannot write a fair question about these organisms without telling you where the patient was or what they touched. So when you read a stem, find the geography and the exposure first, before you read a single lab value. That is the answer most of the time.

🧠 Three patterns that do most of the work
  • Two organisms live inside red blood cells and both make “rings.” Babesia and Plasmodium. You separate them by geography (New England tick versus tropical mosquito) and by fever pattern (Babesia irregular; malaria on a clock).
  • Two organisms are spread by fleas, and the fleas belong to different animals. Cat flea → Bartonella. Rat flea → plague.
  • Two viruses are essentially the same disease. Ebola and Marburg are both filoviruses with the same bat, the same route and the same syndrome. Marburg just does it harder — more bleeding, higher mortality, and no approved treatment at all.

That is genuinely most of it. Everything else in this file is detail hanging off those three observations.

🩺 One rule that explains half the vector biology in this lecture

The lecturer states it twice, once for the flea and once for the mosquito: “you can't be a vector unless the organism grows in you.”

An arthropod that merely carries a pathogen on its mouthparts delivers far too small a dose. A true vector amplifies the organism inside its own body first. This is why plague fleas and Anopheles mosquitoes and Ixodes nymphs are vectors, and it is also why none of them is born infected — each one has to acquire the organism by feeding on an infected reservoir animal before it can pass anything on.

Q: Before you learn a single fact about any of these six organisms, what two things should you always look for first in a question stem?

Answer

The geography and the exposure. Every organism in this lecture is a zoonosis maintained by a specific animal in a specific part of the world, so a fair question must tell you where the patient was or what they touched. Bat cave or funeral in the DRC → filovirus. Kitten scratch → Bartonella. Prairie dog in New Mexico → plague. Hiking in Connecticut → Babesia. Travel to sub-Saharan Africa → malaria. The labs usually only confirm what the first two lines already told you.

Filoviruses and Ebola — the filament with no vector

The family, in five facts

The name

Why “filo”

Filo = filament, “for obvious reasons.” These are unusually shaped viruses — nothing else looks like this.

The dimensions

80 nm × up to 800 nm

~80 nanometres wide, as much as 800 nanometres long — that is 0.8 micrometres, almost the length of an E. coli if you stretched it out. You still cannot see it, because it is so thin.

The shapes

Three of them

Particles are circular, straight, or shaped like a “6” (the shepherd's crook).

The genome

Molecular

Single-stranded RNA — like every hemorrhagic fever virus in Parts 1 and 2.

The route in

Entry

They gain access through mucosal surfaces via small abrasions. Intact skin is a real barrier — but as the lecturer puts it, “they can find a way in.”

The vector

High yield

There is NO insect vector for any filovirus. This is a direct-contact disease from start to finish.

🧠 The genuinely unusual thing about Ebola: it will grow in almost anything

Most viruses are picky — they have a couple of cell types they can enter. Filoviruses have an incredibly wide host range, which is “unusual for viruses” and is a large part of why the disease is so destructive. Cells infected by Ebola virus:

🔑 Reservoir, and the unfairness the lecturer objects to

The natural host is the Egyptian fruit bat, also called the rousette bat. The virus grows in it and the bat can transmit it — but the bat is not sick. (“Which I think is unfair. They should at least be sick if they're gonna transmit it to people.”)

That is a real pattern worth carrying: a healthy-appearing reservoir animal is the rule, not the exception, in this whole block. The same was true of livestock in Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever in Part 1.

🦉

Animal → human

Close contact with the blood, secretions, organs or other bodily fluids of infected animals.

Starting with fruit bats, but a long list of other animals can harbor the virus:

  • Chimpanzees, gorillas, monkeys
  • Forest antelope
  • Porcupines
  • Pigs
🤵

Human → human

Contact with body fluids — blood, feces, vomit, “you name it” — from someone sick with Ebola.

  • Or the body of a person who died of Ebola. The virus persists in a corpse, so death does not make the body safe.
  • Fomites contaminated with those same fluids.
  • Sexual transmission is possible — the virus can be found in semen.

At risk: health care workers and people preparing bodies for funerals.

⚠️ The corpse point is the one people forget

“Ebola can last for some period of time in a corpse, so handling this material after the person dies doesn't mean the virus has disappeared.” This is the reason traditional funerary practices — washing and touching the body — have repeatedly driven outbreaks, and it is why funeral workers are named as an at-risk group alongside health care workers. If a stem mentions a funeral, that is a deliberate clue.

Epidemiology — and the current outbreak

Slide 6 — The current outbreak, concentrated in the eastern DRC.

  • Endemic in Central and West Africa, principally the Democratic Republic of the Congo (formerly Zaire) and sometimes Sudan.
  • Outbreaks are NOT seen every year, and they appear to be somewhat localized — unlike a globally endemic disease such as dengue or malaria.
  • Fatality rate depends on the species: 25% to 90%. Zaire is currently considered the most virulent.
  • The current DRC outbreak, as of the figures quoted in class (2 September): ~6,300 confirmed cases, of which about 48% have died — a little over 3,000 deaths. There have been cases in Uganda, but fewer than 50, so this is overwhelmingly a DRC outbreak.

Clinical picture

Honestly assessed by the lecturer: “it's not a whole lot different from the other hemorrhagic fever viruses.” The Part 1 blueprint applies.

  • Abrupt fever and chills, headache, vomiting, diarrhea
  • Skin rash at about day 6–7
  • Petechial hemorrhage, tachypnea, neurologic and cardiovascular symptoms
  • Organ failure — “the whole thing”

🩺 Two things about the rash, and one of them is a clinical-skills point
  • It is maculopapular and follows a route: it usually starts on the face, then the neck and upper trunk, then spreads out to the arms and legs.
  • The rash is not diagnostic on its own. You need all the other findings alongside it.
  • It is more difficult to visualize as skin gets darker — “which is something that you've got to think about.” The lecturer includes that comparison slide deliberately. A rash you cannot see is still a rash, and assuming its absence in a patient with darker skin is a real diagnostic error, not a theoretical one.

⚠️ Why the current outbreak is so hard to treat — the strain-specificity problem

Every licensed product — Inmazeb, Ebanga and Ervebo — was built against the Zaire glycoproteins, because Zaire was “what was rampant at the time.” In the current outbreak it has been found that there is very little cross-protection against Bundibugyo, whether the person received monoclonals or the vaccine.

The lecturer's forecast: “each one of them is slightly different and probably is going to resist the antibodies produced by somebody who's been vaccinated with the others. So someday there'll be four vaccines that are given for this.” If a question asks why a vaccinated patient still got Ebola, strain mismatch is the answer.

⚠️ One naming correction, repeated from Part 1

In the recording, the Ervebo vector is described as “vesicular stomatitis virus, which is a pox virus.” The virus is right and the family is not: vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) is a rhabdovirus — the same family as rabies — not a poxvirus. The same slip appeared in Part 1 when he described the Lassa vaccine candidates, which use the same VSV platform. Learn the vector as VSV, a rhabdovirus. The vaccine concept described is otherwise exactly right.

Prevention

  • Cut down wildlife→human transmission. In the lecturer's phrasing: don't hang around with bats, don't eat chimpanzees. This is where the initial spillover happens.
  • Reduce human→human transmission — “there's probably more of that than animal to human” once an outbreak has started.
  • Containment during outbreaks, which is often the hardest part in practice.

Q: A health care worker in eastern DRC develops fever, vomiting, diarrhea and, on day 7, a maculopapular rash beginning on the face. She received Ervebo two years ago. How is this possible, and what is the current outbreak strain?

Answer

Strain mismatch. Ervebo is a live attenuated recombinant VSV-vectored vaccine built against the Zaire glycoproteins, and the current outbreak strain is Bundibugyo, against which there is very little cross-protection. The same limitation applies to the two monoclonals, Inmazeb and Ebanga, which also target Zaire surface glycoproteins.

Manage with early supportive care and rehydration; experimental options are remdesivir (inhibits the viral RNA polymerase) plus pan-Ebola monoclonals. Confirm with antigen/antibody capture followed by RT-PCR. Bundibugyo-specific candidates are in trials from AstraZeneca (viral vector) and Moderna (mRNA).

Q: Why is a filovirus outbreak so much more destructive than its viral load alone would suggest?

Answer

Because of the extraordinarily wide host cell range, which is unusual for a virus. Ebola infects monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, hepatocytes, adrenal cells and epithelial cells. That single fact produces the entire syndrome: dendritic cell and macrophage infection gives the early immunosuppression and then the cytokine storm; endothelial infection gives the capillary leak and hemorrhage; hepatocyte infection gives the transaminase rise and loss of clotting factors; adrenal infection gives the hypotension and hypovolemia. The end result is a tissue factor and pro-inflammatory cytokine cascade that “starts to look like sepsis” — the same final common pathway as every agent in Part 1.

Q: A family in a rural DRC village prepares a relative's body for burial three days after he died of confirmed Ebola. Are they at risk, and is there an insect vector to worry about?

Answer

Yes, they are at significant risk — and no, there is no vector. Ebola persists in a corpse for some period after death, so handling the body carries the same body-fluid risk as caring for a living patient. This is precisely why funerary workers are named alongside health care workers as an at-risk group, and it is a documented driver of outbreak amplification.

On the vector: there is NO insect vector for any filovirus. Transmission is entirely by direct contact with blood, secretions, organs or other body fluids, or with fomites contaminated by them, entering through mucosal surfaces or small abrasions. Intact skin is protective, but the virus “can find a way in.”

LO 2 — Marburg virus: how it differs from Ebola

Virus 2 · Filoviridae

Marburg — the same disease, turned up

Marburg is a filovirus with the same bat, the same route, the same syndrome and the same rash as Ebola. Learn it as a short list of differences, not as a new disease. Three of those differences are the whole section: it bleeds more, it kills more, and there is nothing approved to give.

Slide 9 — Marburg virions — morphologically indistinguishable from Ebola.

💡 The name is the mnemonic — and it is the one filovirus not named after Africa

Every other name in this lecture is a place in Africa. Marburg is a university town in Germany.

The first known outbreak occurred there, in laboratory workers at the university who were working with African green monkey blood imported from Uganda. So the disease is African in origin and European in discovery — which is exactly the kind of detail a question uses to test whether you actually know the story or just pattern-matched on “African virus.”

Compare with Lassa fever from Part 1, where the first notable United States outbreak was in a Yale University research group. Laboratory-acquired infection is a recurring theme with these agents.

🧠 Why Marburg is deadlier — the mechanism, not just the number

The higher fatality is not arbitrary. Marburg more aggressively disables the immune system than Ebola does, in three specific ways the lecturer names:

  1. It blocks the interferon response.Type I interferon is the innate antiviral brake. Removing it early is the founding event of the whole Part 1 pathogenesis figure — and Marburg does it harder.
  2. It is much more likely to induce a massive cytokine storm.Higher viral burden → larger mediator surge → more endothelial activation, more disseminated intravascular coagulation, more shock.
  3. It destroys blood vessels.Which is the direct explanation for why hemorrhage is more common in Marburg than in Ebola.
🔑 “The second deadliest virus”

The lecturer's framing: Marburg is probably the second deadliest virus. The deadliest is rabies — “because once you start with symptoms, it's over. Nobody survives that.” Marburg is “pretty close, but you might have a chance.”

Recent outbreaks named in class: Rwanda (mostly 2024 into 2025), and this year Ethiopia and Uganda.

🩺 Why there is no drug — an honest aside about how medicines get made

“There's a certain break point for pharmaceutical houses in developing. If you're talking about something that's 25 cases for a half year, that's not going to stimulate their business acumen, and so they don't put Marburg on the top — but Marburg can be pretty dangerous.”

This is worth registering as more than a joke. The absence of a Marburg vaccine is an economic fact, not a scientific one — the same platform that produced Ervebo would very likely work. It is the reason “neglected tropical disease” is a category at all.

Q: A geologist returns from surveying a cave system in Uganda and develops fever, myalgia, and on day 6 a maculopapular rash, followed by prominent bleeding from the gums and venipuncture sites. What is the most likely agent, and how does treatment differ from Ebola?

Answer

Marburg virus. The tell is cave exposure — Marburg acquisition is specifically exposure to infected bats, especially in caves — combined with prominent hemorrhage, which is more common in Marburg than in Ebola. The reservoir is the same Egyptian rousette fruit bat.

Treatment differs completely by being absent. For Ebola you have two monoclonals (Inmazeb, Ebanga) and a licensed vaccine (Ervebo). For Marburg there are no approved monoclonals, no approved antivirals and no approved vaccine. Management is supportive care aimed at avoiding shock. Confirm with RT-PCR. Expect a fatality rate around 50%, or 88–90% in severe outbreaks.

Q: Name the three mechanisms by which Marburg disables host immunity more effectively than Ebola, and link each to a clinical consequence.

Answer

(1) It blocks the interferon response → loss of the innate antiviral brake → uncontrolled viral replication and a higher viral burden. (2) It is far more likely to induce a massive cytokine storm → endothelial and platelet activation, consumption of clotting factors, disseminated intravascular coagulation and distributive shock. (3) It destroys blood vessels → directly explains why hemorrhage is more common in Marburg than in Ebola.

Together these give the 50% fatality rate, rising to 88–90% in severe outbreaks — and they are the reason Marburg is described as the second deadliest virus after rabies.

LO 3 — Bartonella: cat scratch disease and bacillary angiomatosis

Bacterium 1 · Gram-negative coccobacillus

Bartonella — one bug, one cat, two completely different diseases

This section has an unusually clean structure: one organism, acquired from one animal, that produces two diseases which are told apart by a single variable — the state of the patient's immune system. Immunocompetent gets cat scratch disease. Immunocompromised gets bacillary angiomatosis. If you hold on to that one fork, the rest fills in.

It is cat scratch DISEASE. The lecturer corrects this in passing: “not cat scratch fever, by the way.” Cat Scratch Fever is a Ted Nugent song. Cat scratch disease is the diagnosis.

The cat, and how the bug gets from cat to you

Slide 14 — The whole section in one figure: one cat, one scratch, two diseases split by immune status.

Slide 15 — The cat flea life cycle and the role of contaminated flea feces.

  • The reservoir is cats — both domestic and feral. In the cat, infection may show as cough, loss of appetite, lethargy, breathing difficulties — or nothing at all.
  • Cat → cat transmission is by the CAT FLEA. This is why the flea matters even though it does not bite you.
  • Cat → human transmission is the classic scratch: the mechanism is inoculation of contaminated flea feces via a cat scratch. The claw carries flea dirt; the scratch drives it in.
  • It can also be transmitted by cat saliva, as during a bite.
🧠 The detail that makes the mechanism click

It is tempting to assume the bacterium lives on the cat's claws. It doesn't, really — the organism is in the FLEA FECES, which contaminates the cat's fur and claws during grooming and scratching. The cat's claw is the delivery needle, not the source. That is why flea control on the cat is genuine prevention, and why the CDC figure spends most of its space on the flea life cycle rather than on the cat.

Disease 1 — Cat scratch disease (immunocompetent)

Who

Demographics

Most often children under 10 — because “they're the ones who are always playing rough with the animal and they're more likely to get scratched.” Kittens are the most likely source.

How many

Epidemiology

25,000–30,000 reported cases per year in the United States — and probably twice that unreported, because mild cases resolve on their own.

What kind of disease

Category

Lymphocutaneous — a skin lesion plus the draining nodes. Dissemination is possible but uncommon.

Incubation

Timing

3–10 days — short. The scratch will still be recent enough for the patient to remember it.

🔑 The three stages, with their timings
  1. Stage 1 — cutaneous papule, which may pustulate.At the site of the scratch. Takes 3–14 days.
  2. Stage 2 — regional lymphadenopathy.Head, neck or upper extremities — i.e. draining the scratch. Nodes enlarge, become quite tender, may become draining, and some may pustulate. Takes 1–3 weeks.
  3. Stage 3 — systemic infection.Another 2–4 weeks before this appears, and in immunocompetent people it is not seen terribly often.

Accompanying features: low-grade fever in about half of patients, plus headache, anorexia, sore throat, skin rash and sometimes arthralgias.

Slide 17 — Regional lymphadenopathy in a child — the picture that names the disease.

🩺 Course and dissemination

Localized cat scratch disease is generally self-limiting — but slowly. It can take quite a while: on the order of 5–6 weeks for the whole illness.

Dissemination is fairly rare in the immunocompetent, but when it happens the organism can reach almost any organ: spleen, liver, with neurologic complications and ocular involvement also described.

Diagnosing cat scratch disease

Slide 19 — Warthin–Starry silver stain: the organisms take up the silver and go black.

  1. History of animal contact with a scratch— in conjunction with regional lymphadenopathy. This pairing is the diagnosis in most cases.
  2. Do NOT rely on culture.The organism is fastidious and takes weeks to grow. “Anything that takes 2 or 3 weeks to grow — forget it.”
  3. Serology: enzyme immunoassay (EIA/ELISA) for IgG and IgM, or indirect immunofluorescence assay.These are what actually get ordered.
  4. PCR of swollen lymph node material— this is the definitive diagnosis.
  5. Warthin–Starry silver stain of node tissue.May not be done often, “but it would show up on a USMLE test.”
🔑 Argyrophilic organisms — the silver-stain list

Argyrophilic means “silver-loving” — the organism has an affinity for silver and so is demonstrated by a silver stain such as Warthin–Starry. Only a handful of organisms do this, which makes it a clean board question. The ones named in class:

  • Bartonella
  • Spirochetes (e.g. Treponema)
  • Legionella
  • Helicobacter pylori

If a stem shows you a silver stain with black organisms, your differential is essentially this list plus the clinical context.

🩺 Treating cat scratch disease
  • Standard treatment: azithromycin.
  • Severe cases, especially in immunocompromised patients: azithromycin PLUS rifampin.
  • Be realistic about the benefit: treatment “basically is going to maybe shorten the period” of the illness, which otherwise runs 5–6 weeks.
  • Prognosis is very good.

Disease 2 — Bacillary angiomatosis (immunocompromised)

Bacillary angiomatosis (BA) is a proliferative disease of the small blood vessels of the skin and the viscera, usually following exposure to Bartonella henselae — the same organism, the same cats — in someone who is immunocompromised.

🧠 Who counts as immunocompromised here
  • Patients with AIDS — the classic setting
  • Patients on long-term chemotherapy
  • Recent transplant recipients on immunosuppressive drugs
  • “Plus anything else that will flatten the immune system”

The conceptual point: the same bacterium in a competent host produces a contained lymphocutaneous illness; in a compromised host it produces uncontrolled vascular proliferation. The organism did not change — the host did.

⚠️ The look-alike you must not miss

The lecturer says it directly: these lesions “look a little bit like Kaposi's” — “I've got pictures of people with Kaposi's that look very similar to this.”

This matters enormously, because both occur in AIDS patients and they look alike, but one is a treatable bacterial infection and the other is a vascular malignancy driven by human herpesvirus 8. You cannot tell them apart by looking — which is precisely why the diagnosis rests on lesion biopsy with histopathologic examination.

Slide 22 — It is not only skin: colonic bacillary angiomatosis on endoscopy.

  • Lesions begin on the skin but can reach the organs — the colonic lesions above are the lecturer's example.
  • Systemic symptoms: gastrointestinal upset, fevers, weight loss, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly.
🔑 Diagnosis — and why serology fails here
  • Lesion biopsy with histopathologic examination is the mainstay.
  • PCR is the central test.
  • Serology is supportive but not sensitive or definitive — and the reason is worth understanding rather than memorizing: you are dealing with people whose immune systems are impaired, so they may not be producing many antibodies. A test that looks for the host's antibody response will underperform in exactly the population that gets this disease.

Contrast with cat scratch disease in an immunocompetent child, where serology works fine. Same organism, different test strategy, because the host is different.

🩺 Treating bacillary angiomatosis
  • Erythromycin and/or doxycycline. (Note this differs from CSD, which gets azithromycin.)
  • If the patient is HIV-positive with a low CD4 count, start or optimize antiretroviral therapy (ART). The lecturer is emphatic: “you've got to get that immune system back up before it's going to take care of these organisms.” Antibiotics alone will not hold the line in a host who cannot help.
  • Prognosis is very good if treatment is started early — but the disease can be fatal if untreated, especially once there is organ involvement, where you risk organs breaking down.

Q: An 8-year-old is brought in with a tender 3 cm right cervical node and low-grade fever. Two weeks ago a new kitten scratched her right cheek; a small crusted papule is still visible there. What is the diagnosis, what is the actual infectious material, and what do you give?

Answer

Cat scratch disease, caused by Bartonella henselae. Everything fits: a child under 10, a kitten, an inoculation papule at the scratch site, and regional lymphadenopathy draining that site (head/neck) at the right interval — incubation is 3–10 days, the papule stage lasts 3–14 days and the nodal stage 1–3 weeks.

The infectious material is contaminated FLEA FECES, inoculated by the scratch — the claw is the needle, the flea dirt is the inoculum. Treat with azithromycin (add rifampin only if severe). Expect a self-limiting course of about 5–6 weeks with or without treatment, and a very good prognosis. Do not send a culture — Bartonella is fastidious and takes weeks; use EIA serology, or PCR of node material if you need definitive proof.

Q: A man with AIDS and a CD4 count of 40 has multiple friable cherry-red cutaneous papules and a nodular lesion on his forearm. Bartonella serology is negative. Does that exclude bacillary angiomatosis?

Answer

No — and the negative result is close to meaningless here. Serology detects the host's antibody response, and this patient's immune system is severely impaired, so he may not be producing much antibody at all. Serology in bacillary angiomatosis is described as supportive but neither sensitive nor definitive, for exactly this reason.

Proceed to lesion biopsy with histopathologic examination and PCR, which is the central test. The critical differential is Kaposi sarcoma, which looks very similar in the same population and cannot be distinguished clinically — one is a curable bacterial infection, the other a vascular malignancy.

Treat with erythromycin and/or doxycycline, AND start or optimize antiretroviral therapy, because you have to restore immune function for the antibiotics to hold. Untreated, and particularly with organ involvement, the disease can be fatal.

Q: A lymph node biopsy is stained with silver and shows clumps of black bacilli. Name the stain and the four organisms this finding should put on your differential.

Answer

The stain is the Warthin–Starry silver stain, and the organisms are argyrophilic — literally “silver-loving.” The short list named in class:

  • Bartonella — here, in a node after a cat scratch
  • Spirochetes
  • Legionella
  • Helicobacter pylori

The clinical context then picks the winner. A lymph node with a cat exposure is Bartonella; gastric mucosa is H. pylori; lung in a pneumonia is Legionella.

Where plague is now

🔑 The United States distribution — this is the examinable part
  • The plague bacillus is endemic in at least 17 states, mostly Pacific Coast and Western states.

The animals

Slide 28 — Rattus rattus, the black rat — the primary classic host. “The black rats, they like you.”

Slide 29 — Rattus norvegicus, the Norway or wood rat — competent, but less tolerant of humans.

🧠 “Peri-domestic” — and why the two rats behave differently

The classic hosts are peri-domestic rat populations, which “simply means rats that live close to humans but not with them — but they're around.”

The distinction between the two rats is entirely behavioural, and it is the same logic as Aedes aegypti versus albopictus in Part 1: the organism grows in both, but only one of them wants to be near you. Rattus rattus is the ship rat that runs up mooring ropes and lives in human buildings, and so it is the one that drove the pandemics. Rattus norvegicus is competent but keeps its distance.

Slide 30 — Black-tailed prairie dogs — a United States epizootic host, and a cautionary tale.

⚠️ The United States animals — and the prairie dog problem

Epidemics (properly, epizootics — outbreaks in animals) in this country have occurred in squirrels, prairie dogs and chipmunks.

Prairie dogs became a hot item for exotic pets, people were acquiring them from somewhere, and there were several human cases of plague from pet prairie dogs in Indiana and Missouri — states nowhere near the endemic west. The lecturer's advice, verbatim: “Don't get a prairie dog.”

The teaching point beneath the joke: the exotic pet trade moves a reservoir animal outside its endemic range, which breaks your geographic reasoning. A plague case in Indiana is not impossible; it just requires an unusual exposure history.

The fleas — and the most memorable mechanism in the lecture

Slide 31 — The rat flea, Xenopsylla cheopis — “named after a pharaoh.”

Slide 31 — The human flea, Pulex irritans — “because it irritates.”

🔑 The routes of transmission
  1. Bite of the rat flea, Xenopsylla cheopis — the principal route.The flea feeds on an infected rat, ingests the organism, the organism multiplies in the flea's gut, and it is transmitted at the next bite. “You can't be a vector unless the organism grows in you.”
  2. Bite of the human flea, Pulex irritans.Once plague was established in the human population this could also transmit it — not as effectively as the rat flea, but it could still do it. Yes, people had fleas.
  3. Inhalation of contaminated aerosolsfrom rodent waste.
  4. Handling infected animals — or handling infected people.
  5. Human → human: by flea vector, by contaminated body fluids, or by inhalation of contaminated aerosols (see pneumonic plague below).

Slide 32 — The blocked proventriculus: soft mass → partially soft → hard → extremely hard.

🧠 Why a starving flea is the most dangerous flea — the blocked proventriculus

This is genuinely elegant and worth understanding rather than memorizing, because it explains the entire epidemiology of the pandemics.

  1. The flea ingests Y. pestis in a blood meal.The organisms begin growing in the proventriculus — the valve between the flea's esophagus and its midgut.
  2. Over a short period the bacterial mass blocks it completely.The figure shows the progression from a soft mass to an “extremely hard mass.”
  3. Now the flea bites but gets nothing.No blood can pass the blockage. The flea is starving while full.
  4. So it bites frantically — and expands its host range.“In fact, they went crazy. They would just start biting everything… they started biting these hairless things that were running around.” Each failed feeding attempt regurgitates bacteria into the wound.

The consequence: the flea gets no nutrition from humans and dies, but in the process it inoculates far more hosts than a well-fed flea ever would. Blockage converts an efficient parasite into a desperate, indiscriminate transmission machine — and that is why humans, who are otherwise irrelevant to this organism, get bitten at all.

🔑 Humans are a DEAD-END host

“Humans for plague are a dead-end host. That's not meant to be a joke.” Human infection is accidental and unintended — it does nothing for the organism. Plague is a classic zoonosis: a disease primarily of animals, for which humans get in the way.

Hold this next to dengue and malaria, where humans ARE the reservoir. That contrast — incidental host versus reservoir host — is a clean discriminator across the whole two-part block.

The organism, and where it came from

Slide 33 — Bipolar “safety pin” staining — dark at both ends, pale in the middle.

🔑 Micro identity
  • Gram-negative rod with BIPOLAR STAINING — it takes up stain at both ends and not in the middle.
  • The classic description is a “safety pin” appearance. (The lecturer notes, fairly, that most of you have never used a safety pin.)
  • Demonstrated on Gram stain or Giemsa stain.
🧠 How a gut bug became the Black Death — two mutations

Y. pestis did not start out this way. It evolved from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis — an enterocolitis / gastrointestinal organism — through a series of step mutations somewhere between 1,500 and 20,000 years ago. Two adaptations were required, and each one is a slot in the seven-slot grid:

  1. The ability to grow in, and be transmitted by, the flea.Without this there is no vector, and without a vector this stays a gut infection.
  2. The ability to initiate systemic infection from a peripheral site.“How did a gut bug learn to — OK, you get implanted in the skin or in the limb, and now you're going to cause a systemic infection? That doesn't happen. I mean, you adapt.”

These two changes turn an ordinary enteric pathogen into a flea-borne systemic killer. It is a nice illustration that virulence is not a fixed property of a species — it is a set of acquired capabilities.

Pathophysiology — from bite to Black Death

  1. Rapid multiplication at the bite site.The organisms grow quickly where the flea fed.
  2. Spread to the regional lymph node → the BUBO.They reach the lymph node and cause intense hemorrhage and swelling. These swollen, exquisitely tender nodes are buboes — the word that gives bubonic plague its name.
  3. Into the blood → disseminate to essentially every organ.“Just about any organs that blood hits, which is all of them” — lungs, pericardium, meninges, kidneys.
  4. Extensive hemorrhage and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).The Part 1 endgame, arrived at by a bacterial route.
  5. Gangrene at the cool acral sites → “Black Death.”Fingers, toes, ears and nose — the coldest, most distal tissue, where microvascular thrombosis bites hardest. The result is the classic black-to-purple discoloration of the skin. That is literally where the name Black Death comes from.

⚠️ The insight about why the 14th century was so catastrophic

Most people picture the Black Death as buboes. The lecturer argues the pneumonic forms probably did most of the killing:

“This may be why the progress of the disease was so rapid in the 14th century, because people were getting infected, some of them were getting primary plague pneumonia, and then they were dispensing it directly to other people — and this would keep the body count very high… This probably killed more people than the standard plague, although what people remember was everybody rotting away. The people with plague pneumonia died in a couple of days.”

The chain to understand: bubonic plague → secondary plague pneumonia in that patient → they aerosolize the organism → the next person inhales it and gets primary plague pneumonia → which is 100% fatal untreated within days and is itself highly transmissible. A flea-borne disease becomes an airborne one, and that is the moment the epidemic curve goes vertical.

Diagnosis

  1. Suspect it in a febrile patient exposed to rodents in a known endemic area.In this country: California, Oregon, Arizona, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico are the peak areas.
  2. Get the right specimen.Aspirate of a lymph node if there are buboes; sputum in a pneumonic presentation.
  3. Demonstrate the organism.Look for the bipolar-staining Gram-negative rod on Gram or Giemsa stain.
  4. Culture on blood agarfor identification and antibiotic sensitivity. (You can run a panel of metabolic tests, but that takes too long to be useful acutely.)
  5. Rapid antigen test — the go-to.Rapid tests for Y. pestis antigen are 100% sensitive and 100% specific. “So that is really the go-to.”
  6. Confirm with PCR where available.

Treatment

🔑 Two drugs, one vaccine you can't get, and one red flag
  • Treatment is gentamicin or a quinolone.
  • You do not see a lot of drug resistance in naturally occurring plague.
  • ⚠ The red flag: “If you see a plague where you have very high resistance, you have to suspect something that's been altered by humans to be more resistant” — i.e. a deliberately engineered strain. Highly resistant plague is a bioterrorism signal, not a treatment-failure puzzle.
  • A vaccine exists, but it is not available in this country.

Q: A 30-year-old in rural New Mexico develops abrupt fever to 103 °F, chills and profound weakness, and has an exquisitely tender 5 cm mass in the right groin. He has been camping and handling dead ground squirrels. Aspirate shows Gram-negative rods that stain darkly at both ends. Diagnosis, form, and treatment?

Answer

Bubonic plague, caused by Yersinia pestis. The stem gives you everything: the western United States (endemic in ~17 mostly Pacific Coast and Western states), rodent exposure (squirrels are a named United States epizootic host alongside prairie dogs and chipmunks), abrupt high fever with chills and weakness, and a BUBO in the node draining a lower-limb flea bite. The stain is the confirmation: bipolar “safety pin” staining Gram-negative rods.

Treat with gentamicin or a quinolone. Get a rapid antigen test (100% sensitive and specific) and culture for sensitivities; confirm with PCR if available. Untreated bubonic plague carries ~50% mortality; treated, 1–15%. Watch for progression to secondary plague pneumonia — at which point he becomes infectious by aerosol.

Q: Why does a flea whose gut is completely blocked by bacteria transmit plague far more efficiently than a healthy flea?

Answer

Because Y. pestis multiplies in the flea's proventriculus until it forms a hard mass that occludes it. The flea can still bite, but no blood can pass the blockage, so it never feeds successfully — it is starving despite constant biting. Its response is to bite frantically and repeatedly, and to expand its normal host range onto animals it would usually ignore, including humans. Each failed attempt regurgitates bacteria into the wound.

So the blockage converts a well-adapted rat parasite into an indiscriminate, high-frequency inoculator. This is also the answer to why humans — a dead-end host that offers the organism nothing — get bitten at all.

Q: Distinguish primary from secondary plague pneumonia, and explain why the distinction drives infection control.

Answer

Primary plague pneumonia follows direct inhalation of the bacillus into the lungs. The incubation is very short — 1–3 days — with sudden high fever, chills, headache, tachypnea, dyspnea, hypoxia and hemoptysis, progressing to bilateral multilobar hemorrhagic consolidation with bloody frothy sputum.

Secondary plague pneumonia follows hematogenous spread from bubonic plague — organisms reach the lymph, then the blood, then are filtered through the lungs. It takes a couple of days longer to develop and is preceded by the usual bubonic picture.

Both carry ~50% mortality treated and 100% untreated, and both are transmissible by aerosol. That is the infection-control point: a patient with secondary plague pneumonia — who arrived with an ordinary bubonic presentation — can seed primary plague pneumonia in everyone around them. You do not need to be exposed to a “pneumonic plague” case to catch pneumonic plague; a bubonic case that progressed is enough.

Q: A cluster of plague cases shows high-level resistance to gentamicin and fluoroquinolones. What should this prompt?

Answer

Suspicion of a deliberately engineered strain — a bioterrorism concern — and immediate public health notification. Naturally occurring Y. pestis shows very little drug resistance, and gentamicin or a quinolone is reliably effective. As the lecturer puts it, high resistance means “you have to suspect something that's been altered by humans to be more resistant.” This is a recognized signal because plague has historically been developed as a biological weapon: rapid onset, high mortality, and aerosol transmissibility in the pneumonic form.

The lecturer opens with that line, and it is the whole geographic point. Babesiosis is a New England disease. Reservoir — and the most important mouse in New England

Slide 40 — Peromyscus leucopus, the white-footed mouse — “this is the guy that makes it all happen.”

🔑 Learn this mouse once and you get five diseases

The principal reservoir for Babesia is the white-footed mouse (Peromyscus leucopus), sometimes called the white-footed deer mouse. Birds, chipmunks and raccoons can also carry it, but the mouse is primary.

And here is the payoff for someone who does not enjoy micro — this single animal is the reservoir for a whole cluster of northeastern diseases:

  • Babesiosis (this lecture)
  • Lyme disease (Borrelia burgdorferi)
  • Anaplasmosis (Anaplasma phagocytophilum)
  • Powassan virus
  • Hantavirus

“There's probably more, but this is the guy that makes it all happen.” One mouse, one tick, five diseases — which is also why co-infection is common (below).

Vector — the deer tick, and specifically the nymph

Slide 41 — Ixodes scapularis life stages: adult female, adult male, nymph, larva. Note the size drop.

🧠 Why the tiny nymph — not the adult — is the one that infects you

Two facts combine, and they are exactly the same two facts as in Lyme disease:

  1. Ticks are NOT born infected.There is no transovarial transmission — an infected female does not pass the organism to her eggs. So every tick has to acquire the parasite by feeding. The nymph, the first stage to feed after moulting, gets it by feeding on infected white-footed mice.
  2. You cannot see a nymph.An adult tick walking on you is noticeable — “most people can tell when they've got a 4.5-foot-long tick walking on them.” A nymph is about the size of the dot over the “i” in newsprint. “They look like freckles — unless they're moving. Freckles don't move.”

So the nymph is the primary vector to humans, not because it is more infectious, but because it feeds long enough to transmit without being noticed and removed. This is why most patients have no history of a tick bite — a fact that then makes the diagnosis harder, as you will see.

Life cycle inside you

  1. The feeding nymph deposits about 100,000 sporozoites under the dermis.Just the act of feeding does this, because the organism has been multiplying inside the tick.
  2. Sporozoites enter the bloodstream and attach specifically to red blood cells.This is an intra-erythrocytic parasite — the same lifestyle as Plasmodium.
  3. Inside the red cell they differentiate into TROPHOZOITES — the adult stage.
  4. They replicate by BINARY FISSION (“not exciting”) to produce MEROZOITES.
  5. Maturation to merozoites LYSES the red blood cell — and not just one, a whole population of them.
  6. Released merozoites infect new red blood cells, and the cycle continues.
🔑 Where the symptoms come from — two sources, and both matter
  • Hemolysis of red blood cells causes the fever — and the hemolytic anemia.
  • Production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which “contribute heavily to the symptoms of the disease.”

If that sounds familiar, it should: it is the same two-source model as every hemorrhagic fever in Part 1 — direct cell destruction plus a disproportionate host inflammatory response. Plasmodium works the same way.

⚠️ Co-infection — the point that makes this clinically important

Co-infection with Anaplasma phagocytophilum or Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme disease) is common.

The reason is structural rather than coincidental: all three organisms use the same reservoir (the white-footed mouse) and the same vector (the Ixodes scapularis nymph). One bite can deliver more than one pathogen. Practically: a patient in New England who is not getting better on doxycycline for Lyme disease may have babesiosis as well — and doxycycline does not treat Babesia.

Diagnosis

  1. Suspect it in a patient with typical manifestations plus recent exposure to an endemic area.A history of tick bite would help — but most of the time people do not know they were bitten, because the nymph is invisible. “They may come to that conclusion afterwards, but they have no evidence of it.” Do not require a tick-bite history to make the diagnosis.

  • Azithromycin PLUS atovaquone — the standard combination.
  • Atovaquone targets the parasite's electron transport system — that is the mechanism to be able to state.
  • In severe cases, go intravenous with at least the azithromycin.

Q: A 72-year-old on Nantucket in July develops a week of fever, chills, fatigue and myalgia. Hemoglobin 9.1 g/dL, platelets 88,000/µL, AST and ALT mildly elevated, haptoglobin undetectable. He recalls no tick bite. Diagnosis, and how would you prove it?

Answer

Babesiosis (Babesia microti). The stem is a checklist: coastal New England in summer, a flu-like illness, and the lab triad of hemolytic anemia (low hemoglobin, undetectable haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia and transaminase elevation. His age puts him in the severe-disease group alongside immunocompromised patients.

The absence of a tick-bite history is expected and does not argue against the diagnosis — the vector is the Ixodes scapularis NYMPH, which is the size of a printed full stop and looks like a freckle.

Prove it with a Giemsa-stained blood smear looking for trophozoites and merozoites inside red cells — ring forms; PCR if available. Treat with azithromycin plus atovaquone, intravenously if severe. Also test for Borrelia burgdorferi and Anaplasma phagocytophilum, because they share the reservoir and the vector and co-infection is common.

Q: Explain why the nymph, not the adult tick, is the principal vector of both babesiosis and Lyme disease.

Answer

Two reasons, and they compound.

First, ticks are not born infected — there is no transovarial transmission from an infected female to her eggs. Each tick must acquire the organism by feeding, and the nymph is the first stage to feed after moulting, taking its meal from the infected white-footed mouse (Peromyscus leucopus). So the nymph is the first stage that is both infected and hungry.

Second, and decisively, you cannot see a nymph. It is about the size of the dot over an “i” and is easily mistaken for a freckle, so it stays attached long enough to transmit. An adult tick is conspicuous and gets removed. Transmission is therefore a function of going unnoticed, not of biological potency — which is exactly why most patients report no tick bite at all.

Plasmodium — malaria, and the clock in the fever

Malaria is the largest disease in this lecture by every measure — cases, deaths, and historical toll — and it is also the one with the most moving parts. The good news, straight from the lecturer when a student asked whether all the species need differentiating: “there's only two of them” that really matter, and he wants you to know how malaria WORKS rather than to memorize the genus.

Where

Geography

Tropical and subtropical regions — sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, Oceania, Latin and South America.

🩺 Malaria was endemic in every American state except Alaska

This is the historical fact the lecturer most wants to land, because it dismantles the assumption that malaria is somebody else's problem.

  • Up until the 1880s, malaria was considered endemic in every state except Alaska. Philadelphia. Minnesota. Anywhere.
  • It was probably not native — it appears to have arrived with European settlers and West African slaves. The first American epidemic was at Jamestown in 1607.
  • Washington, Lincoln and Grant all had malaria, acquired domestically. Sessions of the Continental Congress were stopped by malaria outbreaks.
  • The Columbus aside: some writings suggest Columbus and his crew may have contracted malaria during the voyage — but they could not have brought it to the Americas, because the mosquitoes could not survive the cold of the crossing and so could not have transmitted on arrival. (The crew did, however, bring syphilis back.)

Transmission

  • Essentially human-to-human transmission, via the bite of the female Anopheles mosquito. As with dengue in Part 1, humans are the reservoir — contrast with plague, where humans are a dead-end host.
  • There are more than 40 species of Anopheles, and most of them can transmit it — so unlike dengue, where Aedes aegypti is singled out, you do not pick on one particular species here. Genus is enough.
  • Mosquitoes are not born infected. They pick it up while feeding on an infected human, the parasite develops inside them to the sporozoite stage, and the next bite transmits it. Same vector rule as the flea and the tick.

The life cycle — two stages, and the second one is the disease

Slide 46 — The full Plasmodium life cycle, numbered.

Slide 45 — The same cycle, split into pre-erythrocytic, erythrocytic and mosquito/sexual stages.

🧠 Follow it once, in order — this is the whole disease
  1. The female Anopheles bites and deposits SPOROZOITES into the dermis.“It's very much like the Babesia, by the way” — the same deposit-at-the-bite-site mechanism.
  2. Sporozoites enter the blood and lymphatics and reach the LIVER.This is the exo-erythrocytic (pre-erythrocytic) stage.
  3. They invade hepatocytes and form SCHIZONTS.Each schizont contains thousands upon thousands of MEROZOITES.
  4. The hepatocyte lyses and releases merozoites— which infect more hepatocytes, and eventually pour into the blood.
  5. Merozoites attach to RED BLOOD CELLS and transform into RING FORMS.This begins the erythrocytic stage — the stage that makes you sick.
  6. Ring → trophozoite → schizont → new merozoites → lyse the red cell → infect new red cells.A cyclic process. The synchrony of this lysis is what sets the fever interval.
  7. Some parasites become gametocytes, which a feeding mosquito ingests— closing the loop and infecting the next mosquito.

On top of the red cell destruction, the process causes release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which “also contribute to the manifestations.” Same two-source model as Babesia.

🔑 The one structural difference from Babesia, and it matters

Both parasites are deposited at a bite site and both end up multiplying inside red blood cells producing ring forms. The difference is the liver.

Plasmodium has an obligatory pre-erythrocytic LIVER stage, where a single sporozoite amplifies into thousands of merozoites before the blood stage even begins. Babesia goes straight from the dermis to the red blood cell — no liver stage at all.

That liver stage is also where the vaccine attacks (see below), and it is why malaria has an incubation of 1–4 weeks — the liver phase is silent.

Clinical

  • Incubation 1–4 weeks.
  • Chills, rigors and fever at regular intervals — per the species table above — with sweating between.
  • Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and jaundice. These occur at intervals, driven by the waves of red cell lysis.
  • Severe disease: infected red cells can cause infarcts and capillary leakage → organ dysfunction in the liver, kidneys, respiratory system and even neurologically (cerebral malaria) → severe anemia, disseminated intravascular coagulation, shock.
  • In some cases patients go into shock and die within 24 hours.

As the lecturer notes wearily: “severe anemia, DIC — the story is over and over. It's the same thing for all of these guys.” That is the point of Parts 1 and 2 taken together: the final common pathway is always the same. The front end — the animal, the vector, the geography — is what differs.

Diagnosis

Slide 48 — Thick smear (A) versus thin smear (B). The difference is whether the red cells are still there.

🔑 Thick versus thin — the classic, and it is genuinely easy once you see why
🌕

THICK smear

The red blood cells are PRE-LYSED, so the parasites are released and concentrated onto the slide.

  • Purpose: a SCREEN — “kind of a presence/absence test. Are they there? There they are.”
  • More sensitive, because you are looking through far more blood per field.
  • You cannot tell which species, because the cell context is gone.
🌑

THIN smear

The parasites are visualized WITHIN intact red blood cells.

  • Purpose: IDENTIFY the parasite type and estimate parasite density.
  • Preserved cell morphology lets you speciate.
  • Less sensitive — fewer parasites per field.

Both use a Giemsa stain, both “are very nice, but it takes a lot of time and you need somebody who's fairly proficient at doing this quickly.”

Mnemonic: thick = there or not; thin = which one.

  • Suspect malaria in a febrile patient with characteristic symptoms and epidemiologic exposure.
  • Rapid diagnostic tests detect parasite antigens, run in about 10 minutes, and are much easier for everybody — especially in resource-limited areas. This matters practically: “a lot of places you're going to see malaria are going to be in resource-limited areas where you don't have maybe even microscopes,” let alone PCR.
  • PCR where available.

Treatment

Slide 49 — How chloroquine kills the parasite: it blocks the detoxification of heme.

🧠 The chloroquine mechanism — a genuinely satisfying piece of biochemistry
  1. The parasite eats hemoglobin.It breaks hemoglobin down; the globin is used by the parasite as an amino acid source.
  2. That leaves free HEME — which is highly TOXIC to the parasite.It has created its own poison as a by-product of feeding.
  3. So it detoxifies it: HEME POLYMERASE converts toxic heme into non-toxic HEMOZOIN.“All right, that's clever.”
  4. Chloroquine, quinine and mefloquine all block that step.Heme accumulates to concentrations that are toxic to the parasite, and it dies.

Notice the elegance: the drug does not attack the parasite directly at all. It disables the parasite's own waste-disposal system and lets the parasite poison itself.

🔑 Take the name apart and the whole vaccine explains itself
  • Recombinant. P. falciparum surface proteins are produced in yeast cells — Saccharomyces cerevisiae.
  • Combined with hepatitis B surface protein, which acts purely as a CARRIER protein. The lecturer is emphatic that this is incidental: “Nothing special about the hepatitis B itself. It's not part of it. It's just something that is a side issue.” It is scaffolding, not a target.
  • Plus an adjuvant: AS01.
  • It targets the SPOROZOITE stage. The antibodies it raises are specific for sporozoites — so they neutralize the parasite in the bloodstream before it reaches the liver, cutting the life cycle at step 2. “You stop them before they get to the liver.”
🩺 The adjuvant — and why Shingrix hurts

AS01 is a combination of two things:

  • An extract derived from the endotoxin / outer lipopolysaccharide of Salmonella, and
  • A saponin from the Chilean soapbark tree.

“Mix that together and that's a great adjuvant. It hurts like hell though.” This is the same adjuvant family used in Shingrix, and it is why most people have trouble with the Shingrix vaccine — the reactogenicity is the adjuvant, not the antigen. A nice piece of cross-referencing: the same immunological trick shows up in two unrelated vaccines.

⚠️ A figure worth checking before you quote it

In class the protection from RTS,S/AS01 was given as “about 80 to 90% protection, so it's a pretty good vaccine.” That is the number stated in the lecture, so it is what to reproduce if the exam asks what he said.

Be aware, though, that the published phase 3 trial efficacy is substantially lower — on the order of ~30–50% against clinical malaria in the first year, declining thereafter, which is why the vaccine is deployed as one part of a package alongside bed nets and chemoprevention rather than as a stand-alone solution. If a board question asks about RTS,S efficacy, the lower figure is the defensible one. Flagging the discrepancy rather than quietly picking one.

Q: A traveller returns from Nigeria and develops fever with rigors and sweats occurring without any discernible pattern, plus jaundice and confusion. A thick smear is positive. What species is most likely, why is the fever irregular, and what does the confusion signify?

Answer

Plasmodium falciparum — the most common and most virulent species, and the expected answer for sub-Saharan Africa.

The fever is irregular because falciparum's erythrocytic cycles are not synchronized. Fever spikes are produced by waves of red blood cell lysis releasing merozoites; when those waves are synchronous you get the clean 24-, 48- or 72-hour periodicity of knowlesi, vivax/ovale and malariae. Falciparum lacks that synchrony, so there is no rhythm and no fever-free recovery window.

The confusion signifies severe malaria with neurologic involvement — cerebral malaria. In severe disease, infected red cells cause infarcts and capillary leakage producing organ dysfunction (liver, renal, respiratory, neurologic), along with severe anemia, DIC and shock; patients can die within 24 hours. This is a medical emergency. The thick smear told you parasites are present; now get a THIN smear to confirm the species and quantify the parasite density.

Q: Explain precisely how chloroquine kills Plasmodium.

Answer

The parasite digests hemoglobin inside the red cell and uses the globin portion. This liberates free heme, which is highly toxic to the parasite itself. To survive its own metabolism, the parasite uses heme polymerase to convert toxic heme into inert hemozoin.

Chloroquine — along with quinine and mefloquine — inhibits that conversion. Heme therefore accumulates to toxic concentrations and kills the parasite. The drug never attacks the organism directly; it disables the detoxification step and lets the parasite poison itself. Where chloroquine resistance is present, use artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) such as artemether–lumefantrine.

Q: The malaria vaccine RTS,S/AS01 raises antibodies against the sporozoite. Why is that the stage chosen, and what is the hepatitis B surface protein doing in it?

Answer

The sporozoite is the stage chosen because it is the bottleneck. A mosquito deposits sporozoites into the dermis, and they must reach hepatocytes before the infection amplifies — a single sporozoite becomes a schizont containing thousands of merozoites. Neutralizing antibody at the sporozoite stage stops the parasite in the blood before it reaches the liver, cutting the cycle before any amplification occurs. Once the erythrocytic stage begins, the parasite burden is orders of magnitude larger.

The hepatitis B surface protein is purely a CARRIER protein. The P. falciparum surface antigens are produced recombinantly in yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and fused to it so they assemble and present well to the immune system. It is not itself a target and confers nothing relevant to malaria — “nothing special about the hepatitis B itself… it's just a side issue.” The AS01 adjuvant (Salmonella LPS-derived component plus a Chilean soapbark saponin) supplies the immune stimulation — and the sore arm.

Q: Two patients each have ring-form parasites inside their red blood cells on a Giemsa-stained smear. Patient A hikes in Rhode Island and has never left the country. Patient B returned from Ghana three weeks ago. Name each organism and give one further test for each.

Answer

Patient A: Babesia microti. Southern New England is squarely in the endemic zone; the vector is the Ixodes scapularis nymph and the reservoir the white-footed mouse. No travel is needed — this is a domestically acquired infection. Further test: PCR, plus serology for Borrelia burgdorferi and Anaplasma phagocytophilum, since all three share that reservoir and vector and co-infection is common. Treat with azithromycin + atovaquone.

Patient B: Plasmodium, most likely falciparum — sub-Saharan Africa, incubation within the 1–4 week window, and falciparum is both the most common and the most virulent. Further test: a THIN smear to establish the species and the parasite density (the thick smear only tells you parasites are present), or a rapid antigen test. Treat with chloroquine if sensitive, otherwise artemisinin-based combination therapy.

Q: Both plague and malaria are transmitted by an arthropod, but humans occupy opposite positions in the two life cycles. Explain the difference and one practical consequence of it.

Answer

In plague, humans are a DEAD-END host. The organism is maintained in rodents; human infection is accidental and does nothing for the organism. It is a classic zoonosis — “a disease primarily of animals, for which humans get in the way.”

In malaria, humans ARE the reservoir. Transmission is human→mosquito→human, with no animal amplifier; the mosquito acquires the parasite by feeding on an infected person.

The practical consequence is what control looks like. For plague, you cannot eradicate the reservoir — wild rodents across 17 states — so control means reducing human contact with rodents and their fleas, and treating the rare case. For malaria, every infection is a link in a human chain, so treating infected people, vector control and vaccination all reduce onward transmission in a way they never could for plague. Dengue in Part 1 follows the malaria pattern; Crimean–Congo and Lassa follow the plague pattern.

LO 1–2 · FILOVIRUSES

The strategy

  • Seven slots per organism: bug type · reservoir · vector · geography · signature finding · test · drug.
  • The stem MUST give you the geography or the exposure. Read for those first.
  • “You can't be a vector unless the organism grows in you” — and no vector is born infected.

Filoviridae — family

  • Filo = filament. ~80 nm wide × up to 800 nm long (0.8 µm, nearly an E. coli). Shapes: circular, straight, or a “6”.
  • ssRNA. Enter via mucosal surfaces / small abrasions. NO INSECT VECTOR.
  • Extraordinarily wide host cell range: monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, endothelium, fibroblasts, hepatocytes, adrenal cells, epithelium.
  • Triggers tissue factor + pro-inflammatory cytokine cascade → “looks like sepsis.”

Ebola

  • 4 strains: Zaire (most virulent), Sudan, Taï Forest, Bundibugyo (current outbreak).
  • Central & West Africa — DRC. Reservoir Egyptian fruit (rousette) bat — not sick. Also chimps, gorillas, monkeys, antelope, porcupines, pigs.
  • Human→human: body fluids, FOMITES, and CORPSES (virus persists after death → funeral workers + health care workers at risk). Semen → sexual transmission.
  • Fatality 25–90% by strain. Current DRC outbreak ~6,300 cases, ~48% died; Uganda <50.
  • Rash day 6–7, maculopapular, starts on the FACE → neck/upper trunk → limbs. Not diagnostic. Harder to see on darker skin.
  • Dx: antigen/antibody capture → confirm RT-PCR (often unavailable in the field).
  • Tx: supportive/rehydration + Inmazeb or Ebanga (monoclonals vs Zaire glycoprotein) ± remdesivir (inhibits viral RNA polymerase).
  • Vaccine: Ervebo — live attenuated recombinant VSV vector (VSV glycoprotein genes swapped for Ebola's), CDC 2020, Zaire only.
  • ⚠ Very little cross-protection vs Bundibugyo — this is why current cases fail treatment. AstraZeneca (vector) + Moderna (mRNA) Bundibugyo candidates in trials.
  • ⚠ Correction: lecture calls VSV “a pox virus.” VSV is a RHABDOVIRUS (rabies family).

Marburg — learn only the differences

  • Same family, same bat, same routes, same rash.
  • Named for Marburg, GERMANY — first outbreak in lab workers handling African green monkey blood from Uganda.
  • Acquisition: bats, especially in CAVES.
  • ⚠ MORE hemorrhage than Ebola. Fatality 50%, 88–90% severe. Second deadliest virus after RABIES.
  • Why: blocks the interferon response, massive cytokine storm, destroys blood vessels.
  • Outbreaks uncommon/sporadic — ~25 cases this year (Rwanda 2024–25; Ethiopia, Uganda).
  • Dx RT-PCR. Tx SUPPORTIVE ONLY — NO approved monoclonal, antiviral or vaccine.
LO 3–4 · BARTONELLA & PLAGUE

Bartonella — the setup

  • G(−) COCCOBACILLUS, pleomorphic, fastidious. ⚠ Bartonella ≠ Bordetella.
  • Board coccobacilli: Bartonella · Haemophilus · Bordetella.
  • B. bacilliformis → Oroya fever (South America). B. quintana → trench fever (WWI), culture-negative endocarditis, sometimes BA. B. henselae → CSD + BA, most common.
  • Reservoir: cats. Cat→cat by CAT FLEA. Cat→human by inoculation of contaminated FLEA FECES via a scratch (claw = needle, flea dirt = inoculum), or by saliva in a bite.
  • The fork: immunocompetent → CSD. Immunocompromised → BA.

Cat scratch disease

  • Children <10; kittens. 25–30K reported cases/yr, probably 2× that. Lymphocutaneous. Incubation 3–10 d.
  • Papule → pustule (3–14 d) → tender REGIONAL LYMPHADENOPATHY head/neck/upper extremity (1–3 wk) → systemic (2–4 wk more, rare if immunocompetent). Low-grade fever in ~half.
  • Self-limiting over 5–6 weeks. Rare dissemination: spleen, liver, neuro, ocular.
  • Dx: history + node; EIA/ELISA IgG & IgM; PCR of node = definitive; WARTHIN–STARRY SILVER STAIN. ⚠ DO NOT culture — fastidious, takes weeks.
  • Argyrophilic (silver-loving): Bartonella · spirochetes · Legionella · H. pylori.
  • Tx: AZITHROMYCIN (+ rifampin if severe). Prognosis very good.

Bacillary angiomatosis

  • Vascular proliferation of small vessels, skin AND viscera, in the immunocompromised (AIDS, chemo, transplant).
  • Cherry-red papules/nodules — ⚠ MIMICS KAPOSI SARCOMA, cannot be told apart clinically. Also GI upset, fever, weight loss, hepato/splenomegaly.
  • Dx: lesion BIOPSY + histopathology; PCR. ⚠ Serology unreliable — the host isn't making antibody.
  • Tx: ERYTHROMYCIN and/or DOXYCYCLINE + ART if HIV+ — must restore immunity. Fatal untreated with organ involvement.

Plague — Yersinia pestis

  • G(−) rod, BIPOLAR “SAFETY PIN” staining (Gram or Giemsa). Evolved from Y. pseudotuberculosis (a GI bug) via 2 mutations: grow in the flea + cause systemic infection from a peripheral site.
  • 3 pandemics: Justinian 541–549 (~50M/8 yr) · Black Death 1347–1770s (~200M in 7 yr, ~1/3 of the population) · 1850s–today.
  • Endemic in ≥17 WESTERN US states (NM, CO, CA, TX, AZ, OR, NV). ~150 US cases since 2000.
  • Reservoir: peri-domestic rats — R. rattus (black rat, likes humans) > R. norvegicus. US epizootics: squirrels, prairie dogs, chipmunks.
  • Vector: RAT FLEA Xenopsylla cheopis (also Pulex irritans, less efficient). Also aerosols from rodent waste, handling animals/people.
  • HUMANS ARE A DEAD-END HOST.
  • ⚠ BLOCKED PROVENTRICULUS: bacteria mass and occlude the flea's foregut → it can't feed, bites frantically, expands its host range to humans, regurgitating bacteria each time. A starving flea is the dangerous flea.
  • Bite → local lymph node = BUBO → blood → all organs → hemorrhage + DIC → ACRAL GANGRENE (fingers, toes, ears, nose) = “Black Death.”

Plague — forms, Dx, Tx

  • Bubonic: follows flea bite; abrupt fever/chills/weakness + bubo. 1–15% treated / 50% untreated.
  • Primary plague pneumonia: direct INHALATION, incubation 1–3 d; hemoptysis, bloody frothy sputum, bilateral multilobar hemorrhagic consolidation. 50% / 100%.
  • Secondary plague pneumonia: hematogenous from bubonic; also aerosol-transmissible → seeds primary pneumonia in others. 50% / 100%. Death in 3–4 d untreated.
  • Septicemic: fever 103–104 °F, often NO bubo, rapid MOF + shock. 50% / 100%.
  • Dx: node aspirate or sputum → bipolar rods; RAPID ANTIGEN TEST = 100% sensitive AND specific; culture for sensitivities; PCR.
  • Tx: GENTAMICIN or a QUINOLONE. Vaccine exists but not available in the US.
  • ⚠ High-level resistance → suspect a human-engineered strain (bioterrorism).
LO 5–6 · PARASITES

Babesiosis — Babesia microti

  • Protozoan. NORTHEAST US + upper Midwest. 2,000–3,000 cases/yr reported = at best 40% of reality. Trophozoite only 1–2 µm.
  • Reservoir: WHITE-FOOTED MOUSE (Peromyscus leucopus) — also reservoir for Lyme, anaplasmosis, Powassan, hantavirus. One mouse, five diseases.
  • Vector: Ixodes scapularis (deer tick) — the NYMPH. No transovarial transmission, so the nymph must acquire it by feeding on the mouse; and you can't see a nymph (“the dot over the i” — looks like a freckle). → Most patients report NO tick bite.
  • Nymph deposits ~100,000 sporozoites → RBC → trophozoite → BINARY FISSION → merozoites → lyse RBC → infect new RBCs. NO liver stage.
  • Symptoms from hemolysis + pro-inflammatory cytokines. Incubation 1–4 wk.
  • ≥40% asymptomatic; most others flu-like. Severe in ELDERLY / IMMUNOCOMPROMISED: severe GI, ARDS, DIC, hemolytic anemia, shock, CHF.
  • ⚠ Co-infection with Borrelia burgdorferi or Anaplasma is common — same mouse, same tick. Doxycycline does not treat Babesia.
  • Dx: GIEMSA smear — trophozoites/merozoites (RING FORMS) inside RBCs; PCR. Labs: hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, ↑AST/ALT, ↑BUN/Cr.
  • Tx: AZITHROMYCIN + ATOVAQUONE (atovaquone hits the parasite's electron transport system); IV if severe.

Malaria — Plasmodium

  • ~300 million cases/yr, ~600,000 deaths/yr. Historical toll ~5 billion (TB ~1 billion).
  • Species: falciparum (most common AND most virulent), vivax, then ovale, malariae, knowlesi. He only wants falciparum + vivax.
  • FEVER INTERVALS (= the synchronized RBC lysis cycle):
    falciparum = IRREGULAR (unsynchronized → most dangerous) · knowlesi 24 h · vivax + ovale 48 h · malariae 72 h.
  • HUMANS ARE THE RESERVOIR. Vector female Anopheles (>40 species, genus is enough). Mosquitoes acquire it from infected humans.
  • Endemic in every US state except ALASKA until the 1880s. First epidemic Jamestown 1607; Washington, Lincoln, Grant all had it.
  • LIFE CYCLE: mosquito deposits SPOROZOITES in dermis → blood/lymph → LIVER, invade hepatocytes → SCHIZONTS full of MEROZOITES (exo-erythrocytic) → lysis → blood → RBC: RING → trophozoite → schizont → merozoites → lyse RBC (erythrocytic, cyclic) → gametocytes taken up by mosquito.
  • ⚠ vs Babesia: Plasmodium HAS an obligatory LIVER stage; Babesia does not.
  • Severe: infarcts + capillary leak → liver/renal/respiratory/cerebral dysfunction, severe anemia, DIC, shock; can die in 24 h.
  • Dx — GIEMSA: THICK smear = RBCs pre-lysed = SCREEN, is it there (more sensitive, no speciation). THIN smear = parasites inside intact RBCs = SPECIES + DENSITY. Also rapid antigen test (~10 min), PCR.
  • Tx: CHLOROQUINE if sensitive; ACT (artemether–lumefantrine) if resistant.
  • Chloroquine MOA: parasite digests Hgb → uses globin → leaves TOXIC HEME → heme polymerase converts it to non-toxic HEMOZOIN. Chloroquine/quinine/mefloquine BLOCK that step → heme accumulates → parasite poisons itself.
  • Vaccine RTS,S/AS01 (Mosquirix): recombinant falciparum surface protein made in yeast (S. cerevisiae) + hep B surface protein as a CARRIER only + AS01 adjuvant (Salmonella LPS component + Chilean soapbark — same family as Shingrix, hence the sore arm). Targets the SPOROZOITE — stops it before the liver. Lecture said 80–90% protection; published trial efficacy is substantially lower.

⚠ The four fastest discriminators

  • Rings in RBCs + New England = Babesia. Rings in RBCs + tropical travel = malaria.
  • Cat flea = Bartonella. Rat flea = plague.
  • Human = dead-end host in plague; human = RESERVOIR in malaria.
  • Ebola has drugs and a vaccine. Marburg has nothing.
