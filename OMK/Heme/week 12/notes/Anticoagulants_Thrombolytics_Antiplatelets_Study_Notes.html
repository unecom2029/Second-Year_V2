🔑 DOAC and NOAC mean the same thing

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), sometimes called NOACs (novel or non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants), are: dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban. Note that this group crosses two of the deck's sections — dabigatran is a direct thrombin inhibitor (section 2) while the other three are direct factor Xa inhibitors (section 4). Warfarin is not a DOAC.

🧠 Direct versus indirect — the single most useful distinction on this page

Indirect agents (heparin, enoxaparin, fondaparinux) do nothing on their own: they require antithrombin as a cofactor and work by accelerating it. Direct agents (bivalirudin, argatroban, dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban) bind the clotting factor's active site themselves and act independently of antithrombin. That one difference drives the reversal agents, the monitoring, and why the direct agents work on clot-bound as well as free enzyme.

Intrinsic: XII → XI → IX+Extrinsic: tissue factor → VII→FACTOR Xa→prothrombin (II)→THROMBIN (IIa)→fibrinogen (I)→fibrin (Ia)→XIIIa cross-links→FIBRIN CLOT
  • Heparin (unfractionated) hits both Xa and IIa. LMWH hits mainly Xa. Fondaparinux hits Xa only.
  • Direct thrombin inhibitors hit IIa. Direct oral factor Xa inhibitors hit Xa.
  • Warfarin removes factors II, VII, IX and X from the board entirely by blocking their γ-carboxylation.
  • Thrombolytics act at the very end — they convert plasminogen to plasmin, which chews up fibrin that is already there.

Q: A student says “dabigatran and apixaban are both DOACs, so they have the same mechanism.” What is wrong with that?

Answer

They are both direct oral anticoagulants, but they hit different targets. Dabigatran etexilate is a direct thrombin (factor IIa) inhibitor — a prodrug converted in vivo to active dabigatran, which binds the thrombin active site. Apixaban is a direct factor Xa inhibitor, binding factor Xa one step upstream. The practical consequences differ too: idarucizumab specifically reverses dabigatran, and dabigatran is renally cleared (avoid when creatinine clearance ≤ 30 mL/min).

Mechanism of action — chain length decides the target

Slide 10 — Heparin and related products: mechanism of action

  1. Unfractionated heparin (UFH). Long chains bind antithrombin and bridge thrombin, so both thrombin (IIa) and factor Xa are inhibited. The bridge is a physical requirement — you need enough chain to hold antithrombin and thrombin together at once.
  2. Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH). Activates antithrombin the same way, but the chains are too short to bridge thrombin — inhibition is mainly of factor Xa.
  3. Fondaparinux. The synthetic pentasaccharide alone; activates antithrombin for selective factor Xa inhibition with no thrombin bridging.
🧠 One sentence for the whole mechanism

All three drugs act indirectly through antithrombin — chain length decides whether thrombin (IIa) is also inhibited. The antithrombin-binding pentasaccharide sequence is common to all three; the sulfate groups shown in red on the structure are the ones required for antithrombin binding. What differs is only how much chain is left over to reach thrombin.

HIT / HITT — the mechanism, in four steps

Slide 12 — Mechanism of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia / thrombosis

  1. Heparin binds platelet factor 4 (PF4), forming a heparin–PF4 complex.
  2. IgG antibodies recognise the complex, creating circulating immune complexes.
  3. Fc receptors activate platelets — platelets release procoagulant microparticles.
  4. Platelet removal and platelet activation together produce thrombocytopenia (a falling platelet count) plus thrombosis, which is paradoxical.
☐ What HIT/HITT does to the patient

Acute limb ischaemia — pain, pallor, cool mottled skin, pulselessness, paresthesia and paralysis of the affected limb — leading to limb gangrene.

Early onset within 24 hours of exposure in a previously sensitised patient; delayed onset even after heparin is stopped.

Avoid oral warfarin monotherapy during HIT/HITT — it can promote venous thrombosis progression, gangrene development and skin necrosis.

🧠 Why warfarin monotherapy is catastrophic here

Warfarin blocks γ-carboxylation of protein C as well as of factors II, VII, IX and X — and protein C has a half-life of only 8 hours, far shorter than factor II's 60 hours. So the first effect of warfarin is to remove the body's anticoagulant before it removes the procoagulants: a transient hypercoagulable state. Superimpose that on the intensely prothrombotic state of HIT and you get gangrene and skin necrosis.

HIT / HITT — recognise, stop, treat

Slide 13 — Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia / thrombosis: monitoring & management

🔍

Monitoring

  • Baseline platelet count before heparin; recheck every 2–3 days from day 4 to day 14 in higher-risk patients
  • Suspect HIT with a fall in platelets of more than 50% from baseline, typically 5–10 days after exposure
  • Score clinical probability with the 4Ts
  • Confirm with the anti-PF4 (heparin-induced platelet antibody) test — an immunologic enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) detecting IgG antibodies against the platelet factor 4 / heparin complex
  • Verify positives with a functional serotonin release assay (SRA), which measures heparin-dependent platelet activation
  • Examine for new venous or arterial thrombosis, limb ischaemia, and skin lesions at injection sites
🛑

Management

  • Stop all heparin immediately — including LMWH, flushes, and heparin-bonded catheters
  • Start a non-heparin anticoagulant — argatroban, bivalirudin or fondaparinux. Observation alone is not enough
  • Avoid prophylactic platelet transfusion unless the patient is bleeding
  • Delay warfarin until platelets recover (> 150,000/µL), then overlap at least 5 days. NEVER use warfarin monotherapy during HIT/HITT
  • Continue anticoagulation about 4 weeks without thrombosis and 3 months with thrombosis; document the reaction
🔑 The 4Ts score

0–2 points each; 6–8 = high, 4–5 = intermediate, 0–3 = low probability.

  • Thrombocytopenia — extent of the platelet fall
  • Timing of the fall — days 5–10
  • Thrombosis or other sequelae
  • oTher causes of thrombocytopenia absent

Q: On day 7 of unfractionated heparin, a patient's platelet count falls from 240,000 to 90,000/µL and a new deep vein thrombosis is found in the opposite leg. What is the diagnosis, what do you stop, and what do you start?

Answer

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia with thrombosis (HITT). The 4Ts are all satisfied: a fall of more than 50% from baseline, timing at days 5–10, new thrombosis, and no other cause. Stop all heparin — including low-molecular-weight heparin, flushes, and heparin-bonded catheters. Start a non-heparin anticoagulant: argatroban, bivalirudin or fondaparinux; observation alone is not enough. Confirm with the anti-PF4 ELISA and verify with the serotonin release assay. Do not give a prophylactic platelet transfusion unless the patient is bleeding, and do not start warfarin until platelets recover above 150,000/µL — then overlap at least 5 days. Continue anticoagulation about 3 months because thrombosis is present.

Q: Why does unfractionated heparin prolong the aPTT while enoxaparin generally does not?

Answer

The activated partial thromboplastin time is sensitive to thrombin (factor IIa) inhibition. Unfractionated heparin's long chains bridge antithrombin to thrombin, inhibiting both IIa and Xa, so the aPTT is prolonged and can be used to monitor. Enoxaparin's chains are too short to bridge thrombin, so it inhibits mainly factor Xa and leaves the aPTT largely untouched — which is why routine coagulation monitoring is not required for LMWH and, when monitoring is needed (renal impairment, obesity, pregnancy), you measure anti-factor Xa instead.

Q: A patient on long-term unfractionated heparin develops a potassium of 5.9 mmol/L with normal renal function. Why?

Answer

Unfractionated heparin inhibits aldosterone synthesis, producing hyperkalaemia. It is listed on the adverse-effects slide alongside osteoporosis with prolonged unfractionated heparin therapy — the two “also monitor for” items that are easy to forget because they are not bleeding and not HIT.

Direct Thrombin Inhibitors

Three drugs, two routes, and one organising idea: they bind and directly inhibit thrombin (factor IIa) and they act independently of antithrombin. That independence is exactly why two of them are the drugs of choice in heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.

Bivalirudin

Parenteral (intravenous)

A modified form of hirudin, a protein in medicinal leech (Hirudo medicinalis) saliva.

Renally cleared — dosage reduction recommended in renal impairment.

Argatroban

Parenteral (intravenous)

A synthetic compound based on the structure of L-arginine.

Metabolised in the liver — dosage reduction recommended in hepatic impairment.

Dabigatran etexilate

Oral — the only one

Administered as a prodrug, converted to active dabigatran.

Renal impairment: avoid when creatinine clearance ≤ 30 mL/min. Hepatic impairment: no dosage adjustments in labelling; use caution in moderate to severe impairment.

🔑 Dose by the organ that clears the drug

Renal clearance: bivalirudin and dabigatran. Hepatic clearance: argatroban. This is the deck's own summary line and it is the fastest way to answer a dose-adjustment question. A patient in renal failure who needs a direct thrombin inhibitor gets argatroban.

Mechanism of action

Slide 22 — Direct thrombin inhibitors: mechanism of action

💉

Parenteral direct thrombin inhibitors

  1. Given parenterally (intravenously) — acts directly, with no antithrombin cofactor required
  2. Binds the active site of thrombin; bivalirudin also engages exosite 1 on the fibrinogen-binding groove
  3. Inhibits both free circulating and clot-bound thrombin
  4. Fibrinogen is not converted to fibrin and thrombin-driven platelet activation stops
💊

Oral direct thrombin inhibitor

  1. Dabigatran etexilate is absorbed orally as an inactive prodrug
  2. Esterases convert it in vivo to active dabigatran
  3. Dabigatran binds the thrombin active site directly — free and fibrin-bound
  4. Fibrin formation and thrombin-mediated platelet activation are reduced
🧠 “Clot-bound thrombin” is the advantage over heparin

Thrombin already trapped inside a formed clot is inaccessible to the heparin–antithrombin complex, which is too bulky to reach it — so heparin cannot stop a clot propagating from within. Direct thrombin inhibitors bind free and clot-bound thrombin, which is why they are used during percutaneous coronary intervention and in HIT.

💊 Dabigatran and the P-glycoprotein efflux pump

Dabigatran is a P-glycoprotein substrate, so its drug interactions run through that transporter — the same pump you met as the multi-drug resistance mechanism in the antineoplastic lecture.

  • P-glycoprotein inhibitors (raise dabigatran levels → bleeding): amiodarone, clarithromycin, cycloserine, diltiazem, erythromycin, ketoconazole, itraconazole, quinidine, verapamil
  • P-glycoprotein inducers (lower dabigatran levels → clotting): saquinavir, nelfinavir, indinavir, amprenavir, rifampicin, nifedipine, dexamethasone, carbamazepine, trazodone

The identical two lists apply to the direct oral factor Xa inhibitors in Section 4 — learn them once.

🔑 Idarucizumab — the specific dabigatran antidote
  • A humanised monoclonal antibody fragment (Fab) that binds dabigatran and its acylglucuronide metabolites with ~350 times greater affinity than thrombin, neutralising the anticoagulant effect within minutes.
  • Haemodialysis — roughly 57% removed over 4 hours, though supporting data are limited. (This works because dabigatran is renally cleared and poorly protein bound.)
  • Recombinant factor VIIa or prothrombin complex concentrates (PCC) — an unproven, off-label option in life-threatening dabigatran-associated bleeding.
⚠️ Pregnancy

Parenteral direct thrombin inhibitors: limit use in pregnancy to patients who have severe allergic reactions to heparin, including HIT/HITT.
Dabigatran etexilate: use during pregnancy is not recommended. Use other agents besides dabigatran for atrial fibrillation or venous thromboembolism in pregnancy.

Q: A patient with HIT and a creatinine clearance of 18 mL/min needs parenteral anticoagulation. Which direct thrombin inhibitor, and why?

Answer

Argatroban — it is metabolised in the liver, so severe renal impairment does not accumulate the drug (dose-reduce for hepatic impairment instead). Bivalirudin and dabigatran are renally cleared; dabigatran should be avoided outright when creatinine clearance ≤ 30 mL/min, and it is oral, not parenteral. Argatroban is also the agent whose primary labelled indication is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Remember that argatroban affects the INR, which matters when the patient is later transitioned to warfarin.

Q: A patient on dabigatran for atrial fibrillation is started on clarithromycin for pneumonia. What is the risk?

Answer

Bleeding. Clarithromycin is a P-glycoprotein efflux pump inhibitor, so it reduces efflux of dabigatran and raises its plasma concentration. The same applies to amiodarone, cycloserine, diltiazem, erythromycin, ketoconazole, itraconazole, quinidine and verapamil. The mirror-image risk — underanticoagulation and thrombosis — comes from P-glycoprotein inducers such as rifampicin, carbamazepine, dexamethasone, nifedipine, trazodone and the protease inhibitors.

Q: A patient on dabigatran presents with life-threatening gastrointestinal bleeding. What is the specific reversal agent, and what is the backup?

Answer

Idarucizumab (Praxbind®) — a humanised monoclonal antibody Fab fragment that binds dabigatran with ~350 times greater affinity than thrombin and neutralises the effect within minutes. Backups: haemodialysis (~57% removed over 4 hours) and, off-label and unproven, recombinant factor VIIa or prothrombin complex concentrates. Note there is no reversal agent at all for intravenous bivalirudin or argatroban — you stop the infusion and rely on their short half-lives.

Mechanism of action

Slide 31 — Warfarin: mechanism of action and the vitamin K cycle

  1. Stereochemistry. In the racemic mixture of S- and R-enantiomers, S-warfarin is more active.
  2. Vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1 (VKORC1) inhibition. By blocking VKORC1, warfarin inhibits conversion of oxidised vitamin K epoxide into its reduced form, vitamin K hydroquinone → this inhibits vitamin K-dependent γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX and X, because reduced vitamin K is the cofactor for a γ-glutamyl carboxylase that catalyses γ-carboxylation, converting prozymogens into zymogens.
  3. CYP metabolism. S-warfarin is metabolised by CYP2C9; R-warfarin by other CYP450s such as CYP3A4.
  4. Dose variability. Inhibition or induction of CYP450 enzymes and VKORC1 will change the dose of warfarin required.
🔑 The mnemonic for the affected factors: 1972

Warfarin depletes the vitamin K-dependent factors: II, VII, IX and X — and also the vitamin K-dependent anticoagulants protein C and protein S. The classic memory aid is “1972” for factors 10, 9, 7, 2.

LO 3 — why the onset is slow and why you must bridge

  • Anticoagulation effect generally occurs within 24 hours, but the therapeutic effect is delayed for about five days — because of the half-lives of the clotting factors already circulating.

🧠 This is the whole answer to LO 3

The INR rises within a day because factor VII (half-life 4–6 hours) disappears fast — but factor VII is not what protects the patient from thrombus propagation. Factor II, with a 60-hour half-life, is, and it takes about five days to deplete. Meanwhile protein C (8 hours) has already gone, creating a transient hypercoagulable state. Therefore you bridge with heparin or enoxaparin until the INR has been therapeutic for an adequate overlap. An early therapeutic INR on warfarin alone is a trap, not a result.

LO 4a — Pharmacogenomics

Slide 35 — Warfarin: CYP2C9 and VKORC1 polymorphisms

  • Two genes, CYP2C9 and VKORC1, account for most of the genetic contribution to variability in warfarin response.
  • CYP2C9 metabolises warfarin. The *1/*1 wild type predominates in Caucasians (70%) and African Americans (90%), while *2 and *3 variants are far less common in Asians.
  • CYP2C9 *2 and *3 carriers clear warfarin more slowly and need lower doses — dose reductions of about 22% for *1/*2 up to 76% for *3/*3.
  • VKORC1 is the target of warfarin; the non-A/A and A/A forms require dose reductions of roughly 26% and 50%.
🔑 Two genes, two different reasons

CYP2C9 is pharmacokinetic — it changes how fast you clear the drug (variant carriers clear it slowly, so they need less). VKORC1 is pharmacodynamic — it changes how sensitive the target is (A/A carriers need roughly half the dose). A question asking you to classify the interaction type is asking exactly this.

💊 Food and drink
  • Drug/food: keep vitamin K intake consistent — kale, spinach, brussels sprouts, collard greens, mustard greens, turnip greens, swiss chard, broccoli, asparagus, seaweed. The instruction is consistency, not avoidance.

🧠 Cholestyramine is the trick entry

It is the one agent that appears in both columns. It binds bile acids in the gut and therefore impairs absorption of both fat-soluble vitamin K (which would raise the INR) and warfarin itself (which would lower it). If a question offers cholestyramine as an answer for a single direction of INR change, look for a better option.

⚠️ Three route rules on one slide

Q: A patient started on warfarin alone for a new deep vein thrombosis has an INR of 2.4 on day 2. Is the patient adequately anticoagulated? Explain.

Answer

No. The early INR rise reflects loss of factor VII (half-life 4–6 hours), which the prothrombin time is most sensitive to — but antithrombotic protection requires depletion of factor II (prothrombin), whose half-life is 60 hours, taking about five days. Worse, protein C (half-life 8 hours) has already fallen, producing a transient hypercoagulable state. The patient needs bridging with heparin or enoxaparin until the INR has been therapeutic over an adequate overlap.

Q: Classify these three warfarin interactions as pharmacokinetic or pharmacodynamic, and state the direction of INR change: (a) rifampin, (b) high-dose aspirin, (c) fluconazole.

Answer

(a) Rifampin — pharmacokinetic, DECREASES prothrombin time/INR by inducing CYP450 and accelerating warfarin metabolism (same group as barbiturates and carbamazepine). (b) High-dose aspirin — pharmacodynamic, INCREASES bleeding risk by an additive antihaemostatic effect (same group as cefotetan); aspirin also displaces warfarin from albumin, raising the free fraction. (c) Fluconazole — pharmacokinetic, INCREASES prothrombin time/INR; it stereoselectively inhibits oxidative metabolism of the more active S-warfarin enantiomer, as does metronidazole.

Q: A pregnant woman with a mechanical mitral valve asks whether she must stop warfarin. What do you tell her?

Answer

Warfarin is contraindicated in pregnancy because it crosses the placenta and is teratogenic — spontaneous abortion, fetal haemorrhage and fetal death may occur. The one stated exception is patients with mechanical heart valves at high risk for thromboembolism, where the maternal risk of valve thrombosis may outweigh the fetal risk. This decision is individualised and specialist-led; for essentially every other indication in pregnancy, low-molecular-weight heparin is the anticoagulant of choice. Note her target INR is also higher: 2.5–3.5 for high-risk mechanical prosthetic valves rather than 2–3.

Mechanism of action

Slide 43 — Direct oral factor Xa inhibitors: mechanism of action

  1. Absorbed orally and binds factor Xa directly, with no antithrombin cofactor required.
  2. Occupies the active site of both free and clot-bound (prothrombinase) factor Xa.
  3. Blocks conversion of prothrombin (factor II) to thrombin (factor IIa).
  4. Less thrombin is generated, so less fibrinogen is converted to fibrin.
  5. Reduced fibrin formation and thrombin-mediated platelet activation limits clot growth.
🔑 One step upstream of the direct thrombin inhibitors

Both classes are direct (no antithrombin needed) and both work on free and clot-bound enzyme. The difference is only which enzyme: factor Xa for apixaban/rivaroxaban/edoxaban, thrombin (IIa) for dabigatran. Because factor Xa sits upstream, inhibiting it stops thrombin ever being made rather than blocking thrombin after the fact.

Q: A 58-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation refuses warfarin because she does not want frequent blood draws. She is started on apixaban. What must you counsel her about stopping the drug suddenly?

Answer

Premature discontinuation increases the risk of stroke — it is one of the two US boxed warnings for this class (the other being spinal/epidural haematoma). Because these agents have a rapid onset and a short duration, anticoagulant protection is lost quickly, and the patient reverts to her untreated thromboembolic risk within a day. She must take it every day and must not stop without a plan for bridging or an alternative agent.

Q: Why does edoxaban carry a warning against use in nonvalvular atrial fibrillation when creatinine clearance exceeds 95 mL/min?

Answer

Because patients with supranormal renal clearance eliminate edoxaban too rapidly, producing subtherapeutic drug levels and, in the pivotal trial, an increased risk of ischaemic stroke compared with warfarin. It is the only entry in this lecture where good renal function is a contraindication — every other renal caution in the deck runs the other way (avoid dabigatran when creatinine clearance ≤ 30 mL/min; avoid or dose-adjust the factor Xa inhibitors in severe renal impairment).

Everything before this section prevented a clot. These drugs dissolve one that is already there — and because they dissolve fibrin without asking whether it is pathological or haemostatic, the limiting toxicity is bleeding into the brain.

Mechanism of action

Slide 52 — Thrombolytic drugs: mechanism of action

  1. Alteplase, reteplase and tenecteplase are given intravenously and act as tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA).
  2. They bind fibrin within the clot and convert clot-bound plasminogen into active plasmin.
  3. Fibrin binding focuses plasmin generation at the thrombus rather than in circulating blood.
  4. Plasmin cleaves the fibrin mesh, breaking it into fibrin degradation (split) products.
  5. The thrombus dissolves and vessel flow is restored; endogenous antiplasmins limit systemic lysis.
🧠 The antifibrinolytics are on the same diagram, pointing the other way

Aminocaproic acid and tranexamic acid inhibit the plasminogen→plasmin step. That is why they are the adjunctive agents used to reverse thrombolytic toxicity, and why they also appear as adjunctive therapy for factor Xa inhibitor bleeding. One diagram, both directions.

  4. Factor replacement (primary reversal): replace fibrinogen with cryoprecipitate.
  5. Give an antifibrinolytic — tranexamic acid or aminocaproic acid — until bleeding is controlled.

🔑 Cryoprecipitate is the primary reversal — not fresh-frozen plasma

Thrombolytics work by generating plasmin, and plasmin consumes fibrinogen. So the specific fix is to replace fibrinogen with cryoprecipitate, then shut off further plasmin generation with an antifibrinolytic (tranexamic acid or aminocaproic acid). Fresh-frozen plasma and prothrombin complex concentrate are added as needed, not the first move. Contrast with warfarin, where vitamin K is step 2 and prothrombin complex concentrate is step 3.

Q: A 55-year-old man is brought to the emergency department 2 hours after the onset of severe chest pain. ECG shows ST elevation and cardiac enzymes confirm myocardial infarction. The decision is made to open the occluded artery. Which of the following accelerates the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin — argatroban, heparin, reteplase, or warfarin?

Answer

Reteplase. It is a tissue plasminogen activator: it binds fibrin within the clot and converts clot-bound plasminogen into active plasmin, which cleaves the fibrin mesh into fibrin degradation products. Argatroban is a direct thrombin inhibitor, heparin is an indirect thrombin/Xa inhibitor working through antithrombin, and warfarin blocks vitamin K-dependent γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX and X — none of them touch plasminogen. Note also that reteplase's labelled indication here is exactly acute STEMI.

Q: Thirty minutes after alteplase for acute ischaemic stroke, a patient becomes unresponsive with a blown pupil. What are your first four steps?

Answer

Assume intracerebral haemorrhage, the feared complication. (1) Stop the drug and hold all other antithrombotics. (2) Send emergent labs — complete blood count, prothrombin time/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, and type and cross-match. (3) Obtain an emergent non-contrast head CT. (4) Replace fibrinogen with cryoprecipitate as the primary reversal, then give tranexamic acid or aminocaproic acid, adding platelets and factor correction as needed with urgent haematology and neurosurgery input, alongside supportive management of blood pressure, cerebral perfusion pressure, intracranial pressure and mean arterial pressure.

Q: A patient presents with STEMI 90 minutes after symptom onset. They had an ischaemic stroke 6 weeks ago. The hospital has no catheterisation laboratory. Can you give tenecteplase?

Answer

No. Ischaemic stroke within 3 months is an absolute contraindication to fibrinolytic therapy for STEMI. (The exception on the list applies only to an acute ischaemic stroke within 3 hours, which is itself the indication for thrombolysis — not to a stroke 6 weeks old.) The correct move is emergency transfer to a PCI-capable centre; primary percutaneous coronary intervention is in any case the preferred reperfusion strategy, with thrombolysis reserved for centres without PCI capability and no absolute contraindication.

Mechanism — four steps of platelet activation, four drug targets

Slide 61 — Antiplatelet drugs: platelet activation and antiplatelet targets

  1. Adhesion — platelets bind the injured wall via GP Ia to collagen and GP Ib to von Willebrand factor.
    Target: the GP Ib / collagen step is upstream of all current agents — nothing in the lecture blocks it.
  2. Activation and release — degranulation releases ADP, thromboxane A2 (TXA2) and 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) while thrombin is generated at the injury site.
    Targets: aspirin (COX-1 / TXA2); ADP receptor antagonists (P2Y12); vorapaxar (PAR-1).
  3. Amplification — ADP, thromboxane A2 and thrombin signalling lowers cyclic AMP and drives a conformational change in GP IIb/IIIa.
    Target: cilostazol and dipyridamole raise cyclic AMP / cyclic GMP by phosphodiesterase inhibition.
  4. Aggregation — activated GP IIb/IIIa binds fibrinogen, cross-linking platelets into a growing thrombus.
    Target: tirofiban and eptifibatide (GP IIb/IIIa blockade).
🔑 Two mechanism details the deck spells out

ADP receptor antagonists (P2Y12): clopidogrel bisulfate, prasugrel, ticagrelor, cangrelor block the P2Y12 component of the ADP receptor, so released ADP can no longer activate GP IIb/IIIa. Clopidogrel and prasugrel bind irreversibly; ticagrelor and cangrelor reversibly.
PAR-1 antagonist: vorapaxar antagonises protease-activated receptor-1, the platelet thrombin receptor; it inhibits thrombin-induced and TRAP-induced aggregation, with a long-lasting effect.

🧠 Why GP IIb/IIIa blockade is the most complete

Steps 2 and 3 are signals; step 4 is the act. Aspirin removes one agonist (thromboxane A2), a P2Y12 blocker removes another (ADP), vorapaxar removes a third (thrombin) — but the platelet can still be activated by whichever agonists remain. Blocking GP IIb/IIIa blocks the final common pathway: whatever activated the platelet, it cannot cross-link fibrinogen. That is why these are the most potent and why bleeding and thrombocytopenia are their toxicities.

Q: A patient with a recent drug-eluting stent on clopidogrel is prescribed omeprazole for reflux and six weeks later presents with stent thrombosis. What is the pharmacological explanation?

Answer

Clopidogrel is a prodrug that requires CYP2C19 for conversion to its active metabolite. Omeprazole inhibits CYP2C19, so the patient was effectively unprotected. The same enzyme underlies clopidogrel's US boxed warning: diminished antiplatelet effect in patients with two loss-of-function alleles of the CYP2C19 gene. Reasonable alternatives are a proton pump inhibitor with less CYP2C19 inhibition, or switching to prasugrel or ticagrelor, which do not depend on CYP2C19 activation in the same way.

Q: A patient with peripheral arterial disease and intermittent claudication also has an ejection fraction of 30%. Why can you not give cilostazol?

Answer

Cilostazol carries a US boxed warning and is contraindicated in heart failure of any severity. Phosphodiesterase III inhibitors increase mortality in heart failure. Cilostazol's indication is exactly this patient's symptom — intermittent claudication — which makes it a tempting distractor. Vorapaxar is an option for peripheral arterial disease (provided there is no history of stroke, transient ischaemic attack or intracranial haemorrhage), as is aspirin or a P2Y12 inhibitor.

Q: A patient on ticagrelor after acute coronary syndrome is also taking aspirin 325 mg daily. What is wrong?

Answer

Ticagrelor carries a US boxed warning that maintenance aspirin doses above 100 mg daily reduce its effectiveness and should be avoided. The aspirin dose must be reduced to ≤ 100 mg daily. Note this is the opposite direction of the usual interaction worry — the patient is not at extra bleeding risk from the combination so much as at risk of losing the ticagrelor effect they are relying on.

Q: Which antiplatelet class blocks the final common pathway of aggregation, and what is the practical consequence before cardiac surgery?

Answer

The glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors — eptifibatide and tirofiban. Activated GP IIb/IIIa is what actually binds fibrinogen to cross-link platelets, so blocking it prevents aggregation regardless of which agonist activated the platelet. Practical consequence: discontinue at least 4 hours prior to coronary artery bypass graft surgery. Their other listed toxicities are bleeding and thrombocytopenia.

Q: Rank protamine's usefulness across unfractionated heparin, enoxaparin and fondaparinux, and explain the pattern.

Answer

Complete for unfractionated heparin · partial for enoxaparin (LMWH) · none for fondaparinux. Protamine is a positively charged protein that binds the long, negatively charged heparin chains and neutralises them. As chain length falls (15,000 → 5,000 → 1,500 daltons) there is progressively less for protamine to bind — and fondaparinux is only the pentasaccharide, too small to be neutralised at all. The reversal gradient tracks the molecular-weight gradient exactly.

Q: A patient on warfarin needs urgent surgery tomorrow with an INR of 4.2 and no bleeding. Walk through the reversal ladder.

Answer

(1) Stop the drug. (2) Vitamin K — oral, or intravenous by slow infusion; not subcutaneous (variable absorption), not intramuscular (haematoma risk), and never IV push (anaphylaxis). (3) Unactivated 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (e.g. Kcentra) if reversal must be faster than vitamin K allows. (4) Fresh-frozen plasma. For a non-bleeding patient with a surgery tomorrow, steps 1 and 2 are usually sufficient; prothrombin complex concentrate is reserved for urgent or bleeding situations.

Case Study & Practice Questions

Case study

💊 Presentation

A 25-year-old woman presents to the emergency department with acute onset of shortness of breath and pleuritic pain. She had been well until 2 days prior, when she noted her left leg was swollen and red. Her only medication was oral contraceptives. Family history was significant for “blood clots” in multiple members of the maternal side. Examination shows an anxious woman with stable vital signs; the left lower extremity is erythematous, oedematous and tender. Oxygen saturation on room air is 87% (normal > 90%). Ultrasound reveals a deep vein thrombosis in the left lower extremity; chest computed tomography confirms pulmonary emboli. Laboratory tests show an elevated D-dimer.

Q1: What therapy is indicated acutely?

Answer

Immediate parenteral anticoagulation. She is haemodynamically stable (stable vital signs), so this is a submassive rather than massive pulmonary embolism — thrombolysis is not first-line; it is reserved for acute massive pulmonary embolism with instability. Start low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin) or unfractionated heparin, both of which carry the indication for DVT and PE treatment. A direct oral factor Xa inhibitor — apixaban or rivaroxaban — is an acceptable single-drug alternative, since both are labelled for treatment of DVT or PE. Note edoxaban would require 5–10 days of initial parenteral therapy first. Supportive oxygen for the saturation of 87%.

Q2: What are the long-term therapy options?

Answer

An oral anticoagulant. Direct oral factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban) or dabigatran are attractive in a 25-year-old: rapid onset, no dietary vitamin K effect, fewer interactions, predictable response, no routine monitoring. Warfarin is the alternative, with an INR target of 2–3, bridged initially with heparin because of the ~5-day delay and the transient hypercoagulable state from early protein C depletion. The strong family history raises the question of an inherited thrombophilia, which — with an unprovoked-appearing event — pushes toward extended-duration therapy; rivaroxaban specifically carries an indication for reducing recurrence after ≥ 6 months of initial full therapeutic anticoagulation.

Q3: Should this individual use oral contraceptives?

Answer

No. Combined oral contraceptives are a prothrombotic exposure and were almost certainly a contributor here, alongside a probable inherited thrombophilia suggested by the maternal family history of clots. They should be stopped and a non-oestrogen-containing contraceptive method substituted. This also matters for her anticoagulant choice: if she may become pregnant, remember that warfarin and the direct oral anticoagulants are contraindicated or not recommended in pregnancy, and low-molecular-weight heparin is the agent of choice.

The lecture's five practice questions

Question #1: A 55-year-old lawyer is brought to the emergency department 2 hours after the onset of severe chest pain during a stressful meeting. He has poorly controlled mild hypertension and elevated cholesterol but does not smoke. ECG changes (ST elevation) and cardiac enzymes confirm myocardial infarction. The decision is made to attempt to open his occluded artery. Which of the following drugs accelerates the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin? A Argatroban · B Heparin · C Reteplase · D Warfarin

Answer — C, Reteplase

Reteplase is a tissue plasminogen activator: it binds fibrin in the clot and converts clot-bound plasminogen to active plasmin, which degrades the fibrin mesh. Argatroban directly inhibits thrombin; heparin accelerates antithrombin to inhibit thrombin and factor Xa; warfarin blocks vitamin K-dependent γ-carboxylation of factors II, VII, IX and X. Only the thrombolytic touches plasminogen — and reteplase's labelled indication is precisely acute STEMI.

Question #2 — warfarin drug interactions. Which of the following is the most likely explanation for the observed changes in warfarin concentration? A Drug B displaces warfarin from plasma proteins; drug C displaces warfarin from tissue-binding sites · B Drug B inhibits hepatic metabolism of warfarin; drug C displaces drug B from tissue-binding sites · C Drug B stimulates hepatic metabolism of warfarin; drug C displaces warfarin from plasma protein · D Drug B increases renal clearance of warfarin; drug C inhibits hepatic metabolism of drug B

Answer — reason it from the two interaction mechanisms

The two mechanisms this lecture teaches are: (i) CYP450 inhibition or induction — amiodarone, cimetidine, disulfiram, macrolides, fluconazole, metronidazole and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole inhibit and raise the INR; barbiturates, rifampin and carbamazepine induce and lower it; and (ii) displacement from albumin — warfarin is ~99% protein bound and only unbound drug is active, so aspirin, sulfonamides and phenytoin raise the free fraction. Option B is the answer that combines a genuine hepatic-metabolism effect on warfarin with a displacement effect, matching both taught mechanisms; options invoking increased renal clearance of warfarin are wrong because warfarin is excreted as inactive metabolites after hepatic metabolism, not cleared renally as active drug.

Question #3: A 58-year-old woman with chronic hypertension and diabetes was recently admitted for congestive heart failure and new-onset atrial fibrillation. Echocardiogram shows an ejection fraction of 40% with no valvular abnormalities. Her CHADS2 score indicates she requires anticoagulation. She does not want frequent blood draws, so you start apixaban. You counsel her that suddenly stopping the medication can lead to: A Excess bleeding · B Increase in INR · C Stroke · D Thrombocytopenia

Answer — C, Stroke

Premature discontinuation is one of the two US boxed warnings for the direct oral factor Xa inhibitors (the other is spinal/epidural haematoma). These agents have a rapid onset and short duration, so protection is lost within about a day and the patient reverts to her untreated risk of stroke and systemic embolism from nonvalvular atrial fibrillation — the very indication she was started for. Note the vignette specifies no valvular abnormalities, which is what makes a direct oral anticoagulant appropriate; with a mechanical valve these agents are contraindicated and warfarin is required.

Question #4: A 67-year-old woman with a deep vein thrombosis was started on enoxaparin and warfarin. Enoxaparin was discontinued after a week and she continued warfarin. Two months later she returns after a severe nosebleed; the INR is 7 (target 2–3). To prevent severe haemorrhage, warfarin should be discontinued and she should be treated immediately with: A Andexanet alfa · B Idarucizumab · C Protamine · D Vitamin K

Answer — D, Vitamin K

Vitamin K is the specific reversal agent for warfarin, given orally or by slow intravenous infusion — never subcutaneously (variable absorption), intramuscularly (haematoma) or as an IV push (anaphylaxis). Andexanet alfa reversed the direct oral factor Xa inhibitors and has in any case been withdrawn from the market. Idarucizumab is specific to dabigatran. Protamine reverses heparin — and her enoxaparin was stopped two months ago. If reversal needed to be faster, the next rungs are unactivated 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate and then fresh-frozen plasma.

Question #5: A patient develops severe heparin-induced thrombocytopenia on unfractionated heparin and still requires parenteral anticoagulation. The patient is most likely to be treated with which of the following? Note: more than one answer may apply. A Argatroban · B Enoxaparin · C Bivalirudin · D Vitamin K

Answer — A and C, Argatroban and Bivalirudin

Both are parenteral direct thrombin inhibitors that act independently of antithrombin and are not heparins, so they do not cross-react with the heparin–PF4 antibody. Argatroban's labelled indication is HIT; bivalirudin is used off-label for HITT. Enoxaparin is wrong — management requires stopping all heparin, including low-molecular-weight heparin, flushes and heparin-bonded catheters. Vitamin K is wrong — it is a warfarin reversal agent, not an anticoagulant, and warfarin monotherapy in active HIT is explicitly dangerous because early protein C depletion promotes venous thrombosis progression, gangrene and skin necrosis. Fondaparinux is the third acceptable non-heparin option named in the management slide.

LO 1–4 · ANTICOAGULANTS

Direct vs indirect — the master split

  • INDIRECT (heparin, enoxaparin, fondaparinux): need antithrombin as cofactor
  • DIRECT (bivalirudin, argatroban, dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban): bind the enzyme themselves, work on free AND clot-bound
  • DOAC = NOAC = dabigatran + rivaroxaban + apixaban + edoxaban. Warfarin is NOT a DOAC

Chain length decides the target

  • UFH 15,000 Da → bridges thrombin → IIa + Xa
  • LMWH 5,000 Da → too short to bridge → mainly Xa
  • Fondaparinux 1,500 Da → pentasaccharide only → Xa only
  • Bioavailability 30% → 90% → 100%; t½ 1–8 h → 4 h → 17 h
  • Protamine: complete → partial → NONE
  • Thrombocytopenia: <5% → <1% → <0.1%

HIT / HITT

  • Heparin + PF4 → IgG antibody → Fc receptor activates platelets → thrombocytopenia plus paradoxical thrombosis
  • Platelet fall > 50%, typically days 5–10
  • 4Ts: Thrombocytopenia · Timing · Thrombosis · oTher causes absent
  • Confirm anti-PF4 ELISA → verify serotonin release assay
  • Stop ALL heparin (incl. flushes, coated catheters) → start argatroban, bivalirudin or fondaparinux
  • NEVER warfarin monotherapy (protein C t½ 8 h → gangrene, skin necrosis)
  • Anticoagulate 4 weeks without thrombosis, 3 months with

Direct thrombin inhibitors

  • Bivalirudin = modified hirudin (leech), renal, PCI
  • Argatroban = L-arginine derivative, HEPATIC, HIT, affects INR
  • Dabigatran = oral prodrug, renal, avoid CrCl ≤ 30, contraindicated with mechanical valve
  • Reversal: idarucizumab for dabigatran only; none for IV agents

Warfarin — why 5 days (LO 3)

  • Blocks VKORC1 → no reduced vitamin K → no γ-carboxylation of II, VII, IX, X (“1972”) + protein C, S
  • VII t½ 4–6 h → INR rises in 24 h but this is not protection
  • Protein C t½ 8 h → transient hypercoagulable state
  • II t½ 60 h → real effect takes ~5 days → BRIDGE with heparin/enoxaparin
  • IX 24 h · protein S 30 h · X 48–72 h
LO 4 · WARFARIN DETAIL + LO 5

Warfarin pharmacogenomics

  • CYP2C9 = pharmacokinetic; metabolises S-warfarin (the active enantiomer). *2/*3 carriers clear slowly → lower dose (22% for *1/*2 up to 76% for *3/*3)
  • VKORC1 = pharmacodynamic (the target). Non-A/A ↓26%, A/A ↓50%
  • *1/*1 wild type: 70% Caucasians, 90% African Americans; *2/*3 rare in Asians

Warfarin interactions (LO 4)

  • ↑INR pharmacokinetic: amiodarone, cimetidine, disulfiram, macrolides, fluconazole*, metronidazole*, TMP-SMX (*stereoselectively inhibit S-warfarin)
  • ↑INR pharmacodynamic: high-dose aspirin, cefotetan
  • ↓INR pharmacokinetic: barbiturates, rifampin, carbamazepine
  • ↓INR pharmacodynamic: vitamin K
  • Cholestyramine appears in BOTH columns
  • ~99% albumin-bound; aspirin/sulfonamides/phenytoin displace → ↑free fraction
  • Food: keep vitamin K intake consistent, not zero
  • Drink: grapefruit juice; binge alcohol ↓metabolism (bleed), chronic alcohol ↑metabolism (clot)

Warfarin — the rest

  • INR target 2–3; 2.5–3.5 high-risk mechanical valve
  • Boxed: bleeding — monitor INR in ALL patients
  • Teratogenic — contraindicated in pregnancy EXCEPT mechanical valve
  • Adverse: bleeding, purple toe syndrome (cholesterol microemboli), skin/tissue necrosis
  • Reversal ladder: stop → vitamin K → 4F-PCC (Kcentra) → FFP
  • Vitamin K: NOT subcut, NOT IM, NEVER IV push — slow infusion only

Direct oral factor Xa inhibitors

  • Advantages: rapid onset · no dietary vitamin K effect · fewer interactions · predictable response · no routine monitoring
  • Boxed: premature discontinuation + spinal/epidural haematoma
  • Avoid with mechanical heart valve; avoid/adjust in severe renal impairment; avoid Child-Pugh B/C
  • Edoxaban: do NOT use in nonvalvular AF if CrCl > 95; needs 5–10 days parenteral first for VTE
  • Andexanet alfa WITHDRAWN 22 Dec 2025 → 4F-PCC off-label + antifibrinolytic

Thrombolytics (LO 5)

  • Bind fibrin → convert clot-bound plasminogen → plasmin → cleave fibrin into split products
  • Alteplase: stroke < 3 h · massive PE · STEMI without PCI · central line. t½ ~5 min
  • Reteplase: double bolus, STEMI · Tenecteplase: single weight-based bolus, longest t½, greatest fibrin specificity
  • PCI is preferred over thrombolysis for STEMI
  • Toxicity: intracerebral haemorrhage
  • Reversal: cryoprecipitate (fibrinogen) + tranexamic/aminocaproic acid
  • Absolute contraindications: prior ICH · ischaemic stroke < 3 months (except acute < 3 h) · intracranial neoplasm/AVM · aortic dissection · active bleeding · head/facial trauma < 3 months
LO 6–9 · ANTIPLATELETS + CROSS-CUTTING

Four steps, four targets (LO 6)

  • 1 Adhesion — GP Ia→collagen, GP Ib→vWF. No drug blocks this
  • 2 Activation/release — ADP, TXA2, 5-HT, thrombin. Aspirin (COX-1), P2Y12 blockers, vorapaxar (PAR-1)
  • 3 Amplification — ↓cAMP, GP IIb/IIIa conformational change. Cilostazol, dipyridamole raise cAMP
  • 4 Aggregation — GP IIb/IIIa binds fibrinogen. Tirofiban, eptifibatide — final common pathway

Antiplatelet boxed warnings

  • Clopidogrel: diminished effect with two CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles; omeprazole interaction
  • Prasugrel, ticagrelor: bleeding
  • Ticagrelor: aspirin > 100 mg/day reduces its effectiveness
  • Cilostazol: contraindicated in heart failure of any severity
  • Vorapaxar: bleeding — no prior stroke, TIA or ICH
  • Aspirin: Reye syndrome; avoid in asthma/rhinitis/nasal polyps; tinnitus; SJS
  • GP IIb/IIIa: thrombocytopenia; stop ≥ 4 h before CABG

LO 7 · Reversal — the four antidotes

  • Protamine → heparin (complete) / LMWH (partial) / fondaparinux (none)
  • Vitamin K → warfarin, then 4F-PCC, then FFP
  • Idarucizumab → dabigatran (Fab, 350× affinity, minutes); dialysis ~57%/4 h
  • Cryoprecipitate + antifibrinolytic → thrombolytics
  • Factor Xa inhibitors: no marketed antidote since Andexxa withdrawal
  • Bivalirudin/argatroban: none

LO 9 · Only FOUR need routine monitoring

  • UFH → aPTT · Argatroban → aPTT / ACT · Bivalirudin → aPTT / ACT · Warfarin → PT/INR
  • Everything else: no routine coagulation monitoring
  • LMWH anti-Xa only in renal impairment, obesity, pregnancy
  • All: platelet count, H/H, signs of bleeding

LO 8 · Pregnancy in one line

  • Heparins YES — LMWH is the anticoagulant of choice
  • Warfarin NO, except mechanical heart valve
  • All DOACs: not recommended
  • Aspirin: avoid from 20 weeks (except low dose for pregnancy conditions)
  • Thrombolytics: relative contraindication; alteplase does not appreciably cross the placenta; use if life-threatening
  • GP IIb/IIIa: do not withhold for MI — it is an emergency
