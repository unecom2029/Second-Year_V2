Four Classes, One Frame

Dr. Ly opens every one of the three decks with the same slide, and it is worth taking seriously: it is the skeleton the entire block hangs on. These four groups organise the lecture, although mechanisms can overlap: antibody-drug conjugates combine antibody targeting and cytotoxic payloads.

☠️

Traditional “cytotoxic” chemotherapy

Poisons that hit any dividing cell. Broad, cheap, effective, and indiscriminate — the toxicity profile is the toxicity of every fast-dividing normal tissue you own.

🔑 The one-sentence version of the whole block

Traditional chemotherapy is toxic to dividing cells; targeted therapy is toxic to whatever else expresses the target; immunotherapy is toxic because the immune system it releases does not only attack tumour; hormonal therapy is toxic because you are inducing menopause or andropause on purpose. If you can predict the adverse effect from the class before you memorise the drug, you have understood the lecture.

Transcript detail · Cancer terminology and treatment options

Part 1 · transcript clock 16:59:07–17:02:19

  • Oncology studies benign and malignant tumours. “Cancer” comes from the Latin for crab. A tumour is an abnormal tissue growth; the lecturer contrasts well-demarcated benign tumours with malignant growth that invades surrounding tissue and can metastasise to distant sites.

Goals of treatment: cure sits at one end, palliation at the other

Part 1, Slide 6 — Goals of Cancer Treatment (Lippincott Illustrated Reviews: Pharmacology, 8th ed., ch. 37)

  • Localised disease → the goal is cure.
  • Metastatic disease → the goal is usually palliation: extend survival, relieve symptoms, protect quality of life.
  • Locally advanced disease sits in the overlap — this is where the two goals compete and where the decision genuinely is a judgement call.
🧠 Read the goal off the stage, not off the drug

The same drug can be given with curative intent in one patient and palliative intent in another. Exam vignettes frequently hinge on this: a question that tells you the disease is metastatic and the intent is not curative is setting up a different answer (comfort, transfusion avoidance, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents) than the same drug in adjuvant use.

Q: A patient with a solitary, resectable colon lesion and a patient with diffuse hepatic metastases are both started on the same fluoropyrimidine. What differs, and why does it matter for how you counsel them?

Answer

The goal of therapy differs. The resectable patient is receiving adjuvant (or neoadjuvant) chemotherapy with curative intent — toxicity is tolerated in exchange for a chance at cure. The metastatic patient is receiving palliative chemotherapy: initial remissions are transient, symptoms recur between treatments, survival is extended but the patient eventually dies of the disease. That framing changes the acceptable toxicity threshold and the entire conversation about goals of care.

LO 1 — General Principles

Part 1 · Slides 7–11, 15–16

General Principles of Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is the use of pharmacological agents to treat cancer, and it is described in the deck as the most toxic group of medications in medicine. The goal is simple to state — kill cancer cells or stop them growing — and everything difficult about the field follows from the fact that we have no way of doing that which is fully selective.

Transcript detail · Cure, control and palliation

Part 1 · transcript clock 17:02:27–17:04:36

Cure means long-term disease-free survival and, biologically, eradication of every neoplastic cell. Undetectable disease is not proof of eradication. The lecturer mentions five-year survival as a commonly used benchmark; it is not a universal guarantee of cure. Control aims to prevent enlargement/spread, prolong survival and preserve quality of life. Palliation prioritises symptom relief and avoidance of life-threatening toxicity when disease control is unlikely. Treatment intent determines how benefit is weighed against toxicity.

Primary chemotherapy may cure selected advanced cancers, but often aims to reduce symptoms and prolong time to progression. Adjuvant treatment aims to reduce both local and systemic recurrence and improve survival.

Treatment modalities: when the drug is given defines the word

Primary chemotherapy

Chemotherapy alone

Used for advanced disease, or when no alternative exists. No surgery or radiation is doing part of the work.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy

Given before surgery

Goal: reduce tumour size prior to surgical resection — turn an unresectable or morbid operation into a smaller one.

Adjuvant chemotherapy

Given after surgery or radiation

Goal: decrease the risk of recurrence by treating occult micrometastases that imaging cannot see.

🔑 Neo- means “before”

The prefix is the whole mnemonic. Neoadjuvant comes before the local therapy; plain adjuvant comes after it. Both are add-ons to a local treatment — that is what “adjuvant” means. Primary chemotherapy has no local treatment to be adjuvant to.

Combination therapy: why almost nobody gets one drug

Combination chemotherapy is more effective for most cancers, and the reasons given are worth learning as a list because they generate exam questions directly:

  1. Larger spectrum of activity — a heterogeneous tumour contains subclones with different vulnerabilities; more mechanisms hit more of them.
  2. Use drugs with differing mechanisms of action and differing adverse drug reactions, where you are able to. Different mechanism means additive kill; different toxicity means you can give each at full dose.
  3. If two drugs share the same adverse drug reaction, you may have to dose-reduce. This is the practical cost of overlapping toxicity and is exactly why the “different organ toxicity” rule exists.
  4. Decrease the rate of resistance development — a cell must acquire several independent resistance mechanisms at once rather than one.
💊 The worked example: R-CHOP

The deck's example of a combination regimen. Each letter is a different mechanism and a different toxicity, which is the point:

  • C — Cyclophosphamide, an alkylating agent

Leucovorin is a rescue agent for methotrexate but an enhancer for 5-fluorouracil. Same molecule, opposite intent, and the deck's antimetabolite figure states both explicitly. Do not assume “leucovorin = antidote”.

The log-kill hypothesis: first-order kinetics is the reason chemotherapy is repeated

Cytotoxic agents follow first-order kinetics: a constant fraction of cells is killed by each dose, not a constant absolute number.

10⁸ cells→dose kills 50%→5 × 10⁷→dose kills 50%→2.5 × 10⁷=75% total reduction
  • Because tumour cell number is expressed as an exponential, kill is described in log units. A drug that takes the burden from 108 to 105 has produced a “3-log kill” — three orders of magnitude.
  • The consequence: no single dose can ever sterilise a tumour, because a fixed fraction of any remaining population always survives. Repeated cycles are mathematically necessary, not just conventional.

Part 1, Slide 11 — Log-kill and treatment scheduling (Katzung ch. 54; Lippincott ch. 37)

🧠 What the right-hand panel is telling you: the 1-gram threshold
  • 109 cells is the smallest tumour burden that is physically detectable.
  • Those 1 billion cells weigh about 1 gram — roughly the size of a small grape.
  • Clinical symptoms usually first appear at this stage. Everything below that line is subclinical disease you are treating blind.
  • That is the entire justification for adjuvant chemotherapy: “treatment of occult micrometastases continues after clinical signs of cancer have disappeared.”

Solid tumours (e.g. testicular carcinoma): tumour burden is first reduced by surgery and/or radiation, then treatment of occult micrometastases continues after clinical signs have disappeared.
Disseminated cancers (e.g. leukaemia): combination drug chemotherapy reduces the chance of drug resistance; each drug is chosen to have a different cellular site of action or cell-cycle specificity, and a different organ toxicity.

  • Many agents are mutagens, and therefore cause other cancers. This is common with alkylating agents and is the mechanism behind therapy-related secondary malignancy.

Q: A regimen pairs two agents that both cause severe myelosuppression. Why is this a problem, and what does the deck say you may have to do?

Answer

Combination therapy works best when drugs have differing mechanisms of action and differing adverse drug reactions. When two agents share the same adverse drug reaction, the toxicities are additive on the same organ, so you may require dose adjustment — giving each drug at less than its full effective dose and losing some of the benefit you combined them for. This is precisely why curative combination regimens are built with drugs chosen to have different organ toxicity.

Q: One dose of a cytotoxic agent reduces a tumour from 1010 cells to 107 cells. How is this described, and how many cells will a second identical dose leave behind?

Answer

This is a 3-log kill (three orders of magnitude). Because cytotoxic agents follow first-order kinetics, the second dose kills the same fraction, not the same number — it takes 107 down to 104. The tumour is still far above zero, which is why a fixed number of cycles is planned rather than a single treatment, and why residual subclinical disease is the target of adjuvant therapy.

LO 2 — Mechanisms of Resistance

Part 1 · Slides 12–14

Mechanisms of Drug Resistance

Three definitions first, because the exam will ask you to distinguish them, and then a wheel of seven mechanisms, each of which is tied to a specific drug class.

Primary (inherent) resistance

Before any exposure

Resistance present without prior drug exposure — the tumour never responded.

Acquired resistance

After exposure

Resistance that develops in response to drug exposure — the tumour responded, then stopped.

Cross-resistance

Spreads between agents

Resistance to one agent that confers resistance to other agents — including structurally unrelated ones.

Multi-drug resistance and the P-glycoprotein pump

Part 1, Slide 14 — Multi-drug resistance: the P-glycoprotein pump (Lippincott ch. 37, Fig. 37.6)

  • Multi-drug resistance (MDR) arises from increased expression of a normal gene, MDR1, which encodes a cell-surface glycoprotein called P-glycoprotein.
  • P-glycoprotein performs adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent pumping of drugs out of the cell. Its six membrane-spanning loops form the central channel that does this.
  • The P stands for “permeability” glycoprotein.
  • Because the pump recognises a broad range of hydrophobic substrates, cross-resistance occurs with structurally unrelated drugs — vincristine, vinblastine, doxorubicin, bleomycin, etoposide and others all leave through the same door.
  • Normal locations of P-glycoprotein: kidney, liver, pancreas, small intestine, colon, adrenal gland — and cancer cells.
🧠 Why P-glycoprotein explains more than oncology

The same transporter is the reason so many drug–drug interactions in the anticoagulant lecture are described as “P-glycoprotein efflux pump inhibitors and inducers” — verapamil, amiodarone, clarithromycin, ketoconazole, rifampicin. It is one transporter doing one job in many organs; oncology just happens to be where its induction is catastrophic.

Strategies to minimise resistance

  1. Dose optimisation — give enough drug to produce a deep log-kill rather than selecting survivors.
  2. Short-term, intensive, intermittent therapy with combinations of drugs.
  3. Combination therapy — a cell must acquire multiple independent resistance mechanisms simultaneously, which is far less probable than acquiring one.

Q: A tumour treated with doxorubicin initially responds, then progresses. On re-challenge it is also resistant to vincristine and etoposide, drugs it has never seen and which are structurally unrelated to doxorubicin. What is the mechanism?

Answer

Multi-drug resistance from increased expression of the MDR1 gene, producing P-glycoprotein. This is acquired resistance (it developed after exposure) that is also cross-resistance — the ATP-dependent efflux pump exports a broad range of structurally unrelated hydrophobic drugs, so resistance to one selects for resistance to all of its substrates.

Q: Which resistance mechanism would you predict in a cell line selected for methotrexate resistance, and which would you predict for a cell line selected for cytarabine resistance?

Answer

Methotrexate → changes in target enzymes (altered or amplified dihydrofolate reductase, so the drug no longer inhibits it effectively). Cytarabine → decreased activation of prodrugs — cytarabine is a pyrimidine antimetabolite that must be phosphorylated to its active form (by deoxycytidine kinase), and loss of that activating step confers resistance. Drug inactivation is the other named mechanism for the antimetabolites.

The Cell Cycle: CCS versus CCNS

Traditional cytotoxic chemotherapy divides into agents that only work on a cell that is in a particular phase of the cycle — cell cycle-specific (CCS) — and agents that work regardless of phase, including on resting cells — cell cycle-nonspecific (CCNS). Getting this split right lets you predict schedule dependence, tumour selectivity and toxicity.

🔑 The G0 problem is the whole reason cure is hard

Cell cycle-specific drugs can only kill a cell that is currently cycling. A tumour with a large fraction of cells parked in G0 is intrinsically less responsive — and slow-growing solid tumours have exactly that profile. Cell cycle-nonspecific agents (alkylators, platinums, anthracyclines) do not have this limitation, which is why they remain the backbone of many regimens.

Q: Both paclitaxel and vincristine act on microtubules in M phase, yet their effect on tubulin is opposite. State the difference precisely.

Answer

Paclitaxel promotes microtubule formation and prevents disassembly — unusually stable tubulin polymers stack and fail to depolymerise, so the cell remains frozen in metaphase. Vincristine blocks formation of the mitotic spindle by preventing the assembly of tubulin dimers into microtubules — tubulin fails to polymerise, the spindle dissolves, and the cell dies. Stabiliser versus destabiliser; both end in mitotic arrest. Ixabepilone behaves like the taxanes (promotes polymerisation); eribulin behaves like the vincas (inhibits spindle formation) but with less neuropathy.

Q: Why is a slowly growing solid tumour with a large G0 fraction relatively resistant to methotrexate but not to cyclophosphamide?

Answer

Methotrexate is cell cycle-specific (S phase) — it can only kill a cell that is actively synthesising DNA, and cells in G0 are less susceptible to cancer therapy. Cyclophosphamide is cell cycle-nonspecific — it alkylates the nucleotide backbone of DNA regardless of what phase the cell is in, so a resting cell is still damaged and dies when it next attempts to divide.

Antimetabolites in detail

Part 1, Slide 22 — Antimetabolites: mechanism of action (Lippincott ch. 37)

Methotrexate

Folate antagonist · S phase

Enters by active transport, then inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, blocking regeneration of FH4 from FH2. Without FH4 the cell cannot make adenine, guanine, thymidine, methionine or serine.

Leucovorin rescue: give N5-formyl-FH4 (folinic acid), which is converted to N5,N10-methylene-FH4 and bypasses the inhibited reductase.

5-Fluorouracil

Pyrimidine antagonist · S phase

Enters via a carrier, is converted by uridine phosphorylase and phosphoribosyl transferase through 5-FUR/5-FUMP to 5-FdUMP, which inhibits thymidylate synthase. No dTMP → no DNA synthesis → “thymidine-less death.”

Capecitabine

Oral 5-FU prodrug

Converted by carboxylesterase → 5′-dFCR → by cytidine deaminase → 5′-dFUR → by thymidine phosphorylase → 5-fluorouracil. Thymidine phosphorylase is enriched in tumour, giving some selectivity.

Gemcitabine

2′,2′-difluorodeoxycytidine

Phosphorylated by deoxycytidine kinase to the triphosphate, which inhibits DNA synthesis. Loss of deoxycytidine kinase is the classic resistance mechanism (failed prodrug activation).

Topoisomerase inhibitors and the free-radical generators

Part 1, Slide 23 — Topoisomerase inhibitors and other DNA-damaging agents (Lippincott ch. 37)

  1. Why topoisomerases exist. Unwinding the double helix creates positive supercoiling ahead of the fork. Topoisomerases nick, swivel and reseal the DNA to relieve it.
  2. Topoisomerase I inhibitors (topotecan, irinotecan). Topoisomerase I nicks one strand and reseals it. Topotecan stabilises the cleavable complex so the nick is never sealed → single-strand breaks.
  3. Topoisomerase II inhibitors (etoposide). Topoisomerase II normally forms a transient cleavable complex on double-stranded DNA and then a noncleavable one. Etoposide makes the cleavable complex persistent → irreversible double-strand breaks.
  4. Doxorubicin. Beyond intercalation and topoisomerase II inhibition, cytochrome P450 reductase reduces doxorubicin in the presence of oxygen to generate superoxide ion and hydrogen peroxide → strand breaks. This free-radical arm is what damages the myocardium and what dexrazoxane interrupts by chelating iron.
  5. Bleomycin. The DNA–bleomycin–Fe2+ complex oxidises to Fe3+, releasing superoxide and hydroxyl radicals → single- and double-strand breaks.

Q: A patient on high-dose cyclophosphamide develops haematuria. What is the mechanism, and what should have been co-administered?

Answer

Haemorrhagic cystitis, caused by the urotoxic metabolite acrolein concentrating in the bladder. It should have been prevented with mesna (mercaptoethanesulfonate), a rescue agent that binds acrolein in the urine. The same toxicity and the same prevention apply to ifosfamide.

Q: Two weeks after starting a platinum-containing regimen, a patient reports that drinking cold water makes their throat feel like it is closing. Which platinum is it, and is this an allergic reaction?

Answer

Oxaliplatin. This is pharyngolaryngeal dysesthesia and cold-induced neuropathy — a neurotoxic, not an allergic, phenomenon, and specific to oxaliplatin among the three platinums. Patients are counselled to avoid cold food, cold drinks and cold air. (A separate delayed hypersensitivity reaction is a shared toxicity of all three platinums, but it does not present this way.)

Q: A patient on procarbazine eats aged cheese and cured meats and develops a hypertensive crisis. Why?

Answer

Procarbazine is a monoamine oxidase inhibitor. Tyramine-rich foods are normally degraded by monoamine oxidase in the gut wall; with that enzyme inhibited, tyramine reaches the circulation and displaces noradrenaline from sympathetic nerve terminals, producing a hypertensive crisis. Procarbazine is a triazene alkylating agent, and the monoamine oxidase inhibition is its named drug-specific toxicity.

Q: A patient who has completed six cycles of a doxorubicin-containing regimen for lymphoma now needs treatment for a second, unrelated malignancy, and the proposed regimen also contains an anthracycline. What is the specific concern?

Answer

Cumulative lifetime anthracycline dose. Anthracycline cardiomyopathy is dose-cumulative across a patient's entire lifetime, not per regimen — prior exposure counts. The team must total the lifetime anthracycline exposure, obtain baseline cardiac function, and consider liposomal doxorubicin or dexrazoxane (which chelates intracellular iron and prevents free-radical injury to cardiac muscle), or select a non-anthracycline regimen.

Q: Which single traditional cytotoxic agent would you expect to spare the bone marrow almost entirely, and what is its dose-limiting toxicity instead?

Answer

Bleomycin. On the myelosuppressive-potential figure it sits in the mildest tier alongside vincristine and methotrexate-with-leucovorin. LO 7 — Targeted Therapy

Part 2 · Slides 6–17

Targeted Therapy — Classification & Mechanisms

Targeted therapy is organised into three groups, and the first group splits in two by which side of the membrane the drug works on. Getting that three-and-a-half-way split right is what makes the drug tables navigable.

  1. Transmembrane receptor inhibitors
    • Extracellular disruptors — typically monoclonal antibodies (mAbs)
    • Intracellular disruptors — typically tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) or RAF kinase inhibitors
  2. Signal transduction (intracellular) — all small-molecule targeted agents, typically identified as kinase inhibitors. No monoclonal antibodies in this group, because an antibody cannot get inside the cell.
  3. “Biological missiles” — antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and radioligands.
🔑 Size decides where a drug can act

An antibody is roughly 145,000 daltons; a tyrosine kinase inhibitor is a small molecule of a few hundred daltons. That single fact explains the whole classification: antibodies work only on the outside of the cell; small molecules can cross the membrane and hit intracellular kinases. It is also why signal-transduction inhibitors are exclusively small molecules.

Antibody structure: why “-ximab” and “-zumab” mean different things

Part 2, Slide 10 — Classification of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies by antibody type (Imai & Takaoka, Nat Rev Cancer 2006)

  • An intact immunoglobulin G (IgG) molecule has a pair of light chains (each with one variable region, VL, and one constant region, CL) and a pair of heavy chains (VH, CH1, CH2, CH3).

  • Chimeric mAbs (cetuximab, rituximab) have murine variable regions on human constant regions.
  • Humanised mAbs are predominantly human except for the CDRs, which remain murine.
  • Murine and human mAbs are entirely mouse- or human-derived respectively.

Most approved mAbs are IgG1, which has a long half-life and triggers potent immune-effector functions — complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), complement-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (CDCC) and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). Trastuzumab can also recruit immune attack, but the principal classification here is targeted payload delivery, combining antibody and cytotoxic mechanisms.

Why antibody-drug conjugates are toxic at all

Part 2, Slide 17 — Mechanisms of antibody-drug conjugate toxicity (Nguyen, Bordeau & Balthasar, Cancers 2023)

  • Intact conjugates enter normal cells by non-specific endocytosis, or by internalisation after binding the target antigen or Fc / C-type lectin receptors.
  • Payload released by deconjugation, or from targeted and non-targeted apoptotic cells, sits in the extracellular fluid and enters normal cells by passive diffusion (membrane-permeable payloads) or non-specific endocytosis (membrane-impermeable linker–payload adducts).
  • Either route ends in lysosomal catabolism, drug release and cell death in a healthy non-targeted cell.
🧠 The “biological missile” has collateral damage by design

The targeting is real but not absolute. An antibody-drug conjugate delivers a payload that would be far too toxic to give systemically — and some of it still reaches normal tissue, through routes that have nothing to do with the target antigen. That is why the toxicity tables above look so much like plain chemotherapy toxicity tables.

Q: Why is there no monoclonal antibody in the signal-transduction group of targeted therapies?

Answer

Signal-transduction targets — BCR-ABL, RAF, MEK, mTOR, BTK, PARP, PI3K, CDK4/6 — are intracellular. A monoclonal antibody is a ~145,000-dalton protein that cannot cross the cell membrane, so it can only engage the extracellular domain of a transmembrane receptor. Every signal-transduction inhibitor is therefore a small molecule, and the deck states this explicitly: “all small molecule targeted agents, no mAbs.”

Q: A patient on a monoclonal antibody targeting the epidermal growth factor receptor develops a florid acneiform rash over the face and upper trunk. Is this an allergy?

Answer

No — it is an on-target, mechanism-based toxicity. The epidermal growth factor receptor is highly expressed in normal skin, so blocking it produces the characteristic acne-like rash. The deck lists it as an “MOA-dependent toxicity” and gives it as the worked example of that concept for monoclonal antibodies. Both the small molecules (erlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, osimertinib) and the antibodies (cetuximab, panitumumab, necitumumab) cause it. Infusion reactions would be the separate, antibody-specific toxicity.

Q: Two antibody-drug conjugates both target HER2 in breast cancer, yet one causes prominent peripheral neuropathy and the other prominent myelosuppression and diarrhoea. Explain.

Answer

The payloads differ. Trastuzumab emtansine carries emtansine, an anti-microtubule agent — hence neuropathy (and thrombocytopenia, transaminitis). Trastuzumab deruxtecan carries deruxtecan, a topoisomerase inhibitor — hence neutropenia, anaemia, thrombocytopenia, nausea and diarrhoea. The antibody determines where the drug goes; the payload determines what the toxicity looks like.

Q: A patient with metastatic melanoma receives an antibody combination in a single infusion that blocks two different checkpoints. Name the drugs and the checkpoints.

Answer

Relatlimab + nivolumab (Opdualag®). Relatlimab blocks LAG-3 — lymphocyte activation gene-3 protein, an inhibitory receptor highly expressed by exhausted T cells. Nivolumab blocks PD-1 — programmed cell death protein 1. Two antibody drugs, two checkpoints, one infusion.

Q: Blinatumomab and tisagenlecleucel both target CD19 in B-cell malignancy. What is the fundamental difference in how they work?

Answer

Blinatumomab is a bispecific T-cell engager — an off-the-shelf antibody construct that physically bridges CD19 on the malignant B cell to CD3 on any passing T cell, recruiting the patient's existing, unmodified T cells. Tisagenlecleucel is a CAR T-cell product — the patient's own T cells are collected, genetically engineered ex vivo to express a chimeric antigen receptor (anti-CD19 scFv + CD8a hinge/transmembrane + 4-1BB costimulatory + CD3ζ activation domain), and re-infused, where they proliferate. A drug versus a living cell product.

Q: Why does an IgG4 monoclonal antibody make a poor choice when the intended mechanism is complement-dependent cytotoxicity?

Answer

Because IgG4 cannot activate the classical complement pathway. Complement-dependent cytotoxicity requires C1q to bind the antibody's CH2 constant region and trigger the cascade to the membrane-attack complex. IgG1 and IgG3 do this potently and also bind Fcγ receptors well — which is why most approved therapeutic monoclonal antibodies are IgG1. (IgG4 is used deliberately for checkpoint inhibitors, where you specifically do not want to lyse the T cell you are trying to activate.)

Toxicities of Targeted Therapy & Immunotherapy

These agents do not share traditional chemotherapy's toxicity profile, and that is the point of the section. Instead there are two families: class toxicity that follows the molecule's format (small molecule versus antibody), and toxicity that follows the immune response you have unleashed.

Targeted therapy: small molecules versus monoclonal antibodies

💊

Small-molecule drugs

  • Almost all: drug–drug interactions via CYP3A4 (cytochrome P450 3A4)
  • Drug–food interactions — many, so most should be taken on an empty stomach
  • Many: QT prolongation, diarrhoea, increased liver function tests
  • Some: diarrhoea
  • Multi-kinase inhibitors have more adverse drug reactions — hitting more kinases means hitting more normal tissue
🧪

Monoclonal antibodies

  • Many: infusion reactions
  • Mostly mechanism-of-action-dependent toxicities — the toxicity follows wherever the target is normally expressed
  • Worked example: acne-like rash, which is EGFR-specific
  • A few: progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) — a rare, devastating JC virus reactivation of the brain
🔑 The format predicts the toxicity family

Small molecule → pharmacokinetic problems (CYP3A4 interactions, food effects) and off-target kinase effects (QT prolongation, transaminitis, diarrhoea). You can answer most “which toxicity would you expect?” questions from format alone.

Answer

Cytokine release syndrome. The discriminators are timing beyond 6 hours (4–16 hours is typical), fever, and the triad of fever, hypotension and hypoxia. An infusion-related reaction occurs within the first 6 hours and is generally afebrile. Treatment: an anti-interleukin-6 receptor antibody (tocilizumab), plus corticosteroids, plus vasopressors in severe cases. Cytokine release syndrome is largely confined to the first administrations during step-up dosing, which is exactly when this patient presented.

Q: A patient on pembrolizumab for melanoma presents four months into therapy with fatigue, hypotension and hyponatraemia. What class of adverse event is this, and how does its behaviour differ from a T-cell engager reaction?

Answer

This is an immune-related adverse event — an endocrinopathy (hypophysitis with secondary adrenal insufficiency). Checkpoint inhibitor toxicity is largely unpredictable, quite unrelated to dose intensity, highly variable in profile, can affect any organ, and has a very variable time course — even after the end of therapy. A T-cell engager reaction, by contrast, is highly dose-intensity related, has a constant profile confined mainly to cytokine release syndrome and neurologic events, occurs during step-up dosing, and is quite predictable. Treatment here is corticosteroid replacement — you put a brake back on.

Q: Day 6 after a T-cell engager infusion a patient becomes confused, cannot name objects, and has a witnessed seizure. Fever has resolved. What is this, and which steroid do you choose?

Answer

ICANS — immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, which occurs 3–9 days after infusion and presents with speech difficulties, behaviour modification, memory trouble, confusion, headache and seizure. The pathophysiology is endothelial and blood–brain barrier disturbance. Choose dexamethasone — the deck states it is preferred — plus a non-sedating anticonvulsant (sedation would obscure the neurologic assessment), and an interleukin-1 receptor antagonist in severe cases.

⚠️ The tumour flare is the counter-intuitive step

An agonist at the gonadotropin-releasing hormone receptor initially increases luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone — and therefore testosterone — before continuous stimulation downregulates the receptor and shuts the axis down. Q: A 48-year-old premenopausal woman with estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer is started on anastrozole alone. What is wrong with this plan?

Answer

Aromatase inhibitors used alone for breast cancer are for postmenopausal women. They work by inhibiting aromatase and reducing synthesis of estrogens from androgenic precursors — the peripheral route that dominates after menopause. Before menopause, feedback can increase gonadotropins and ovarian estrogen production, so aromatase-inhibitor monotherapy does not reliably suppress estrogen adequately. Appropriate options are tamoxifen (a SERM, effective regardless of menopausal status) or ovarian suppression with a GnRH/LHRH agonist (leuprolide, goserelin, triptorelin) — which the deck notes is used to induce menopause in patients with breast cancer — with an aromatase inhibitor added afterwards.

Q: A woman on long-term tamoxifen reports postmenopausal vaginal bleeding. What is the concern, and why does this drug cause it while anastrozole does not?

Answer

Endometrial cancer. Tamoxifen is a selective estrogen receptor modulator — it exhibits both agonist and antagonist effects in different tissues. It is an antagonist in breast (the therapeutic effect) but an agonist in endometrium, which drives proliferation and raises endometrial cancer risk. It is also an agonist in bone, which is the origin of its protective bone properties. An aromatase inhibitor removes estrogen from every tissue, so it has no endometrial agonist effect — but for the same reason it causes osteoporosis, hot flashes and arthralgias instead. Tamoxifen additionally carries thromboembolic risk.

Q: A man with metastatic prostate cancer and vertebral metastases is started on leuprolide. Two weeks later he develops severe back pain and leg weakness. What happened, and what should have been given?

Answer

Tumour flare from the initial surge in luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone that a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist produces before it downregulates the pituitary. The resulting testosterone surge stimulates the tumour, and in a patient with vertebral metastases that can mean cord compression. Concurrent anti-androgen therapy — a receptor antagonist such as bicalutamide, flutamide or nilutamide — should have been started to cover the flare window, exactly as the mechanism slide specifies.

LO 1–3 · PRINCIPLES

Modality by timing

  • Primary: chemo alone — advanced disease / no alternative
  • Neoadjuvant: BEFORE surgery — shrink the tumour
  • Adjuvant: AFTER surgery/radiation — kill micrometastases, cut recurrence

Log-kill hypothesis

  • First-order kinetics — constant fraction killed, never a constant number
  • 108 → 105 = 3-log kill
  • 109 cells = 1 g = smallest detectable tumour; symptoms first appear here
  • Everything below that line = the target of adjuvant therapy

Rescue agents (3 pairs)

  • Leucovorin ↔ methotrexate (but enhances 5-FU)
  • Mesna ↔ cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide — binds acrolein
  • Dexrazoxane ↔ anthracyclines — chelates iron, blocks free radicals

Resistance — mechanism → drug

  • ↑ DNA repair → alkylators, cisplatin
  • Trapping agents → bleomycin, cisplatin, anthracyclines
  • Altered target enzyme → methotrexate (DHFR)
  • Failed prodrug activation / inactivation → antimetabolites
  • Efflux (MDR1 → P-glycoprotein, ATP-dependent) → cross-resistance to structurally unrelated drugs
  • Pharmacologic sanctuary → drug can't reach the site

Cell cycle

  • G0 cells are LESS susceptible — the reason cure is hard
  • G1 asparaginase · S antimetabolites · G2 topoisomerase, bleomycin · M anti-microtubules
  • CCNS: alkylators, platinums, anthracyclines, dactinomycin, mitomycin
LO 4–6 · TRADITIONAL

Microtubules — opposite directions

  • Taxanes + ixabepilone: PROMOTE polymerisation → frozen in metaphase
  • Vincas + eribulin: BLOCK assembly → spindle dissolves
  • Vincristine → neurotoxicity; vinblastine/vinorelbine → myelosuppression
  • Vincristine NEVER intrathecal — fatal

Platinums

  • Cisplatin: oto-, nephro-, highly emetogenic, neuropathy
  • Carboplatin: ↓ all of those, ↑ myelosuppression
  • Oxaliplatin: cold-induced neuropathy, pharyngolaryngeal dysesthesia
  • All three: delayed hypersensitivity, metallic taste

CHEMO MAN — the 6 pairings

  • Cisplatin → oto + nephro + vomiting
  • Bleomycin / busulfan → pulmonary fibrosis
  • Doxorubicin / daunorubicin / trastuzumab → cardiotoxicity
  • Cyclophosphamide / ifosfamide → haemorrhagic cystitis (acrolein)
  • Oxaliplatin / vincristine / taxanes → peripheral neuropathy
  • Irinotecan → potentially fatal diarrhoea
  • Plus cytarabine → chemical conjunctivitis + cerebellar ataxia

Alkylator specifics

  • Class: myelosuppression + mucositis, neurotoxicity, secondary malignancies
  • Nitrosoureas (carmustine, lomustine): N/V + delayed myelosuppression
  • Procarbazine = MAO inhibitor

Supportive therapy

  • Epoetin alfa → red cells · Filgrastim (G-CSF) → neutrophils
  • Sargramostim (GM-CSF) → granulocytes + macrophages
  • Plerixafor (CXCR4) + G-CSF → stem cell mobilisation
  • Eltrombopag / romiplostim / avatrombopag → platelets
  • Antiemetics: -setron = 5-HT3; -pitant = NK1; dronabinol/nabilone = cannabinoid
LO 7–10 · NEWER + HORMONAL

Targeted therapy — 3 groups

  • Transmembrane receptor: extracellular = mAbs; intracellular = TKIs/RAF inhibitors
  • Signal transduction: ALL small molecules, NO mAbs (targets are intracellular)
  • Biological missiles: ADCs + radioligands
  • Small molecule → CYP3A4 interactions, food effects, QT, diarrhoea, ↑LFTs
  • mAb → infusion reactions, MOA-dependent toxicity, rare PML
  • Trastuzumab → heart failure (HER2 on cardiomyocytes)

ADC payload = second word

  • -emtansine, -vedotin = anti-microtubule → neuropathy
  • -deruxtecan, -govitecan = topoisomerase → myelosuppression, GI

Checkpoints

  • CTLA-4 → ipilimumab, tremelimumab
  • PD-1 → pembrolizumab, nivolumab, cemiplimab, dostarlimab, toripalimab
  • PD-L1 → atezolizumab, avelumab, durvalumab
  • LAG-3 → relatlimab (+ nivolumab = Opdualag)
  • Toxicity = autoimmunity, any organ, unpredictable, even after therapy ends → steroids

Immunotherapy timing — the discriminator

  • <6 h, afebrile = IRR → antihistamines (epinephrine if anaphylaxis)
  • 4–16 h, FEBRILE + hypotension + hypoxia = CRS, IL-6 → tocilizumab
  • Days 1–3 = tumour flare · Days 1–14 = infection
  • Days 3–9, confusion/seizure = ICANS → dexamethasone + non-sedating anticonvulsant
  • Days 3–14 = cytopenia → G-CSF
  • Late, febrile, ↑ferritin ↑triglycerides ↓fibrinogen = HLH

Hormonal — receptor vs depletion

  • SERM (tamoxifen): antagonist in breast, AGONIST in bone + endometrium → ↑endometrial cancer, thromboembolism, protects bone
  • Fulvestrant: pure antiestrogen — blocks dimerisation, degrades receptor
  • Aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane): after menopause or with ovarian suppression → osteoporosis, hot flashes, arthralgias
  • Anti-androgen receptor antagonists → gynecomastia
  • Abiraterone (CYP17 inhibitor) → hepatotoxicity, hypokalaemia
  • GnRH/LHRH agonists → initial LH/FSH surge = tumour FLARE → cover with an anti-androgen; ↓bone mineral density
