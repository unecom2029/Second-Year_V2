Orientation — a surgeon’s view of the lymphatic system

Dr. Ciraulo opened by answering the obvious question: why is a surgeon lecturing on the lymphatic system? His answer was that surgeons spend a great deal of time with it — partly because of the complications that occur from other people trying to intervene in it. The spleen is the organ we operate on; the thoracic duct and the groin lymphatics are the structures we injure.

  5. Very few diseases are actually CURED by splenectomy.His explicit study advice: make a list of what splenectomy cures versus what it merely palliates. The cure list is short.

Topic 1 — Structure and physiology

The lymphatic system

Structure and physiology — a low-pressure, protein-scavenging drainage system

🔑 What the lymphatic system is
  • A sub-system of the circulatory system — the drainage system of the interstitial compartments.
  • A complex network of vessels, tissues and organs that circulates lymph back to the venous circulation.
  • Lymph is the pale fluid bathing the tissues: it maintains fluid balance and removes bacteria from tissues, entering the blood by way of lymphatic channels and ducts.
  • Composition: lymphocytes and macrophages — the primary cells of the immune system.
  • Lymph is filtered through the lymphatic organs (spleen and thymus) and lymph nodes before entering the venous system.

The system as a whole — ducts, nodes and lymphoid organs. Source: lecture slide 4

Generative organs (marrow, thymus) feed the peripheral organs (nodes, spleen, mucosa) — the architecture of the whole lecture. Source: lecture slide 3

The three physical properties that matter

Low pressure

versus blood vessels

Pressure within lymph vessel walls is LOWER than in blood vessels, and lymph flows more slowly than blood. Lymphatic vessels have valves and behave much like veins; when those valves are damaged or inflamed, flow fails.

High permeability

the key property

Lymphatic vessel walls are MORE permeable than blood capillary walls. This is why they can admit what capillaries cannot.

Protein-rich contents

the consequence

Proteins too large to re-enter the capillaries — along with waste products and large proteins synthesized in local tissue cells — enter the lymphatic vessels for return to the bloodstream. Lymphatic fluid is therefore very heavy in protein.

Tiny caliber

why we cannot repair them

The thoracic duct is only about 2–3 mm; peripheral lymphatic vessels are the width of pencil lead. In his words, “you can’t really sew pencil lead” — which is why there is no good surgical option for lymphatic injury.

Lymphatic capillaries pick up what the blood capillaries cannot re-absorb. Source: lecture slide 6

Valves stop backflow; the densely packed B and T cells are why an antigen-loaded node enlarges. Source: lecture slide 7

🧠 The primary function — and why it is not the immune one

The slide states it plainly: the primary function of the lymphatic system is to return proteins and fluids to the blood. Immune function is real but secondary in this framing. As he put it: yes, it fights bacteria and allergens — but when the system goes awry and we cannot get proteins out of the interstitial space back into the circulation, that is when complications set up.

Node enlargement follows the same logic: bacteria, allergenic particles and cancerous cells collected in the nodes stimulate lymphocyte proliferation, greatly enlarging the node. And interference with lymphatic flow causes accumulation of fluid in the tissues drained by the blocked vessel → lymphedema.

Q: Why does obstruction of a lymphatic vessel produce such marked, persistent tissue swelling, when obstruction of a small vein often does not?

Answer

Because lymph is protein-rich. Lymphatic capillaries are more permeable than blood capillaries and therefore admit large proteins that cannot re-enter the venous capillaries at all. When the lymphatic route is blocked, those proteins have no alternative drainage path and accumulate in the interstitium, raising oncotic pressure and drawing water with them. Venous obstruction, by contrast, raises hydrostatic pressure but leaves the protein-clearing route intact.

Q: A patient discharged after a severe leg injury returns with a swollen, red, hot leg and a wound draining clear fluid. What is the diagnosis?

Answer

Injury to a lymphatic vessel with a lymphatic leak. Dr. Ciraulo described exactly this patient: “He’s got a big swollen leg, it’s red, it’s hot, it’s nasty and his wound is draining clear fluid. He’s got an injury to a lymphatic vessel. It’s that simple.” The clear drainage is the discriminator — this is lymph, not pus, and the erythema and warmth reflect the acute phase of lymphedema rather than infection.

Topic 2 — Thoracic duct

The lymphatic system

The thoracic duct — and the one line you must never place

This section carries the single most emphatic practical instruction of the entire lecture. He repeated it three times and asked the class to raise their hands and take an oath.

Figure 1 — Lymph returns to the venous system at the subclavian/internal jugular confluence. The left-sided thoracic duct is the major one, and it sits directly in the path of a left internal jugular needle.

⚠️ “Do not ever, ever put a left internal jugular catheter in”

Anatomy: the thoracic duct enters at the posterior wall of the internal jugular vein at the confluence of the left subclavian and left internal jugular veins. It is 2–3 mm across.

Mechanism of injury, exactly as he described it: you place the needle, pass the wire, and believe you are in the vein — but you may be in the thoracic duct. You then pass the dilator, which is what does the damage. The line will not thread properly, so you pull everything and try again, succeed on the second pass, and the chest radiograph shows no pneumothorax, so all seems well. The next morning the film is white.

Why it is so hard to fix: repair requires entering the left chest and working at the apex of the lung — he described having to almost hang upside down to see it, sometimes needing a counter-incision — and roughly 90% of the time nothing can be done. The maneuver of last resort is having anesthesia instill high-fat feeds (he uses ice cream down the nasogastric tube) to increase chyle flow and make the leak visible enough to place a stitch. Usually the duct is simply ligated, relying on the right-sided duct to compensate.

Consequences: chylothorax (leak of lymph into the left chest) and chylous ascites. He deals with one to two chylothoraces a year, “and it’s always because somebody put a central line into the left internal jugular vein and nailed the thoracic duct.”

Use instead: the right internal jugular, a subclavian, or a femoral vein.

Q: A resident places a left internal jugular central line. The immediate post-procedure chest radiograph shows no pneumothorax and correct catheter position. The following morning the left hemithorax is opacified. What has happened, and why did the first film look normal?

Answer

Thoracic duct injury with chylothorax. The duct enters the posterior wall at the confluence of the left subclavian and left internal jugular veins, directly in the needle’s path. The first film was normal because chyle accumulates over hours — flow depends on lymphatic return, which is slow, and increases substantially only once the patient is fed. The correct lesson is preventive: do not place a left internal jugular line; use the right internal jugular, subclavian, or femoral vein.

Q: Why is a right lymphatic duct injury far less clinically significant than a thoracic duct injury?

Answer

Because the right lymphatic duct is smaller and drains only the right upper body, whereas the thoracic duct is the major duct draining essentially everything else — the entire lower body, abdomen, left thorax, left arm and left head and neck. The volume of chyle lost after a thoracic duct injury is therefore far greater. Conveniently, this asymmetry also means the right-sided duct can compensate to some degree when the thoracic duct must be ligated.

Topic 3 — Lymphedema

The lymphatic system

Lymphedema — square toes, and a diagnosis that depends on geography

🔑 Pathophysiology in one sentence

Lymphedema ensues if the production of lymph exceeds the maximal transport capacity of the lymphatic conduits. Accumulation of protein and cellular metabolites in the extracellular space generates water accumulation, collagen deposition, and elevated interstitial pressure. The collagen deposition is why chronic lymphedema becomes firm and fibrotic rather than remaining soft and pitting.

Obstruction → valve dilation and incompetence → reversal of flow → lymphedema. Source: lecture slide 8

Clinical findings — depend on duration and severity

🔥

Acute

Skin is pink or red with a mildly elevated temperature. Easily mistaken for cellulitis or deep vein thrombosis.

Pain: aching or heaviness of the limb. Severe pain is rare — a useful discriminator against infection and acute ischemia.

🪨

Chronic

Thickened skin with areas of hyperkeratosis, lichenification, and development of peau d’orange.

Progressive fibrosis from collagen deposition. In the breast cancer context he described it as disfiguring and debilitating, requiring sequential wrapping and special garments.

🔑 Stemmer sign — flagged explicitly as a board question

Edema extends to the distal aspects of the feet, resulting in “SQUARE TOES” — the Stemmer sign (sometimes Kaposi-Stemmer sign).

The formal definition: loss of cutaneous elasticity with an INABILITY TO TENT the skin of the interdigital web space. As he explained it at the bedside: with ordinary edema you can usually pinch it; with lymphedema the skin is firm and tough and cannot be pinched, despite obvious swelling.

He said you will see this: “You will see that sometime in your life, you 100% will. Lymphedema’s pretty common.”

Note on the name: the recording renders this as “steamer sign” throughout. The correct spelling is Stemmer.

NORMAL vs LYMPHEDEMA — this is the Stemmer sign: the skin cannot be tented. Source: lecture slide 9

🩺 The two vignettes he built the teaching around

Case 1 — the United States. A patient presents with a swollen, painful, heavy UNILATERAL leg. History reveals a hernia repair or kidney transplant 6–8 weeks ago — both approached through the groin, where the lymphatics run. (Kidney transplant is a retroperitoneal approach above the inguinal ligament.) There is pitting edema, erythema, warmth, pain.

First thought must be deep vein thrombosis — “because DVTs are deadly” — so you obtain an ultrasound. It is negative. Then, on examination, you notice square toes and a positive Stemmer sign → iatrogenic lymphedema from surgical injury to the groin lymphatics.

Note the deliberate rule-out: unilateral is doing real work here. He pointed out that heart failure gives bilateral lower extremity edema, so a unilateral heavy leg is not heart failure.

Case 2 — overseas. A woman from the Philippines with a swollen, painful arm and swollen, painful breast, unilateral. Everyone fears malignancy; you obtain a mammogram and it shows no malignancy. The answer is filariasis.

The prognostic difference matters: “the parasitic one is actually easier to treat, because you give medication. The surgical damage one, you’re not going to probably reverse.”

🩺 The surgical culture point he embedded in this case

He paused to make a professional point: there is a phrase in surgery that if you are operating somewhere in the body and something bad happens, it is your fault, and you need to identify the problem. Applied here: a patient with a swollen leg weeks after your groin operation deserves an honest consideration that “maybe I messed up the lymphatics.”

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis

clinical first

Clinical presentation, history and physical findings. Examination: inspect for cutaneous and subcutaneous fibrosis, peau d’orange, and Stemmer sign.

Lymphoscintigraphy

the confirmatory study

Reliable and reproducible. A radiolabeled macromolecular tracer is injected into one of the interdigital spaces of the affected limb and its transit imaged.

Nonsurgical management

first line, always

Elevation, therapeutic exercise, manual massage, and compression therapy (wraps, pumps, garments).

Surgical therapy

rarely, and late

Should be considered only after a trial of conservative therapy. He was frank that heroic attempts — such as sewing an opened vein onto a lymph node to drain lymph — do not pan out well.

Preventive measures

the durable win

Skin hygiene, clothing precautions, trauma avoidance, and infection control.

Complications

including a malignancy

Infection, malnutrition, immunodeficiency, and malignancy — specifically LYMPHANGIOSARCOMA.

Q: A 54-year-old woman has a heavy, aching, swollen right leg that began 6 weeks after an inguinal hernia repair. Duplex ultrasonography shows no deep vein thrombosis. The skin of the second interdigital web space cannot be tented. What is the diagnosis and the underlying cause?

Answer

Secondary lymphedema from iatrogenic injury to the groin lymphatics. The inability to tent the interdigital web space is a positive Stemmer sign (square toes). Unilateral involvement excludes heart failure, which gives bilateral edema; the negative ultrasound excludes deep vein thrombosis. In developed countries the leading cause of secondary lymphedema is iatrogenic — lymphatic trauma or dissection. Treatment is conservative: elevation, exercise, manual massage, compression, and it is unlikely to reverse.

Q: A 40-year-old woman who emigrated from the Philippines has a unilateral swollen, painful arm and breast. Mammography shows no malignancy. What is the most likely cause and the causative organism?

Answer

Filariasis — the most frequent cause of secondary lymphedema worldwide, producing elephantiasis. The organism named on the slide is Wuchereria bancrofti. It is waterborne and typically enters through skin invasion or injury, destroying the lymphatic system. Importantly, unlike surgical lymphedema, the parasitic form is treatable with medication and may be reversible.

Q: Distinguish the acute from the chronic skin findings of lymphedema, and name the malignancy that can arise in a chronically lymphedematous limb.

Answer

Acute: pink or red skin with mildly elevated temperature. Chronic: thickened skin with hyperkeratosis, lichenification, and peau d’orange, reflecting progressive collagen deposition and fibrosis. In both, pain is typically only aching or heaviness — severe pain is rare. The malignant complication is lymphangiosarcoma; other complications are infection, malnutrition and immunodeficiency.

Topic 4 — Thymus and bone marrow

Lymphoid organs

Thymus and bone marrow — where the cells are made and trained

🧠 The overall architecture he sketched

It all starts with the bone marrow. Precursor cells emerge into the bloodstream. B cells mature primarily in the lymph nodes; T cells get their activation and signalling through thymic tissue. The final destinations are the lymph nodes, the spleen, and the mucosal tissues — and it is those end organs that surgeons have to address.

The architecture he sketched: marrow → thymus (T cells) → spleen, nodes and mucosa, with continuous recirculation. Source: lecture slide 15

Thymus

Location

anatomy

A soft, roughly triangular organ in the mediastinum, anterior and superior to the heart and posterior to the sternum. Two distinct but identical lobes, each with a superficial cortex and a deep medulla.

Cells

composition

Epithelial tissues and lymphatic tissues containing dendritic cells and macrophages.

Hormones

secreted products

Secretes hormones and cytokines regulating T-cell maturation: thymulin, thymopoietin, and thymosins.

Involution

the age curve

The thymus enlarges throughout childhood but slowly shrinks from the onset of puberty, being replaced by adipose tissue — because the immune system produces most of its T cells during childhood and requires very few new ones after puberty.

Position: anterior and superior to the heart, behind the sternum. Source: lecture slide 16

Involution made visible: the pink infant thymus versus the fatty adult thymus. Source: lecture slide 18

🔑 T-cell training, and the term used on the slide

The thymus receives immature T cells produced in the red bone marrow and trains them into functional, mature T cells that attack only foreign cells.

  1. T cells first reside in the CORTEXwhere they contact antigen-presenting epithelial cells.
  2. Immature T cells that respond to the antigens are selected to survivethey mature and migrate to the MEDULLA.
  3. Non-responders die via APOPTOSISand are cleaned up by macrophages.

The slide names this process POSITIVE SELECTION. Learn it as the deck states it, since that is what the course will test.

Figure — T-cell training in full: bone marrow precursor → positive selection in the thymic CORTEX → negative selection in the MEDULLA → CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic lineages. The lecture tested only the cortex–medulla step; the helper subsets are board-review context. Source: AMBOSS

Bone marrow

  • Plays a central role in the immune system as the primary hematopoietic organ, and has an integral role in immune responses themselves.
  • Serves as a reservoir for long-lived, antibody-secreting plasma cells (B cells) — the basis of long-term immunity.
  • For T cells, the marrow holds both naive and memory populations; central memory CD8+ T cells are preferentially recruited to, and accumulate in, the bone marrow.

The marrow as primary hematopoietic organ — and the long-term reservoir for plasma cells and memory T cells. Source: lecture slide 28

🧠 The age shift — the thread that ties this lecture together

He returned to this idea repeatedly. In young children, gut and mucosal lymphoid tissue (Peyer’s patches, tonsils, appendix) and the thymus carry enormous immunological weight. As we get older, that responsibility shifts toward the bone marrow and other parts of the lymphatic system.

This is why the same operation carries different risk at different ages: an ileocecal resection or a tonsillectomy in a small child removes a meaningful fraction of their immune capacity, while in an adult it does not. It is also why splenectomy is deferred past early childhood whenever possible, and why tonsillectomy is now rare and generally deferred to about age 12 or older.

Q: Where do immature T cells first encounter antigen-presenting cells in the thymus, and what happens to those that fail to respond?

Answer

They first reside in the CORTEX, where they contact antigen-presenting epithelial cells. Those that respond are selected to survive, mature, and migrate to the MEDULLA. Non-responders die by apoptosis and are cleared by macrophages. The slide calls this positive selection.

Q: Why does the thymus involute after puberty without producing immunodeficiency?

Answer

Because the immune system produces most of its T cells during childhood and requires very few new T cells after puberty. The thymus enlarges through childhood, then shrinks from the onset of puberty and is replaced by adipose tissue. The existing mature and memory T-cell pool — maintained in part by the bone marrow, which preferentially accumulates central memory CD8+ T cells — carries immune function forward.

Topic 5 — MALT, GALT and BALT

Lymphoid organs

Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue — and “mucosal antiseptic paint”

He was explicit about why this appears in a surgery lecture: “MALT and GALT are important because we do tonsils, and we do appendectomies, and we do cecal resections.” Surgeons remove this tissue, so surgeons need to know what it does.

🔑 The three-letter families
  • MALT — mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue, the umbrella term. It includes:
  • GALT — gut-associated lymphoid tissue (Peyer’s patches, appendix)
  • BALT — bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (tonsils)

Major function: generation and dissemination of antigen-sensitized B cells, which require presentation to antibody-producing plasma cells in various secretory tissues.

Peyer’s patches (GALT)

What they are

structure

Small masses of lymphatic tissue found throughout the ILEUM of the small intestine. Histologically roughly egg-shaped lymphatic nodules, similar to lymph nodes except NOT surrounded by a connective tissue capsule — i.e. non-encapsulated lymphatic tissue, a category that also includes the tonsils and the lymphatic tissue of the appendix.

M cells

the sampling gate

Specialized microfold (M) cells line the side of the patch facing the intestinal lumen; the outer side contains many lymphoid cells and lymphatic vessels.

The antibody

the examinable fact

Predominantly IgA antibodies — called “MUCOSAL ANTISEPTIC PAINT.”

Function

what they accomplish

Monitor intestinal bacterial populations and prevent the growth of pathogenic bacteria in the intestines, analyzing and responding to microbes in the ileum.

Peyer’s patches in cross-section — lymphoid masses in the ileal wall. Source: lecture slide 22

The sampling gate: M cells face the lumen, follicles sit beneath. Source: lecture slide 23

🧠 How a Peyer’s patch actually works
  1. Antigens from gut microbes are absorbed by ENDOCYTOSISvia the microfold (M) cells lining the surface of each patch.
  2. Antigens are passed to the lymphoid tissuewhere they are absorbed by macrophages and presented to T and B lymphocytes.
  3. For dangerous pathogenic antigens, lymphocytes trigger the immune responseby producing pathogen-specific antibodies and by turning into pathogen-killing cytotoxic T lymphocytes.
🔑 “Mucosal antiseptic paint” — the mnemonic he built the whole segment around

He introduced this as a genuine board question he had seen (he attributed it to TrueLearn and to a recommended surgical board review book), and he drilled it hard: “Mucosal paint, you’re never gonna forget that. You’re gonna dream about mucosal paint tonight.”

The chain to memorize: Peyer’s patches → ileum / ileocecal region → IgA → “mucosal antiseptic paint” → coats the bowel → prevents bacterial invasion of the mucosa.

The surgical consequence: a child who undergoes ileocecal resection — classically for necrotizing enterocolitis, which he described as a necrotic, liquefying infection leaving bowel “black, gray nastiness” — loses the major site of IgA production and is therefore at risk for gastrointestinal sources of sepsis from bacterial translocation across an unprotected mucosa.

“Mucosal antiseptic paint” being made: Peyer’s patch → plasma cells → secretory IgA coating the lumen. Source: lecture slide 24

⚠️ A student asked about selective IgA deficiency — and the honest answer

A student asked whether this is the same as selective IgA deficiency. Dr. Ciraulo answered “I don’t know” — explicitly modelling that admitting uncertainty is correct — and the class looked it up in real time.

They are different. Selective IgA deficiency is the most common primary immunodeficiency, an inherited failure to produce IgA. As the class found: it causes recurrent respiratory and gastrointestinal infections, but is often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally, and is associated with autoimmune disease and allergies. (Board-relevant addition not raised in class: these patients are at risk of anaphylactic transfusion reactions to IgA-containing blood products.)

Surgical IgA loss after ileocecal resection is by contrast an acquired, local deficit of mucosal IgA production. The final common pathway — a less-protected mucosal barrier — is genuinely similar, which is why the question was a good one.

Tonsils (BALT)

🧠 Why tonsils are different from lymph nodes
  • The tonsils sit at the gateway of the respiratory and alimentary tracts and are continually and DIRECTLY exposed to antigen — unlike lymph nodes, which receive antigen via the bloodstream. That is the key structural contrast.
  • At the base of the tonsillar crypts are specialized micropore (M) cells with a tubulovesicular system for antigen transport.
  • Antigen is taken up by antigen-processing cells (macrophage-type) and presented to T helper cells and B cells, resulting in immunoglobulin-secreting plasma cells.
  • Predominantly IgG, and to a lesser degree IgE — note the deliberate contrast with the IgA of Peyer’s patches.

The tonsillar crypt — directly open to the pharyngeal lumen, which is exactly the contrast with a lymph node. Source: lecture slide 26

🩺 Why we do fewer tonsillectomies and think twice about a child’s appendix
  • Tonsillectomy is rare these days, and when performed is generally deferred until about age 12 or older — precisely because this tissue matters most in the young.
  • The appendix is a wealth of immunological importance in very young children, contributing to IgA and B-cell function.
  • Right lower quadrant pain in a young child is not always appendicitis. His advice: use a laparoscopic approach; if the appendix looks normal, whether to remove it is a judgment call.

Q: A 6-week-old infant undergoes ileocecal resection for necrotizing enterocolitis. Which immunoglobulin is most reduced, and what is the clinical consequence?

Answer

IgA — the “mucosal antiseptic paint” produced by Peyer’s patches, which are concentrated in the ileum and ileocecal region. Losing that tissue removes the major site of IgA production, leaving the intestinal mucosa vulnerable to bacterial invasion and placing the infant at risk for gastrointestinal sources of sepsis through translocation.

Q: How do tonsils differ structurally from lymph nodes in the way they encounter antigen, and which immunoglobulin predominates in each of tonsils and Peyer’s patches?

Answer

Tonsils are directly and continually exposed to antigen at the gateway of the respiratory and alimentary tracts, whereas lymph nodes receive antigen via the bloodstream. Tonsils are also non-encapsulated, unlike lymph nodes. Tonsils: predominantly IgG, less IgE. Peyer’s patches: predominantly IgA.

Q: What are M cells and where are they located?

Answer

Microfold (M) cells are specialized epithelial cells that sample antigen from the lumen by endocytosis and pass it to underlying lymphoid tissue. In Peyer’s patches they line the surface facing the intestinal lumen. Analogous micropore (M) cells with a tubulovesicular transport system sit at the base of the tonsillar crypts. They are the entry gate of mucosal immunity.

Q: A patient in a motor vehicle collision has fractures of the left 9th and 10th ribs and left upper quadrant tenderness. What injury must be excluded, and what imaging is used first?

Splenic injury. The spleen lies in the left upper quadrant protected by ribs 8–11, and there is a very high correlation between splenic injury and fractures of those ribs. The tail of the pancreas, which lies medially, immediately adjacent to the splenic hilum. The complication is a pancreatic leak. Q: Trace the venous drainage of the spleen and explain why portal vein thrombosis causes splenomegaly.

Answer

The splenic vein runs along the lower border of the pancreas and merges with the superior mesenteric vein to form the portal vein. Because the spleen’s only venous outflow passes through the portal system, portal vein thrombosis obstructs that outflow → blood backs up → the spleen becomes congested and enlarges. As he put it: “it’s gonna back up and grow really big.” Note that splenectomy does not help, because the thrombosis remains.

🔑 Opsonization — the concept the whole final section depends on

He defined it during the Q&A: opsonization is the process of coating an organism so that it can be recognized and destroyed. His framing was that it breaches the capsule so the organism can be attacked.

Encapsulated bacteria are the problem because their polysaccharide capsule hides them from ordinary phagocytosis. They must first be opsonized by antibody — principally IgM, which the spleen is the major site of producing — and complement, after which splenic macrophages can clear them.

Remove the spleen and you lose both halves of that system at once: the major source of IgM and the filtration bed that removes the opsonized organisms. That is the mechanism of overwhelming post-splenectomy sepsis in Topic 12.

A precision note: in the recording he described opsonization as breaking down the capsule so that antibiotics can get in. Strictly, opsonization tags the organism for phagocytosis — it does not degrade the capsule or admit antibiotics. The clinical conclusion he drew is entirely correct; the mechanism is worth stating precisely for an exam.

🩺 What a pathological spleen is actually like to operate on
  • Diseased spleens are enormous, very soft, and extremely friable — he compared them to “a rotten peach in the grocery store” and to pudding. They are hard to operate on and easy to tear.
  • The young-surgeon error is assuming it will be quick — “until you find out you lose like about 50 units of blood.”
  • His technique for an ELECTIVE pathological spleen: have interventional radiology COIL THE SPLENIC ARTERY 24 hours beforehand. The spleen then “starts to dry” like a sponge. It still bleeds somewhat because of the second blood supply, the short gastrics — but far less. This applies to pathological, not trauma, splenectomy.

Q: Which immunoglobulin is the spleen the major site of producing, and why does its loss specifically endanger a patient against encapsulated organisms?

Answer

IgM. Encapsulated bacteria resist ordinary phagocytosis because their polysaccharide capsule conceals them; they must first be opsonized by antibody and complement before macrophages can clear them. Splenectomy removes both the major source of IgM and the filtration bed where opsonized organisms are removed — so the patient loses the ability to clear Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis and Haemophilus influenzae.

Q: Which part of the spleen constitutes most of its volume, and what does it do?

Answer

The RED PULP — 75% of splenic volume, consisting of cords of reticular cells with sinuses. It is the primary blood filter and recycles iron. The white pulp (lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma cells) is immunologic, and the marginal zone between them holds macrophages and antibody-generating B cells.

The spleen

🔑 Definitions first
  • Hypersplenism: functional hyperactivity resulting in loss of one or more hematologic cell populations. It is a functional diagnosis, not a size one.

Q: A 48-year-old with a 3-year history of chronic alcoholic pancreatitis presents with hematemesis. Endoscopy shows isolated gastric varices with no esophageal varices. Liver function tests are normal. Hemoglobin 9.4 g/dL, WBC 3,100/mm3, platelets 84,000/mm3, spleen 18 cm. What is the diagnosis and the definitive treatment?

Answer

Splenic vein thrombosis secondary to pancreatitis. The chain: pancreatic inflammation → thrombosis of the low-pressure splenic vein running behind the pancreas → congestive splenomegaly → hypersplenism with pancytopenia, and separately gastric varices because gastric veins drain into the splenic vein. Isolated gastric varices with NORMAL liver function is the discriminator from cirrhotic portal hypertension, which causes esophageal varices too. Splenectomy is CURATIVE.

Q: A patient with cirrhosis and portal hypertension has a 20 cm spleen with pancytopenia. Should the spleen be removed?

Answer

Generally no. Portal hypertension is the most common cause of secondary hypersplenism, and splenectomy is generally not indicated because the spleen is a bystander — the obstruction is upstream and persists after removal. Contrast with splenic vein thrombosis, where splenectomy IS curative because removing the spleen eliminates the obstructed segment. The one situation prompting reconsideration is a spleen so large that capsular pain and rupture risk dominate.

Q: Why is a rising white cell count reassuring, but a falling white cell count alarming, in severe pancreatitis?

Answer

Patients with pancreatitis usually maintain a good white count while they are merely sick — it reflects an intact inflammatory response. A falling white count typically marks the onset of sepsis, when the marrow can no longer keep pace with consumption. In this population it may also reflect developing hypersplenism from splenic vein thrombosis, which lowers all three cell lines.

⚠️ Accessory spleens — why ITP recurs

Accessory spleens occur in 20% of people and ARE FUNCTIONAL (slide 45). Over 80% are located near the splenic hilum.

The consequence, stated on the slide: “If accessory spleens are present but not found and removed at surgery, diseases such as ITP will RECUR.”

The practical rule he gave: you DO chase accessory spleens when operating for an autoimmune or hematologic disease — because leaving one behind means the disease comes back. You do NOT chase them when operating for something else, such as trauma, where they serve no harm and may even preserve some splenic function. Nuclear imaging is the modality for locating accessory splenic tissue when disease recurs.

Note: in the Zoom session he said accessory spleens “usually are not functional.” The slide and standard teaching say they are functional — which is the only reason removing them matters. See Conflicts in the Source Material.

🩺 A second, subtler indication he raised

Beyond the classic lists, he described taking out a large spleen as a risk-reduction measure in a patient who will require lifelong anticoagulation. The logic: a large, friable spleen plus lifelong warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant, in an elderly patient who falls, is a recipe for catastrophic splenic hemorrhage. “Be aware that might be another indication — risk-benefit — when people have large spleens, we may actually take them out in order to protect them from the potential problems.”

Answer

Hereditary spherocytosis, an autosomal dominant dysfunction or deficiency of SPECTRIN, an RBC cytoskeletal protein. Fragile red cells lyse, the spleen clears them and enlarges. Watch for bilirubin gallstones and biliary colic. Splenectomy is CURATIVE — one of the few genuinely curative indications — but should be delayed until after age 4 where possible to avoid septic complications, since splenic immune function matters most in young children. At 9 she is past that threshold.

Q: A 34-year-old woman has fever, petechiae, confusion, renal failure and anemia. Platelets 18,000/mm3; smear shows schistocytes and nucleated red cells. What is the diagnosis and first-line treatment?

Answer

TTP. The pentad is fever, petechiae, hemolytic anemia, neurologic symptoms and renal failure, with thrombocytopenia and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia; the smear showing schistocytes, nucleated RBCs and basophilic stippling confirms it. First-line is PLASMA EXCHANGE (plasmapheresis) — NOT splenectomy, which is reserved for relapse or patients requiring multiple exchanges. The disease is rapidly progressive and usually fatal, with long-term survival under 10%.

Q: Six months after splenectomy for ITP, a patient’s platelet count falls again. What is the most likely explanation and how is it confirmed?

Answer

A retained ACCESSORY SPLEEN. They occur in 20% of people, are functional, and over 80% lie near the splenic hilum. If not found and removed at the original operation, ITP recurs — which is why accessory spleens are actively sought when operating for autoimmune or hematologic disease. Confirm with nuclear imaging, the modality specifically used to locate accessory splenic tissue.

Q: A patient with long-standing rheumatoid arthritis has splenomegaly, a neutrophil count of 600/mm3, and recurrent infections with chronic leg ulcers. Name the syndrome and its triad.

Answer

Felty syndrome — a complication of long-standing rheumatoid arthritis. The triad is rheumatoid arthritis + splenomegaly + neutropenia, with recurrent infections and chronic leg ulcers. Splenectomy is performed for symptomatic neutropenia, anemia requiring transfusion, or severe thrombocytopenia.

Q: A 17-year-old with infectious mononucleosis collapses after a fall while skateboarding. He is hypotensive with left upper quadrant pain and left shoulder pain. What is the diagnosis and why was he predisposed?

Answer

Splenic rupture. Mononucleosis produces a large, soft, friable spleen that ruptures with minimal trauma — the same character as the big soft spleens of portal hypertension. This is precisely why activity restriction in mononucleosis is a genuine medical instruction.

Q: A CT scan in a febrile intravenous drug user shows a splenic fluid collection containing gas. What does the gas signify and how is this managed?

Answer

Splenic abscess, and the gas indicates a severe, likely anaerobic infection — his rule was that air in a solid organ means a bad infection that must be dealt with. In an intravenous drug user the route is hematogenous seeding. Mortality is 40–100%; commonest organisms are Staphylococcus aureus or Streptococcus. Treat with broad-spectrum antibiotics plus splenectomy (traditional gold standard for refractory cases) or increasingly percutaneous drainage; culture the specimen and then give directed therapy.

Q: Why must the specimen be placed in a tear-resistant bag during laparoscopic splenectomy?

Answer

To prevent SPLENOSIS — splenic tissue seeding and growing within the abdominal cavity. Because that ectopic tissue is functional, splenosis can cause recurrence of the underlying disease the splenectomy was performed to treat, such as ITP. The technique is to place the spleen in a tear-resistant nylon bag, macerate it inside the bag, and remove it without spillage. The same principle — with far worse consequences — applies to echinococcal cysts.

🩺 Incision choice, and what he actually does in trauma

Open splenectomy may be performed through a midline laparotomy or a left subcostal incision. His strong preference: almost always a MIDLINE incision. A left flank or subcostal approach is reserved for elective pathological spleens; patients dislike it because the rectus muscle must be cut and it hurts as it heals.

In trauma, the sequence he described: make the midline incision, then pack all four quadrants with gauze, lift the omentum and mesentery and look for a ZONE ONE injury first. If there is a zone one (aortic) injury, nothing else matters — clamp the aorta, or open the chest and clamp the aorta or cava, then go to work. Only once zone one is excluded do you address the spleen.

Then, two accepted approaches to the spleen itself: some surgeons mobilize the colon and come down on the hilum; others “put my hand up there in the left upper quadrant and rip the spleen up”, tearing the ligaments and packing behind as they go — because those ‘avascular’ ligament planes bleed. Bringing the spleen up to the surface avoids operating down a deep hole.

🧠 Laparoscopic splenectomy

Operative steps are similar to laparotomy, but care must be taken to avoid rupture and consequent SPLENOSIS. Sequence: trocar placement → vascular control → ligation of vessels → mobilization of ligamentous attachments → place in collection bag → maceration in the bag → removal.

Because splenosis can lead to recurrence of the underlying disease, surgeons must avoid spillage of any part of the splenic specimen; tear-resistant nylon bags are available.

Topic 12 — OPSI / OPSS

Operating and aftermath

Overwhelming post-splenectomy sepsis — the most important thing in this lecture

He introduced this section with: “This is a very important question. If you remember anything from me in the entire four years, please remember this. I can’t tell you how important this is, because I’ve watched people die before my very eyes, and there’s nothing I can do about it.”

⚠️ The case he tells every year

A woman dropped her children at daycare with a mild runny nose. Feeling unwell, she went to her primary care office and waited. When they went to get her she was slumped in a chair, hypotensive, febrile, mottled. The ambulance brought her in hypotensive and tachycardic — “just dying right before your very eyes.”

Large-volume resuscitation was begun. Her skin began turning black from the periphery upward. Once lines were placed she bled from everywhere — gums, nose, the central line site. CT showed previous surgery and no spleen. Her husband arrived and confirmed a prior splenectomy — and that she had never received any vaccines.

She was dead at 3:30 that afternoon — roughly six and a half hours after dropping her children at daycare.

The mechanism: “The reason she was turning black before our eyes is she was hemorrhaging into all of her tissues. She basically went into massive DIC, septic shock.” He has seen a second, nearly identical case that took eight hours. Both died.

🔑 The mechanism and the organisms

Etiology: the spleen is no longer able to opsonize encapsulated bacteria. Incidence is very rare — under 1%.

Causative organisms — the three named on the slide, all ENCAPSULATED:

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae
  • Neisseria meningitidis
  • Haemophilus influenzae

Note: in the live session he initially said “four organisms”, and the class worked through it before he corrected himself: “Neisseria, Haemophilus, Strep pneumoniae. Yeah, maybe there’s three.” The slide says three; learn three for this exam. (For boards more broadly, the encapsulated list is longer — see Conflicts in the Source Material.)

Timing: most septic episodes occur within 2 years after splenectomy.

Risk is greatest if splenectomy occurs during the first 2–4 years of life — the reason spherocytosis splenectomy is delayed past age 4.

⚠️ Clinical presentation — why it is missed

Nonspecific, mild, influenza-like symptoms, which can rapidly progress to high fever, shock, and death.

That first clause is the killer. The woman in his case had a runny nose. There is no reliable early warning: the presentation is indistinguishable from a trivial viral illness until it is unsurvivable. This is why the entire management strategy is PREVENTIVE.

Prognosis: in his 32 years he has seen two cases and both died. During the session the class looked up reported figures and found roughly 50–70% mortality, which he accepted as more accurate than his own impression. Treatment is supportive care and high-dose antibiotics — there is no specific therapy.

trauma

Q: A 34-year-old who underwent splenectomy after a motor vehicle collision 14 months ago presents with 6 hours of fever, myalgias and a runny nose. She is now hypotensive and mottled with purpura. What is the diagnosis, mechanism, and immediate treatment?

Answer

Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection/sepsis (OPSI/OPSS). Mechanism: the spleen can no longer opsonize encapsulated bacteria — Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae — and the major site of IgM production is gone. Note the timing: most episodes occur within 2 years of splenectomy. The purpura and mottling signal progression to DIC and septic shock. Initiate penicillin therapy immediately, plus aggressive supportive care and broad-spectrum antibiotics — treatment is otherwise only supportive, and mortality is roughly 50–70%.

Q: When should vaccines be given for an elective versus an emergency splenectomy, and why does the timing differ?

Answer

Elective: 2 weeks BEFORE the operation, while the immune system is intact and can mount an antibody response. Emergency/unplanned: before discharge — and specifically once the patient is recovering rather than acutely ill or in hemorrhagic shock, because a downregulated immune system will not generate an adequate response. Document the vaccination and arrange primary care follow-up, with revaccination every 3–5 years.

Q: Why is splenectomy for hereditary spherocytosis delayed until after age 4 when possible?

Answer

Because the risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy sepsis is greatest when splenectomy occurs during the first 2–4 years of life. Splenic immune function — clearing encapsulated organisms and producing IgM — matters most in early childhood, before the child has developed an adequate independent antibody repertoire. Delaying past age 4 lets that repertoire mature while accepting a few more years of hemolysis.

Conflicts in the source material — resolved

Six places where the slides, the recorded lecture and the live Q&A disagree, or where a spoken statement does not match standard usage. These are collected here rather than buried, because at least one of them would cost you a board question. This is not criticism of the lecturer — much of it is the ordinary imprecision of speaking without notes, and in several cases he corrected himself in real time.

⚠️ 1. Passive versus active immunization — the definitions were inverted

What was said: “These are passive vaccines. Passive vaccines means your body has to be able to create the antibodies... An active vaccine, you’re actually giving them the antibodies.”

Standard usage is the exact opposite:

  • ACTIVE immunization = giving an antigen (a vaccine) so that the patient’s own immune system makes the antibodies. Slower in onset, but produces memory and long-lasting protection. The post-splenectomy vaccines are ACTIVE immunization.
  • PASSIVE immunization = giving preformed antibodies (immunoglobulin — for example rabies immunoglobulin, tetanus immunoglobulin, or Rho(D) immunoglobulin). Immediate protection, but temporary and without memory.

Critically, the clinical point he was making is entirely correct and does not depend on the labels: a vaccine requires a functioning immune system to respond to it, so you should not give it to a patient who is acutely ill, septic, or in hemorrhagic shock — wait until they are recovering. Learn the reasoning; use the standard definitions.

🧠 2. Three encapsulated organisms, or four?

He said four, the class worked through them, and he then revised to three. The slide lists three: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae. Answer three for this course.

For boards more broadly, the encapsulated organism list is longer and is worth knowing: add Klebsiella pneumoniae, group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhi, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Cryptococcus neoformans. A student in the session suggested Salmonella, which is a legitimate member of the wider list. The three vaccine-preventable ones are what matter after splenectomy.

🧠 3. Are accessory spleens functional?

Zoom session: “Accessory spleens usually are not functional, usually.” Slide 45: “Accessory spleens occur in 20% of people and ARE functional.”

Follow the slide. Their being functional is the entire reason they matter — it is why ITP recurs if one is left behind, and why they are hunted at operation for hematologic disease and localized with nuclear imaging afterwards. Note that his practical instruction was correct regardless: chase them in autoimmune/hematologic disease, don’t chase them in trauma.

🧠 4. Which immunoglobulin do Peyer’s patches make?

In the closing summary he said “if you have the Peyer’s patches, they’re going to produce, I think it was primarily IgG.” That contradicts both the slide and his own extended teaching earlier in the same lecture.

Correct: Peyer’s patches → predominantly IgA (“mucosal antiseptic paint”). Tonsils → predominantly IgG, less IgE. The IgA answer is the one he drilled hardest and the one the board question turns on.

🧠 5. Primary hypersplenism — men or women?

Recorded lecture: “rare, more commonly affecting men than women.” Slide 37: “rare, more commonly affects women.” Follow the slide: women.

🧠 6. What opsonization actually does

He described opsonization as “breaking down the capsule so that the antibiotics can get in and kill the organism.” More precisely: opsonization COATS the organism with antibody and complement, TAGGING it for recognition and ingestion by phagocytes. It does not degrade the capsule, and it has nothing to do with antibiotic penetration.

The conclusion he drew from it is correct — no spleen → cannot opsonize → cannot clear encapsulated organisms → overwhelming sepsis — but state the mechanism properly on an exam.

Elevated platelet count, common after splenectomy. Begin antiplatelet therapy above 1 million.
Opsonization
Coating a microbe with antibody or complement so that phagocytes can recognize and ingest it. Lost with the spleen.
Encapsulated organism
A bacterium whose polysaccharide capsule resists phagocytosis until opsonized. The three named after splenectomy: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae.
OPSI / OPSS
Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection/sepsis. Incidence under 1%; most episodes within 2 years of splenectomy; risk greatest if splenectomy occurs in the first 2–4 years of life. Nonspecific influenza-like onset progressing rapidly to shock and death; roughly 50–70% mortality.
Active immunization
Giving an antigen so the patient's own immune system makes antibodies — what the post-splenectomy vaccines are. Requires a functioning immune system.
Passive immunization
Giving preformed antibodies, providing immediate but temporary protection without memory.
DIC
Disseminated intravascular coagulation — widespread clotting consuming clotting factors, producing simultaneous thrombosis and hemorrhage. The terminal event in overwhelming post-splenectomy sepsis.

LYMPHATICS & LYMPHEDEMA

Physiology — one fact drives everything

  • Lymph is PROTEIN-RICH. Lymphatic walls are MORE permeable than blood capillaries; proteins too large to re-enter capillaries return only by lymphatics.
  • Lower pressure, slower flow than blood. Vessels have valves, like veins.
  • Primary function = return proteins and fluid to the blood (immune function is secondary in this framing).
  • Composition: lymphocytes and macrophages. Filtered through spleen, thymus and lymph nodes before entering the venous system.
  • Too small to repair: thoracic duct 2–3 mm; peripheral vessels are “pencil lead.” No good surgical option.

⚠ THE oath: never a LEFT IJ line

  • Thoracic duct = LEFT, the major duct, entering the POSTERIOR wall at the confluence of the left subclavian + left internal jugular veins.
  • Right lymphatic duct is smaller, superficial, drains right upper body — can partly compensate if the thoracic duct is ligated.
  • Injury → CHYLOTHORAX and chylous ascites. The dilator does the damage.
  • First chest film is normal; the next morning the chest is white.
  • ~90% of the time nothing can be repaired — usually ligated.
  • Use right IJ, subclavian, or femoral.

Lymphedema

  • Lymph production > transport capacity → protein and metabolites in the extracellular space → water accumulation, COLLAGEN deposition, raised interstitial pressure.
  • STEMMER SIGN = square toes = cannot TENT the interdigital web skin. Flagged as a board question.
  • Acute: pink/red, mildly warm. Chronic: thickened skin, hyperkeratosis, lichenification, peau d’orange.
  • Pain is aching/heaviness — SEVERE pain is rare.
  • UNILATERAL — heart failure gives bilateral edema.
  • Primary: females 2–10×. Congenital <1 yr · praecox 1–35 · tarda >35.
  • Secondary is MORE COMMON. Developed world → IATROGENIC (dissection, cancer, radiotherapy). Developing world → FILARIASIS, Wuchereria bancrofti (elephantiasis).
  • Parasitic form is treatable with medication; surgical damage usually is NOT reversible.
  • Diagnosis: clinical + lymphoscintigraphy (interdigital tracer). Treat conservatively first: elevation, exercise, massage, compression.
  • Complications: infection, malnutrition, immunodeficiency, LYMPHANGIOSARCOMA.

Thymus

  • Mediastinum, anterior/superior to heart, posterior to sternum. Cortex + medulla.
  • Cortex: immature T cells meet antigen-presenting epithelial cells → responders survive and move to the medulla; non-responders die by apoptosis. Slide calls this POSITIVE SELECTION.
  • Hormones: thymulin, thymopoietin, thymosins.
  • Enlarges through childhood, involutes from puberty, replaced by adipose tissue.
MALT & SPLENIC ANATOMY

MALT / GALT / BALT — “mucosal antiseptic paint”

  • MALT = umbrella. GALT = gut (Peyer’s patches, appendix). BALT = bronchial (tonsils).
  • PEYER’S PATCHES → ILEUM → IgA → “MUCOSAL ANTISEPTIC PAINT”. The signature board fact.
  • Non-encapsulated lymphoid nodules (like tonsils and appendix) — unlike lymph nodes.
  • M (microfold) cells line the luminal face and sample antigen by endocytosis → macrophages → T and B cells.
  • TONSILS → predominantly IgG, less IgE, and are DIRECTLY exposed to antigen (lymph nodes get antigen via blood).
  • Surgical consequence: ileocecal resection (e.g. for necrotizing enterocolitis) removes the major IgA site → GI source of sepsis.
  • Selective IgA deficiency is a DIFFERENT entity — the commonest primary immunodeficiency, often asymptomatic, linked to autoimmunity/allergy.
  • Tonsillectomy now rare, usually deferred to ~age 12+; the appendix matters immunologically in the very young.

Spleen — position and ligaments

  • Largest secondary lymphoid organ. LUQ, below diaphragm, protected by ribs 8–11 → left rib fractures 8–11 + abdominal pain = worry about the spleen.
  • Borders: left kidney posteriorly · diaphragm superiorly · gastric fundus and splenic flexure anteriorly · TAIL OF PANCREAS medially.
  • Ligaments: gastroSPLENIC · splenoRENAL · splenoPHRENIC · splenoCOLIC. Textbooks call splenocolic avascular — “it bleeds every damn time.”

Spleen — blood supply

  • Splenic ARTERY: branch of the CELIAC axis, along the SUPERIOR border of pancreas, terminates at hilum. Gives short gastrics → gastric fundus.
  • Splenic VEIN: LOWER border / BEHIND pancreas → joins SMV → PORTAL VEIN.
  • Artery above, vein below — “it takes a lot to compress the artery, not much to compress the vein.”
  • Two blood supplies (splenic artery + short gastrics) — why coiling the artery preoperatively still leaves some bleeding.

Spleen — histology & function

  • Red pulp = 75% of volume = primary blood FILTER, recycles iron (“the oil filter”).
  • White pulp = immunologic (lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma cells). Marginal zone = macrophages + antibody-generating B cells.
  • Functions: FILTRATION (debris, damaged RBCs) · IMMUNITY (removes poorly opsonized / encapsulated organisms; major site of IgM production) · RESERVOIR (minor).
  • Opsonization = coating an organism with antibody/complement so phagocytes can ingest it.

Hypersplenism

  • Hypersplenism = FUNCTION (loss of cell lines). Splenomegaly = SIZE.
  • Primary: rare, no underlying hematologic disease; per slide, more common in women.
  • Secondary: more common. Portal hypertension is the #1 cause → splenectomy generally NOT indicated (bystander).
  • Symptoms by line: anemia → pallor/fatigue/dyspnea · leukopenia → infections · thrombocytopenia → bruising/epistaxis · splenomegaly → capsular pain, rupture.
  • Splenic pain = capsular stretch = rupture risk.

★ Pancreatitis → pancytopenia chain

  • Pancreatitis / pseudocyst → compresses the low-pressure SPLENIC VEIN behind the pancreas → SPLENIC VEIN THROMBOSIS → congestive splenomegaly → HYPERSPLENISM → PANCYTOPENIA.
  • Separately: gastric veins drain into the splenic vein → GASTRIC VARICES → UPPER GI BLEEDING.
  • Isolated gastric varices + NORMAL liver function = splenic vein thrombosis, not cirrhosis.
  • SPLENECTOMY IS CURATIVE. ~20–25% of pancreatitis patients get splenic problems.
  • In pancreatitis a FALLING white count signals sepsis.
DISEASE · SURGERY · OPSI

Splenectomy CURES (short list)

  • Splenic vein thrombosis
  • Hereditary spherocytosis (AD, SPECTRIN, spherocytes, jaundice, bilirubin gallstones) — delay past age 4
  • Hereditary elliptocytosis (same, milder)
  • Echinococcal cyst — do not spill

Splenectomy HELPS / PALLIATES

  • ITP refractory to medical therapy, or with intracranial hemorrhage → sustained remission 75–85%. Only 20% sustain a steroid response. Mnemonic: ITP → I for INCISION.
  • AIHA: steroids first (75% respond); splenectomy helps up to 80% of refractory.
  • TTP: PLASMA EXCHANGE first; splenectomy is a bailout. Survival <10%.
  • Felty = RA + splenomegaly + neutropenia.
  • Sickle cell: only massive splenomegaly / sequestration crisis. Thalassemia, G6PD, PK: not curative, may cut transfusions.
  • CLL, CML, hairy cell: for refractory hypersplenism.

Accessory spleens

  • 20% of people, FUNCTIONAL, >80% near the hilum.
  • Miss one and ITP RECURS. Chase them in autoimmune/hematologic surgery; not in trauma.
  • Nuclear imaging locates accessory splenic tissue.
  • SPLENOSIS = spilled splenic tissue growing in the abdomen → same recurrence problem → use a tear-resistant bag.

Other pathology

  • Cysts: operate only for pain or mass effect; prefer partial splenectomy or marsupialization to preserve function.
  • Sarcoidosis: noncaseating granulomas; splenectomy only to rule out cancer.
  • Splenic abscess: mortality 40–100%; S. aureus or Streptococcus. AIR IN A SOLID ORGAN = bad (anaerobic) infection.
  • Mono → big SOFT friable spleen → ruptures with minimal trauma (the skateboarding case).
  • Histoplasmosis → splenic CALCIFICATION.
  • Hodgkin: staging laparotomy rarely done — does not change therapy.
  • Blunt trauma = leading cause of splenectomy; OR accounts for 20% of all splenectomies; many injuries managed nonoperatively.

Operation

  • Absolute: hemorrhage · splenic abscess · malignancy. Most other indications are relative (failure of medical management).
  • Steps: 1) mobilize by dividing splenophrenic/splenocolic/splenorenal ligaments · 2) careful hilar dissection — BLIND CLAMPING INJURES THE PANCREATIC TAIL · 3) ligate short gastrics.
  • Midline incision preferred; in trauma pack four quadrants and exclude ZONE ONE (aorta) first.
  • Elective pathological spleen: IR coils the splenic artery 24 h before.

Complications

  • Pancreatic tail injury → pancreatic leak · short gastric injury → gastric wall necrosis · colonic injury.
  • Hemorrhage from splenic pedicle or short gastrics.
  • Subphrenic abscess — with a LEFT PLEURAL EFFUSION.
  • Thrombocytosis: if platelets >1 million, start ANTIPLATELET therapy.

★★ OPSI / OPSS — remember this above all

  • Spleen can no longer OPSONIZE encapsulated bacteria. Incidence <1%.
  • Three organisms: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae.
  • Most episodes within 2 YEARS of splenectomy. Risk greatest if splenectomy in the first 2–4 years of life (→ delay spherocytosis surgery past 4).
  • Presentation: nonspecific, mild, INFLUENZA-LIKE → rapid progression to high fever, shock, DIC, death. His case: dead ~6.5 hours after dropping her kids at daycare.
  • Mortality ~50–70%; treatment is supportive care + antibiotics only.
  • PREVENTION: vaccinate 2 WEEKS BEFORE elective splenectomy; BEFORE DISCHARGE after unplanned splenectomy (once recovering, not while in shock). Revaccinate every 3–5 years.
  • If symptoms begin → PENICILLIN immediately.
  • Any patient with no spleen: ask about vaccine history.
