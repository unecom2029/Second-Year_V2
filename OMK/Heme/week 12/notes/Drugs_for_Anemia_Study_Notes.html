Four Drug Families, One Question

Everything in this lecture answers one clinical question — what is this patient's red cells missing, and how do I give it back? The missing thing is either a building block (iron, vitamin B12, folic acid) or a signal (erythropoietin). Get the category right and the drug follows.

The building block for haem. Deficiency gives a microcytic, hypochromic anemia.

ESAs & HIF-PH inhibitor

🔑 One number splits the whole lecture: mean cell volume

MCV < 80 fL → iron deficiency (microcytic, hypochromic). MCV > 100 fL → vitamin B12 or folate deficiency (macrocytic). Then one more test separates those two: methylmalonic acid is HIGH in B12 deficiency and NORMAL in folate deficiency. Those two facts answer most questions in this lecture.

🧠 Why the route question keeps coming back

Three of the twelve learning objectives are essentially “oral versus parenteral.” That is not padding — for iron the choice turns on gastrointestinal tolerance and absorption, for vitamin B12 it turns on whether absorption is intact at all, and for folic acid oral is nearly always enough. Each has an algorithm slide, and each algorithm is a likely exam stem.

Clinical Presentation and the Labs That Distinguish Anemias

Slide 6 — Clinical presentation of anemia (modified from AccessPharmacy, McGraw Hill)

🩺

Signs

  • Tachycardia
  • Pale appearance — most prominent in the conjunctivae
  • Decreased mental acuity
  • Increased intensity of some cardiac valvular murmurs
  • Diminished vibratory sense or gait abnormality in vitamin B12 deficiency
💬

Symptoms

  • Decreased exercise tolerance · fatigue · dizziness · irritability · weakness
  • Palpitations · vertigo · shortness of breath · chest pain
  • Neurologic symptoms in vitamin B12 deficiency

Patients may be asymptomatic or have vague complaints — which is why the labs, not the history, make the diagnosis.

⚠️ Only one of the three deficiencies damages nerves

Vitamin B12 deficiency produces diminished vibratory sense, gait abnormality and neurologic consequences. Iron and folate deficiency do not. This single asymmetry drives the most important safety rule in the lecture — see LO 7.

🔑 Methylmalonic acid is the discriminator, homocysteine is not

Both vitamin B12 and folate deficiency raise homocysteine, so homocysteine cannot tell them apart. Only vitamin B12 deficiency raises methylmalonic acid — in folate deficiency it is normal. If a question gives you a macrocytic anemia and asks how to distinguish, the answer is methylmalonic acid.

🧠 Two ferritin traps worth knowing

Ferritin below 20 mcg/L is diagnostic of iron deficiency, but ferritin is an acute-phase reactant — inflammation, infection and malignancy can push it into the normal range in a genuinely iron-deficient patient. In that setting the transferrin saturation below 10% is the more reliable marker. And note the direction of the iron studies: iron down, total iron-binding capacity UP — the body makes more transferrin when it is starved of iron, so the carriers are more numerous but less full.

Q: A patient has an MCV of 112 fL, low serum cobalamin, raised homocysteine and a normal methylmalonic acid. Which deficiency is it?

Answer

The normal methylmalonic acid points to folate deficiency, not vitamin B12 — a genuine B12 deficiency would show raised serum (> 0.4 µmol/L) and urine (> 3.6 µmol/mol creatinine) methylmalonic acid. Raised homocysteine occurs in both and does not discriminate. A low cobalamin with a normal methylmalonic acid is most consistent with a borderline or falsely low B12 assay in a patient whose real problem is folate; the methylmalonic acid is the more specific functional test and should be trusted here.

Q: Which single physical finding in the anemia examination points specifically toward vitamin B12 rather than iron or folate deficiency?

Answer

Diminished vibratory sense or gait abnormality. Vitamin B12 deficiency produces neurologic consequences — subacute combined degeneration of the dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts — which neither iron nor folate deficiency does. The other listed signs (tachycardia, conjunctival pallor, decreased mental acuity, increased intensity of some valvular murmurs) are non-specific features of anemia of any cause.

☐ Iron dextran needs a test dose

Low molecular weight iron dextran is the one intravenous iron on this list that requires a mandatory test dose — 25 mg (0.5 mL) before the first full dose. Dextran-containing preparations carry the highest anaphylaxis risk. For ferric gluconate and iron sucrose a test dose is recommended only when the patient has a history of multiple drug allergies; for ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose and ferumoxytol it is not required.

💊 Ferumoxytol and the MRI

Ferumoxytol is an iron oxide nanoparticle — the same physics that makes it an iron replacement makes it a magnetic resonance contrast agent. Notify the radiologist about any MRI within 3 months of administration, including non-contrast scans, because it changes the appearance of imaging sequences and can be misread as pathology.

LO 2 — advantages and disadvantages of oral versus intravenous iron

💊

Oral iron

Advantages

  • Effective for most patients
  • Extremely low risk of serious adverse events
  • Initial costs very low

Disadvantages

  • Gastrointestinal side effects are common
  • Adherence may be low
  • May be inadequate for severe or ongoing blood loss
  • May require administration for several months
💉

Intravenous iron

Advantages

  • Effective for most patients
  • More rapid correction of anemia and resolution of symptoms
  • Ability to administer large doses (up to 1000 mg elemental iron) in a single infusion
  • Adherence is assured
  • No gastrointestinal side effects

Disadvantages

  • Requires monitored intravenous infusion
  • Rare cases of allergic or infusion reactions
  • Requires equipment and personnel to treat allergic or infusion reactions
  • Initial costs may be higher
🧠 The trade-off in one sentence

Oral iron is cheap and safe but slow and badly tolerated; intravenous iron is fast, complete and guaranteed to be taken, but needs a monitored setting and costs more up front. Notice that efficacy is not the deciding factor — both are listed as “effective for most patients.” The decision is about tolerance, adherence, speed and access, which is exactly how the treatment algorithm in LO 5 is built.

Q: A patient needs 1000 mg of elemental iron replaced and has failed two trials of oral therapy because of constipation and nausea. What are the two decisive advantages of switching to intravenous iron here?

Answer

(1) The ability to administer large doses — up to 1000 mg elemental iron — in a single infusion, and (2) no gastrointestinal side effects, which is the exact reason oral therapy failed. Adherence is also assured and correction is more rapid. The costs are a monitored infusion setting, rare allergic or infusion reactions requiring staff and equipment able to treat them, and higher initial cost. Note that “lack of response to, intolerance of, or inability to adhere to oral iron therapy” is the first bullet in the treatment algorithm's list of indications for intravenous iron.

Q: Which intravenous iron requires a test dose, and what is the dose?

Answer

Low molecular weight iron dextran — 25 mg (0.5 mL) prior to the first dose. It is the only agent on the list where a test dose is required rather than merely recommended. Ferric gluconate and iron sucrose have test doses recommended only in patients with a history of multiple drug allergies. Ferric carboxymaltose, ferric derisomaltose and ferumoxytol do not require one. (High molecular weight iron dextran is no longer available.)

LO 3 — Iron Interactions

Section 02 · Slide 12

Iron Salt–Drug Interactions

The learning objective asks for interactions in both directions: drugs that reduce iron absorption, and drugs whose own effect is reduced by iron. They are different lists and both get tested.

⬇️

Drugs that decrease iron absorption

  • Aluminium-, magnesium- and calcium-containing antacids
  • Tetracycline and doxycycline
  • Histamine H2 antagonists
  • Proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs)
  • Cholestyramine
⬅️

Drugs affected by iron

  • Levodopa ↓ — chelates with iron
  • Methyldopa ↓ — decreases efficacy of methyldopa
  • Levothyroxine ↓ — decreased efficacy of levothyroxine
  • Fluoroquinolones ↓ — forms a ferric ion–quinolone complex
  • Tetracycline and doxycycline ↓ — when administered within 2 hours of an iron salt
  • Mycophenolate ↓ — decreases absorption
🔑 Two mechanisms explain both lists

Acid suppression. Iron is absorbed best in the ferrous (Fe2+) state, which requires an acidic stomach. Anything that raises gastric pH — antacids, H2 antagonists, proton-pump inhibitors — reduces absorption.
Chelation. Iron is a metal cation that binds drugs directly in the gut lumen, forming complexes neither party can absorb. That is why tetracyclines appear in both columns — iron blocks the antibiotic and the antibiotic blocks the iron — and why levodopa, fluoroquinolones, levothyroxine, methyldopa and mycophenolate all lose potency.

💊 The practical instruction

Separate iron from the interacting drug by a time gap — the deck specifies 2 hours for tetracycline and doxycycline. In practice this is the counselling point for every drug in the right-hand column: take them apart. And when a patient on a proton-pump inhibitor fails oral iron, the failure may be pharmacological rather than adherence — which pushes toward intravenous iron.

Q: A hypothyroid patient on levothyroxine is started on ferrous sulfate and six weeks later her thyroid-stimulating hormone has risen. What happened?

Answer

Iron decreased the efficacy of levothyroxine by binding it in the gut, so less thyroxine was absorbed and the thyroid-stimulating hormone rose. Levothyroxine appears on the “drugs affected by iron” list for exactly this reason. The fix is to separate the doses in time rather than to stop either drug — the same principle that applies to levodopa, methyldopa, fluoroquinolones, tetracycline/doxycycline and mycophenolate.

Q: Why might oral iron fail in a patient taking omeprazole daily?

Answer

Proton-pump inhibitors decrease iron absorption by raising gastric pH — iron requires an acidic environment to stay in the absorbable ferrous form. The same applies to histamine H2 antagonists and to aluminium-, magnesium- and calcium-containing antacids. This is a genuine pharmacological cause of oral iron failure, and “lack of response to oral iron therapy” is an indication in the treatment algorithm for switching to intravenous iron.

LO 4 — Adverse Effects & Toxicity

Section 02 · Slides 13–14

Adverse Effects of Iron, and Reversal of Iron Toxicity

Slide 13 — Adverse effects of iron therapy

💊

Oral formulations

  • Gastrointestinal side effects are extremely common with oral iron administration
  • Metallic taste, nausea/vomiting, flatulence, constipation and epigastric distress
  • Patients may also be bothered by itching and by black/green or tarry stools that stain clothing or cause anxiety about bleeding
  • As a result, compliance with oral iron administration may be low
💉

Intravenous formulations

  • Allergic reactions, including potentially life-threatening anaphylaxis — but serious allergic reactions are exceedingly rare
  • Hypotension, dizziness, dyspnoea, headaches, lower back pain, arthralgia, syncope and arthritis
  • Some of these reactions can be minimised by decreasing the dose or rate of infusion
💊 Black stools are expected, not alarming — but do not dismiss them either

Oral iron reliably produces black, green or tarry stools. Counsel the patient in advance so it does not read as gastrointestinal bleeding. The caution runs the other way too: in a patient on iron who is also at risk of bleeding, dark stools cannot be assumed benign — the deck notes patients may develop anxiety about bleeding, and clinically melaena must still be excluded on its own merits.

Acute iron toxicity and chronic iron overload

Slide 14 — Reversal of acute iron toxicity and chronic iron overload

🚨

Acute iron toxicity

Seen almost exclusively in young children who accidentally ingest iron tablets.

  • Severe gastrointestinal distress → necrotising gastroenteritis with haematemesis (vomiting of blood), abdominal pain and bloody diarrhoea
  • Followed by dyspnoea, lethargy and shock
  • This may be followed by severe metabolic acidosis, coma and death
  • Peak serum iron level > 500 mcg/dL

Urgent treatment is needed

  • Intestinal decontamination (whole bowel irrigation)
  • Gastric lavage
  • Iron-chelating compounds — deferoxamine intravenously, the preferred route
⛓️

Iron overload reversal — the three chelators

  • Deferoxamine — intravenous, the preferred route for severe, life-threatening iron overload. Labelled indications: acute iron toxicity AND chronic iron overload
  • Deferasirox — oral. Labelled indication: chronic iron overload
  • Deferiprone — oral. Labelled indication: transfusional iron overload
🔑 Deferoxamine is the only one that covers acute toxicity

Acute iron poisoning → deferoxamine, intravenously. The two oral agents — deferasirox and deferiprone — are labelled for chronic (and, for deferiprone, specifically transfusional) iron overload. A child who has swallowed iron tablets needs the intravenous agent, not an oral one.

Q: A 2-year-old is brought in after swallowing a bottle of her mother's prenatal iron tablets. She is vomiting blood and has bloody diarrhoea; serum iron is 640 mcg/dL. What are the treatment steps?

Answer

This is acute iron toxicity — the classic scenario, seen almost exclusively in young children who accidentally ingest iron tablets — with necrotising gastroenteritis and a peak serum iron above 500 mcg/dL. She is at risk of progressing to dyspnoea, lethargy, shock, severe metabolic acidosis, coma and death. Urgent treatment: intestinal decontamination with whole bowel irrigation, gastric lavage, and intravenous deferoxamine — the preferred route and the only chelator labelled for acute iron toxicity. Activated charcoal is useless here; it does not bind iron.

Q: A thalassaemia patient on chronic transfusions needs long-term iron chelation as an outpatient. Which agents are appropriate?

Answer

The oral chelators: deferasirox (labelled for chronic iron overload) and deferiprone (labelled specifically for transfusional iron overload, which is precisely this situation). Deferoxamine is also labelled for chronic iron overload but is intravenous and is the preferred route for severe, life-threatening overload — impractical as routine outpatient maintenance.

LO 5 — IDA Treatment Algorithm

Section 02 · Slide 15

Treatment of Iron Deficiency Anemia

Slide 15 — Treatment of iron deficiency anemia (modified from UpToDate, “Anemia treatment algorithm”)

  1. First question: is the anemia severe or life-threatening?
    Defined as a haemoglobin level of < 7 to 8 g/dL, or anemia with symptoms of haemodynamic compromise or cardiac ischaemia.
    → YES: red blood cell transfusion is generally indicated.
  2. If NO — second question: are any of the following present?
    • Lack of response to, intolerance of, or inability to adhere to oral iron therapy
    • Surgery planned within the next 2 months
    • Inflammatory bowel disease
    • Gastrectomy or bariatric surgery
    • Dialysis-dependent kidney disease
    → YES: intravenous iron is generally preferred.
    → NO: oral iron is generally preferred.
🔑 The five indications for intravenous iron are one idea in five forms

Every one of them means either “the gut cannot absorb it” (inflammatory bowel disease, gastrectomy or bariatric surgery), “the gut route has already failed” (lack of response, intolerance, non-adherence), or “there is not enough time” (surgery within 2 months, dialysis-dependent kidney disease). If the vignette contains any of those, the answer is intravenous.

🧠 Transfusion is not iron therapy

The top of the algorithm is a haemodynamic decision, not a replacement decision. A haemoglobin below 7–8 g/dL, or symptoms of haemodynamic compromise or cardiac ischaemia, means the patient needs red cells now — transfusion buys time. The iron replacement question is then asked separately, because transfusion does not fix the underlying deficiency.

Q: A patient with Crohn's disease has iron deficiency anemia with a haemoglobin of 9.8 g/dL and no cardiac symptoms. Oral or intravenous iron?

Answer

Intravenous. Working the algorithm: the haemoglobin is above the 7–8 g/dL threshold and there is no haemodynamic compromise or cardiac ischaemia, so transfusion is not indicated. But inflammatory bowel disease is one of the five named conditions in which intravenous iron is generally preferred — inflamed bowel absorbs iron poorly, and oral iron may worsen gastrointestinal symptoms. The other four are lack of response/intolerance/non-adherence to oral iron, surgery planned within 2 months, gastrectomy or bariatric surgery, and dialysis-dependent kidney disease.

Q: A patient with iron deficiency anemia has a haemoglobin of 6.4 g/dL and is describing exertional chest pain. What is the first step?

Answer

Red blood cell transfusion. The anemia is severe or life-threatening by both criteria in the algorithm — haemoglobin below 7–8 g/dL and symptoms of cardiac ischaemia — so transfusion is generally indicated before anything else. Iron replacement still has to happen, but neither oral nor intravenous iron raises the haemoglobin quickly enough for a patient with ongoing myocardial ischaemia.

LO 6 & 7 — Vitamin B12

Section 03 · Slides 17–19

Vitamin B12 — Products, Adverse Effects & Treatment

💊

Products — cyanocobalamin

  • The cyanocobalamin content of over-the-counter products varies. The dosage in B-complex products or products containing only vitamin B12 is higher than in multivitamin products
  • Routes of administration: intramuscular (IM), subcutaneous (SUBQ), oral, intranasal
✅

Adverse effects — notably few

  • Potential adverse effects with vitamin B12 replacement therapy are rare
  • Uncommon side effects include hypokalaemia, due to a marked increase in potassium utilisation during production of new haematopoietic cells
  • Rare cases of hypersensitivity with vitamin B12 have been reported
⚠️ Hypokalaemia in the first week of treatment

When you correct a severe B12 deficiency, the marrow suddenly starts building cells at speed — and new haematopoietic cells pull potassium into themselves. The result is a marked increase in potassium utilisation and a fall in serum potassium. This is why the monitoring slide says to check serum potassium in the first week of vitamin B12 therapy. It is a treatment effect, not a disease effect, and it is a favourite exam detail.

Oral versus intramuscular vitamin B12

💊

Oral

Advantages

  • Outpatient visit not required
  • Preferred for dietary deficiency
  • Equal efficacy to intramuscular therapy

Disadvantages

  • Adherence may not be assured
  • Higher doses required for any condition that interferes with absorption
  • For initial repletion of severe or symptomatic deficiency there may be concern about rapid and sufficient repletion — however, oral repletion can be effective for maintenance therapy
💉

Intramuscular

Advantages

  • Adherence is assured — especially important for severe or symptomatic deficiency
  • Opportunity to interact with the medical team
  • Preferred for altered gastrointestinal anatomy or impaired absorption
  • Less-frequent dosing after the deficiency is corrected

Disadvantages

  • May require an outpatient visit — nurse time, wait time, travel
  • Higher cost, including co-pay for the visit and possibly a facility fee
  • Extremely rare reports of hypersensitivity
🔑 Oral B12 has equal efficacy — the route question is about absorption and adherence

Unlike iron, where intravenous therapy is genuinely faster and more complete, oral vitamin B12 is stated to have equal efficacy to intramuscular. The decision therefore hinges on two things only: can the patient absorb it (altered gastrointestinal anatomy, impaired absorption → intramuscular) and will the patient take it (adherence concern → intramuscular). Dietary deficiency with an intact gut → oral is preferred.

LO 7 — treatment algorithm for vitamin B12-deficiency anemia

Slide 19 — Treatment of vitamin B12-deficiency anemia (modified from UpToDate)

  1. Ask: are there any of the following?
    • Symptomatic or severe anemia (haemoglobin < 8 g/dL)
    • Neuropsychiatric or neurologic symptoms
    • Possible / likely malabsorption
    • Concern about adherence or follow-up
  2. YES → initiate parenteral (intramuscular) therapy.
  3. NO → initiate parenteral (intramuscular) therapy OR oral therapy, based on shared decision-making.
🧠 Note what the “no” branch does not say

It does not say “give oral.” It says either route, based on shared decision-making — because oral has equal efficacy, so the patient's preference genuinely matters. Contrast the iron algorithm, where the “no” branch states plainly that oral iron is generally preferred. Reading the algorithms carefully is the difference between a right and a nearly-right answer.

Q: A patient after total gastrectomy is found to have vitamin B12 deficiency with a haemoglobin of 10.2 g/dL and no neurologic symptoms. Which route?

Answer

Parenteral (intramuscular). The haemoglobin is above 8 g/dL and there are no neurologic symptoms, but gastrectomy means possible/likely malabsorption — one of the four triggers in the algorithm. It is also listed separately as a reason the intramuscular route is preferred: “preferred for altered gastrointestinal anatomy or impaired absorption.” Oral therapy could work only at higher doses required for any condition that interferes with absorption, but the algorithm's answer here is parenteral.

Q: A vegan patient with dietary vitamin B12 deficiency, a haemoglobin of 11 g/dL, an intact gastrointestinal tract and reliable follow-up asks whether she can avoid injections. What do you say?

Answer

Yes — oral therapy is appropriate. None of the four algorithm triggers is present (no severe or symptomatic anemia, no neurologic symptoms, no malabsorption, no adherence concern), so the choice is parenteral or oral by shared decision-making. Oral is specifically preferred for dietary deficiency, has equal efficacy to intramuscular therapy, and avoids the outpatient visit and cost. Check her serum potassium in the first week for treatment-related hypokalaemia, and remember therapy is lifelong if the cause is not reversible — here it is reversible with diet or ongoing supplementation.

LO 9 — prevention and treatment of folic acid-deficiency anemia

Slide 21 — Prevention and treatment of folic acid-deficiency anemia (modified from UpToDate)

🛡️

Prevention

  • Individuals at risk for folate deficiency — e.g. malnutrition, chronic alcohol use, chronic haemolytic anemia
  • Folic acid supplementation — typical dose 1 mg orally per day to prevent deficiency developing in these conditions
  • Recommended daily allowance for adults is 400 mcg daily
  • Pregnant patients should get 600 mcg daily to prevent some major birth defects of the baby's brain (anencephaly) and spine (spina bifida)
  • Patients who have previously given birth to offspring with neural tube defects, or who have a family history of neural tube defects, should get 4 mg daily through the first trimester
💊

Treatment

  • Typically treated with oral folic acid, 1 to 5 mg daily
  • Folic acid in the diet should be encouraged — fortified cereals, beef liver, lentils, green leafy vegetables, rice, orange juice
  • Parenteral folic acid is available but often not necessary — it can be found in “banana bags” for alcohol use disorder
  • Intravenous folic acid may be appropriate for patients unable to take folic acid orally or who require more urgent correction

☐ The one rule that could harm a patient

Exclude vitamin B12 deficiency BEFORE giving folate alone. Folate corrects the anemia but NOT the neurologic damage of vitamin B12 deficiency. Give folate to a B12-deficient patient and the blood count normalises — removing the very signal that would have prompted you to look further — while the subacute combined degeneration of the cord progresses silently and irreversibly.

🧠 Why chronic alcohol use is on the at-risk list twice

Chronic alcohol use causes folate deficiency through poor dietary intake, impaired absorption and impaired hepatic storage at once — which is why it appears in the prevention list and why parenteral folic acid is a component of the “banana bag” given in alcohol use disorder. It is also why the monitoring slide tells you to reassess diet, alcohol use and interacting drugs when the response is inadequate.

Q: A 56-year-old woman with megaloblastic anemia has a history of alcohol use disorder. Which of the following is the best treatment option — oral vitamin B12, parenteral vitamin B12, oral folic acid, or oral vitamin B12 with oral folic acid?

Answer — oral vitamin B12 with oral folic acid

Chronic alcohol use is a named risk factor for folate deficiency, so folate replacement is clearly needed — oral folic acid, 1 to 5 mg daily. But you must exclude or simultaneously treat vitamin B12 deficiency first, because folate corrects the anemia but not the neurologic damage. Since both deficiencies produce an identical macrocytic picture and can coexist in a malnourished patient, covering both is the safe answer. If you could only choose one test to separate them, it would be methylmalonic acid — raised in B12 deficiency, normal in folate deficiency.

Q: A woman whose first child was born with spina bifida is planning another pregnancy. What folic acid dose do you recommend, and for how long?

Answer

4 mg daily through the first trimester — the high-dose regimen for patients who have previously given birth to offspring with neural tube defects or who have a family history of them. This is roughly seven times the standard pregnancy dose of 600 mcg daily and ten times the adult recommended daily allowance of 400 mcg. The neural tube closes early, so supplementation must begin before conception, not when pregnancy is confirmed.

Folate is fastest (2–5 days), then vitamin B12 (3–5 days, peaking about day 7), then iron (5–7 days). All three are within a week — so a patient with no reticulocyte response at two weeks either has the wrong diagnosis, is not taking the drug, or has a second problem (ongoing blood loss, inflammation, a coexisting deficiency).

💊 Two duration rules worth separating

Iron: continue 3–6 months after the haemoglobin normalises — because normalising the haemoglobin only refills the circulating red cells; the stores (ferritin) are still empty and that is what prevents relapse. Vitamin B12: lifelong therapy if the cause is not reversible — pernicious anemia or gastrectomy never stops being a malabsorption problem.

Q: A patient's haemoglobin normalises after 10 weeks of oral iron. Can you stop the iron?

Answer

No. Continue for 3 to 6 months after the haemoglobin normalises in order to replenish iron stores. A normal haemoglobin means the circulating red cell mass has been restored, but ferritin and transferrin saturation may still be low — stopping now guarantees relapse. Check ferritin and transferrin saturation once repleted to confirm the stores are genuinely full.

Q: Three days after starting therapy for a macrocytic anemia the reticulocyte count has risen sharply, and on day 5 the potassium is 3.1 mmol/L. What is being treated and what is happening?

Answer

Vitamin B12 deficiency — reticulocytosis appears at 3–5 days, peaking about day 7 — and the falling potassium is the expected hypokalaemia from a marked increase in potassium utilisation during production of new haematopoietic cells. This is precisely why the monitoring slide says to check serum potassium in the first week. Replace potassium and continue therapy; also track neurologic symptoms, and remember therapy is lifelong if the cause is not reversible.

Mechanism — where in erythropoiesis the ESAs act

Slide 26 — Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents: steps of erythropoiesis and where ESAs act

  1. Haematopoietic stem cell — multipotent marrow stem cell commits to the erythroid lineage
  2. BFU-E (burst-forming unit-erythroid) — earliest committed erythroid progenitor; begins to express the EPO receptor. ESA ACTS
  3. CFU-E (colony-forming unit-erythroid) — highest EPO-receptor density, the most ESA-responsive stage. ESA ACTS
  4. Pronormoblast (proerythroblast) — haemoglobin synthesis begins; ~4 divisions yield ~16 red cells
  5. Basophilic → polychromatophilic → orthochromatic normoblast — cell shrinks, chromatin condenses, nucleus is extruded
  6. Reticulocyte — ~7 days in marrow, then released; matures in blood over ~1–2 days
  7. Mature erythrocyte — circulates ~120 days; ~26 days total from stem cell to red blood cell
🧠 The ESA mechanism in one sentence

Epoetin alfa and darbepoetin alfa bind the EPO receptor on BFU-E and CFU-E, activating JAK2/STAT5 to block apoptosis and drive proliferation and differentiation — reticulocytes rise within days, haemoglobin over 2–6 weeks.

Epoetin alfa, darbepoetin alfa and methoxy PEG-epoetin beta engage this same receptor at step 1 and run the identical cascade. Darbepoetin's extra glycosylation and Mircera's PEGylation extend half-life — permitting less frequent dosing — but they do not change the mechanism. Note also that the same JAK2/STAT5 axis you met as a targeted-therapy pathway in the antineoplastic lecture is the physiological signal here.

🧠 The elegant part of the vadadustat mechanism

Prolyl hydroxylases are the oxygen sensors that mark hypoxia-inducible factor for destruction when oxygen is plentiful. Inhibit them and HIF is stabilised — the cell behaves as though it were hypoxic and transcribes the erythropoietin gene in kidney and liver. So where an ESA supplies erythropoietin from outside, vadadustat makes the patient produce their own. Same endpoint, and — as the boxed warning shows — the same class of thrombotic risk.

Q: A 63-year-old woman with anemia secondary to chronic kidney disease and a haemoglobin of 8.6 g/dL is treated with epoetin alfa. Eight days after the initial dose her haemoglobin is 10.5 g/dL. What is the most appropriate next step — discontinue epoetin alfa; discontinue and switch to darbepoetin; continue epoetin alfa; or increase the dose?

Answer — discontinue epoetin alfa

The initiation threshold for chronic kidney disease is a haemoglobin less than 10 g/dL, and she is now at 10.5 g/dL — above it, after a rapid rise. The dosing principle is “the lowest ESA dose sufficient to reduce the need for red blood cell transfusions”, and the boxed warnings cover cardiovascular events and chronic kidney disease: continuing or increasing the dose would push the haemoglobin higher and raise the risk of hypertension and thrombosis for no transfusion benefit. Switching to darbepoetin changes nothing — it is the same mechanism with a longer half-life.

Q: A dialysis patient started on an ESA six weeks ago has had no rise in haemoglobin. Before increasing the dose, what should you check?

Answer

Do not escalate the dose prematurely — check iron, inflammation, blood loss, and vitamin B12 or folate first. An ESA supplies the signal, but the marrow still needs the building blocks: without adequate iron the progenitors cannot make haemoglobin no matter how strongly they are stimulated. Also remember the expected timeline — reticulocytes rise within days but haemoglobin takes 2–6 weeks, because it is ~26 days from stem cell to mature erythrocyte. And consider antibody-associated pure red cell aplasia if the haemoglobin is actually falling with a very low reticulocyte count.

Q: How does vadadustat differ mechanistically from epoetin alfa, and what is the key safety restriction?

Answer

Epoetin alfa is exogenous erythropoietin — it binds the EPO receptor on BFU-E and CFU-E directly and activates JAK2/STAT5. Vadadustat is an oral hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor: it reversibly inhibits PH1, PH2 and PH3, stabilising HIF so the erythropoietin gene is transcribed in the kidneys and liver, raising the patient's own endogenous erythropoietin. Safety restriction: its boxed warning covers death, myocardial infarction, stroke, venous thromboembolism and thrombosis of vascular access, and it must be avoided in patients with a myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular event or acute coronary syndrome within the previous 3 months. It is licensed only for dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease of ≥ 3 months.

The Lecture's Practice Questions

Question #1: A 45-year-old woman visits her physician for follow-up after an emergency room visit where she was diagnosed with anemia. She reports taking a pill three times a day for anemia and is concerned because her stool seems darker. Which medication is she most likely taking? A Ferrous gluconate · B Folic acid · C Iron dextran · D Vitamin B12

Answer — A, Ferrous gluconate

Two clues, both pointing to oral iron. First, black/green or tarry stools are a hallmark of oral iron — the deck notes patients are bothered by stools that stain clothing or cause anxiety about bleeding. Second, it is a pill taken three times a day. Iron dextran is intravenous, so it is out despite being an iron. Folic acid and vitamin B12 do not discolour the stool. Ferrous gluconate is an oral iron salt (~10–14% elemental iron; a 324 mg tablet delivers 38 mg elemental).

Question #2: A 20-year-old woman on aggressive chemotherapy for ovarian carcinoma presents with severe fatigue and significant iron deficiency anemia. Therapy with iron dextran is recommended. Which is most appropriate to recommend prior to initiating this therapy? A Recheck haemoglobin levels · B Give a test dose · C Pretreat with antinausea medication · D Start a blood transfusion

Answer — B, Give a test dose

Low molecular weight iron dextran requires a test dose of 25 mg (0.5 mL) prior to the first dose — it is the only intravenous iron on the list where a test dose is required rather than merely recommended, because dextran-containing preparations carry the greatest risk of life-threatening anaphylaxis. Rechecking the haemoglobin adds nothing before an already-indicated infusion; antiemetic pretreatment addresses oral iron's gastrointestinal effects, not an intravenous infusion; and transfusion is reserved for haemoglobin < 7–8 g/dL or haemodynamic compromise / cardiac ischaemia, which is not described here.

Question #3: A 56-year-old woman is discovered to have megaloblastic anemia. Her past medical history is significant for alcohol use disorder. Which would be the best treatment option? A Oral vitamin B12 · B Parenteral vitamin B12 · C Oral folic acid · D Oral vitamin B12 with oral folic acid

Answer — D, Oral vitamin B12 with oral folic acid

Chronic alcohol use is a named risk factor for folate deficiency, and folate is treated with oral folic acid 1–5 mg daily with dietary encouragement. But folate corrects the anemia and NOT the neurologic damage of vitamin B12 deficiency — and both deficiencies produce an identical macrocytic picture, are both common in malnutrition, and frequently coexist. Giving folate alone risks masking an unrecognised B12 deficiency while subacute combined degeneration progresses. Covering both is the safe choice. Her gut is intact and there is no stated malabsorption or adherence concern, so the oral route is appropriate for both.

Question #4: A 63-year-old woman with anemia secondary to chronic kidney disease and a haemoglobin of 8.6 g/dL is treated with epoetin alfa. Eight days after the initial dose her haemoglobin is 10.5 g/dL. What is the most appropriate next step? A Discontinue epoetin alfa · B Discontinue epoetin alfa and initiate darbepoetin · C Continue epoetin alfa · D Increase the dose of epoetin alfa

Answer — A, Discontinue epoetin alfa

Her haemoglobin has risen from 8.6 to 10.5 g/dL in eight days, which is above the < 10 g/dL threshold at which ESA treatment is indicated in chronic kidney disease. The governing principle is “the lowest ESA dose sufficient to reduce the need for red blood cell transfusions”, and the boxed warnings cover cardiovascular events and chronic kidney disease — continuing (C) or increasing (D) exposes her to hypertension, thrombosis and seizures with no transfusion benefit. Switching to darbepoetin (B) is pointless: it is the same receptor, same JAK2/STAT5 cascade, differing only in half-life.

  • Parenteral if: Hb < 8 g/dL · neurologic/neuropsychiatric symptoms · likely malabsorption · adherence/follow-up concern. Otherwise either, by shared decision-making
  • Adverse effects rare; hypokalaemia from ↑K utilisation making new cells; rare hypersensitivity

Folic acid (LO 8–10) — the five doses

  • 400 mcg = adult RDA
  • 600 mcg = pregnancy (prevent anencephaly, spina bifida)
  • 1 mg = prevention in at-risk (malnutrition, chronic alcohol use, chronic haemolytic anemia)
  • 4 mg through 1st trimester = prior or family history of neural tube defect
  • 1–5 mg = treatment of established deficiency
  • Adverse: flushing, malaise, erythema, pruritus, rash, hypersensitivity, bronchospasm
  • ⚠ EXCLUDE B12 DEFICIENCY FIRST — folate corrects the anemia but NOT the neurologic damage
LO 5,7,9 MONITORING + LO 11 ESAs

The reticulocyte clock

  • Folate: 2–5 days; Hb and MCV improve over 2–8 weeks
  • B12: 3–5 days, peak ~day 7; MCV normalises over 1–2 months
  • Iron: 5–7 days; Hb rises 1–2 g/dL every 2–3 weeks
  • Iron duration: continue 3–6 months AFTER Hb normalises to refill stores
  • B12: lifelong if the cause is not reversible; check K+ in the first week; track neurologic symptoms
  • Folate: CBC at ~1 month; reassess diet, alcohol, interacting drugs (methotrexate, phenytoin)

ESAs — the agents

  • Epoetin alfa (Epogen, Procrit; biosimilar Retacrit): chemo anemia + CKD
  • Darbepoetin alfa (Aranesp): chemo anemia + CKD
  • Methoxy PEG-epoetin beta (Mircera): CKD ONLY
  • All SUBQ or IV

ESA mechanism

  • Bind the EPO receptor on BFU-E and CFU-E (CFU-E = highest receptor density)
  • Preformed EPOR homodimer → conformational change → JAK2 transphosphorylation → EPOR tyrosine phosphorylation → STAT5, PI3K/AKT, RAS/MAPK → STAT5 dimerises → BCL-xL → blocks apoptosis
  • Terminated by SHP-1, SOCS3, CIS
  • Glycosylation/PEGylation extend half-life only — same mechanism
  • Timeline: ~26 days stem cell → RBC; retics in days, Hb over 2–6 weeks; RBC lives ~120 days

ESA safety & when to start

  • Adverse: hypertension, thrombosis, seizures, hypersensitivity, SJS/TEN, antibody-associated pure red cell aplasia
  • Boxed: cardiovascular events · chronic kidney disease · cancer (epoetin alfa also perisurgery)
  • Contraindicated: serious hypersensitivity · uncontrolled hypertension · PRCA after an ESA
  • Start when Hb < 10 g/dL in nondialysis and dialysis CKD
  • Chemo anemia: only when treatment is NOT curative in intent and Hb ≤ 10 g/dL
  • Lowest dose sufficient to avoid transfusion; goal Hb unclear

Vadadustat — oral HIF-PH inhibitor

  • Reversibly inhibits PH1, PH2, PH3 → stabilises HIF → transcribes the EPO gene in kidney and liver → ↑endogenous erythropoietin
  • Use: CKD anemia, dialysis-dependent ≥ 3 months
  • Adverse: GI erosions/bleeding, hepatotoxicity, hypertension, seizures, malignancy
  • Boxed: death, MI, stroke, VTE, thrombosis of vascular access
  • Avoid within 3 months of MI, cerebrovascular event or acute coronary syndrome
  • Contraindicated: hypersensitivity, uncontrolled hypertension. Pregnancy unknown
