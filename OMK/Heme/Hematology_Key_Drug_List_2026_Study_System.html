Two machines, manipulated every way medicine knows how

Heme pharm looks like a wall of unrelated drugs. It is really two machines. The erythron makes red cells — iron in, vitamins in, erythropoietin signal on, hemoglobin out. The hemostasis machine makes a clot — platelets stick, the cascade fires, fibrin sets, plasmin dissolves. Learn each machine once; then every drug is just a labeled hand reaching into a specific gear.

Memory Hook — the master rule of thumb
Every anemia drug answers one question: "which ingredient is missing?" — iron, vitamin B12, folate, or the erythropoietin signal itself. Every clotting drug answers a different one: "which step am I cutting?" — platelet sticking, cascade firing, fibrin setting, or clot dissolving. Name the missing ingredient or the cut step and the drug names itself.

Reading an anemia before you treat it know cold

Abbreviations used below — MCV = mean corpuscular volume (average red cell size); TIBC = total iron-binding capacity; TSAT = transferrin saturation (serum iron ÷ TIBC).

  • Microcytic (MCV < 80 fL) → think iron deficiency, thalassemia, anemia of inflammation.
  • Macrocytic (MCV > 100 fL) → think vitamin B12 or folate deficiency (megaloblastic), alcohol, liver disease.
  • Normocytic → think anemia of chronic kidney disease, acute blood loss, hemolysis.
  • The single most useful discriminator between true iron deficiency and anemia of inflammation is ferritin — low in iron deficiency, normal-to-high in inflammation, because ferritin is an acute phase reactant (a protein that rises during inflammation regardless of iron stores).
Clinical pearl
Iron deficiency anemia in an adult is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Replacing the iron without asking where it went misses the colon cancer, the menorrhagia, or the celiac disease that caused it.

Memory Hook — hepcidin is the doorman
Hepcidin has one job: destroy ferroportin, the only exit door iron has out of the gut cell and out of the macrophage. High hepcidin (inflammation, interleukin-6) = doors locked = iron is in the building but not in the blood. That single sentence explains anemia of inflammation, why oral iron fails in it, and why intravenous iron works — it enters past the locked door.

Iron-deficiency anemia · class 1 of 2 · over-the-counter

Oral Iron Therapy

The cheapest drug on the list and the one patients quit most. Nearly every rule about oral iron traces back to one fact: only ferrous (Fe²⁺) iron crosses the gut wall, and only in an acidic duodenum. Everything that raises stomach pH or holds iron in the wrong oxidation state is a failure mode.

My confidence:

Abbreviations in this section — OTC over-the-counter (no prescription needed); GI gastrointestinal; SR sustained-release; PPI proton pump inhibitor; H2RA histamine-2 receptor antagonist; Fe²⁺ ferrous iron; Fe³⁺ ferric iron.

Class blueprint

Oral iron salts as a class

Goal
Replenish iron stores, not just the hemoglobin. Dietary change alone will not do it once someone is genuinely deficient.
Absorption chemistry
Iron is absorbed as the ferrous (Fe²⁺) salt in a mildly acidic medium. Intestinal uptake happens only in the Fe²⁺ form. Gastric acid helps; anything that removes it hurts.
Best dietary sources
Meat, fish, and poultry (heme iron). Milk and tea reduce absorption — allow in moderation only.
Timing
Give at least 1 hour before meals because food interferes with absorption — but giving it with food is an acceptable trade when that is the only way the patient will tolerate it. An absorbed smaller dose beats a refused larger one.
Dosing
Classic adult target: 150–200 mg of elemental iron daily. Newer data favor alternate-day dosing (every other day, or Monday/Wednesday/Friday) — it produces greater total absorption, because each dose spikes hepcidin for roughly a day and a second dose runs into that closed door.
Formulations to avoid
Enteric-coated and sustained-release capsules — they release iron too far distally, past the duodenum where absorption happens, so they are poorly absorbed.
Memory Hook — "Fe²⁺ = Free to enter"
Fe2+ (ferrous) = the "2" that gets through the door. Fe³⁺ (ferric) has to be reduced first. Vitamin C is the reducing agent: it drops the pH and converts Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺. And "E-C and S-R are D-O-A" — Enteric-Coated and Sustained-Release arrive Dead On Arrival, downstream of the absorption window.

Reason it out — why the same dose fails in two different patients
  • Acid suppression: antacids, H2 receptor blockers, and proton pump inhibitors all raise gastric pH and impair iron absorption. Separate them: iron 2 hours before, or 4 hours after, an antacid.
  • Wrong oxidation state: ascorbic acid (vitamin C) lowers gastric pH and converts Fe³⁺ to the absorbable Fe²⁺. Note the nuance: co-administration is not routinely recommended in current guidance, although many clinicians still do it.
  • Wrong place in the bowel: enteric-coated/sustained-release dissolve past the duodenum.
  • Wrong patient entirely: a locked ferroportin door (inflammation) or a bypassed duodenum (gastric surgery) means no oral dose will work.
Side effects — the real reason therapy fails
Gastrointestinal side effects are extremely common: metallic taste, nausea, flatulence, constipation, diarrhea, epigastric distress, vomiting. Patients are also bothered by itching and by black/green or tarry stools that stain clothing and cause alarm about bleeding. The result is poor adherence — which is the single most common reason a "treatment failure" is not a treatment failure at all.

When to give up on the oral route high yield

Switch to parenteral iron for:

  • Iron malabsorption
  • Intolerance of oral iron — cannot get past the GI side effects
  • Nonadherence
  • Severe or ongoing blood loss — losing it faster than the gut can absorb it
  • Gastric surgery (bypass, resection) — less gastric acid and a bypassed duodenum severely impair absorption
  • Malabsorption syndromes — celiac disease, Whipple disease, bacterial overgrowth
Clinical pearl — knowing it worked
Expect a reticulocyte bump at 7–10 days and a hemoglobin rise of roughly 1 g/dL every 2–3 weeks. Then keep going: continue iron for 3–6 months after the hemoglobin normalizes to refill ferritin. Stopping when the hemoglobin looks pretty is the classic under-treatment.

Ferric maltol (Accrufer) — the escape hatch

A non-salt-based oral iron for adults with iron deficiency, and the reason a patient who cannot tolerate ferrous sulfate no longer has to jump straight to an infusion.

  • Because iron is absorbed off the ferric maltol molecule rather than as a salt, it does not routinely cause the salt-driven nausea, bloating, and constipation that wreck adherence.
  • Before ferric maltol, intravenous iron was the only realistic alternative for patients intolerant of or unwilling to take salt-based oral iron — an invasive, costly, inconvenient option carrying a small risk of spontaneous hypersensitivity reactions.

Iron-deficiency anemia · class 2 of 2 · hospital & infusion centers

Parenteral (Intravenous) Iron

Not available over the counter, and usually given in hospitals or infusion settings. It bypasses the entire absorption problem — the duodenum, the acid, the hepcidin-locked door — and delivers iron straight to transferrin. The price is infusion reactions and one product-specific electrolyte trap.

My confidence:

Abbreviations in this section — IV intravenous; FGF23 fibroblast growth factor 23 (a hormone that makes the kidney dump phosphate); PO4 phosphate; NYHA New York Heart Association (heart failure symptom classes I–IV); HF heart failure; HFrEF heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; Hgb hemoglobin.

Class blueprint

Every IV iron product, same three rules

Administration
Administer every IV iron product slowly at first and observe the patient for infusion reactions.
Non-allergic infusion reactions
Common and self-limiting: urticaria, palpitations, dizziness, and neck and back spasm. This is not anaphylaxis — recognizing the difference prevents needlessly labeling a patient iron-allergic for life.
True adverse effects
Allergic reactions including potentially life-threatening anaphylaxis (exceedingly rare), hypotension, dizziness, dyspnea, headache, lower back pain, arthralgia, syncope, arthritis. Many are minimized by decreasing the dose or the rate of infusion.
Premedication
For asthma or more than one drug allergy — slightly higher reaction risk — premedicate with methylprednisolone 125 mg IV.
Do NOT premedicate with diphenhydramine
Counterintuitive and heavily testable. Diphenhydramine does not prevent the reaction and causes its own: hypotension, somnolence, flushing, dizziness, irritability, nasal congestion, wheezing, and supraventricular tachycardia — a list that looks exactly like the infusion reaction you were trying to prevent, which then gets misattributed to the iron. Use methylprednisolone 125 mg IV instead.

Mechanism worth knowing because it explains why this one product and not the others: ferric carboxymaltose raises FGF23, which increases the urinary fractional excretion of phosphate → phosphate is dumped in the urine → serum phosphate falls. Severe, symptomatic hypophosphatemia (weakness, bone pain, in extreme cases osteomalacia) has been reported with repeated dosing. Check a phosphate level in patients getting repeat courses.
Clinical pearl — iron as a heart failure drug
Ferric carboxymaltose is approved for NYHA class II/III heart failure patients with HFrEF and iron deficiency, with or without anemia, to improve exercise capacity. Iron deficiency is present in roughly 30% of heart failure patients. Note the striking part: the patient does not have to be anemic — this is treating the iron-starved myocyte, not the hemoglobin.

Related: IV iron may be useful in post-operative anemia (usually multifactorial), where recent data suggest a mean hemoglobin increase of about 0.45 g/dL.

Memory Hook — "CarboxyMaltose Munches Phosphate"
Carboxymaltose → Crashes phosphate (via FGF23), and Cardiac indication (heart failure). Three C's, one drug. And for premedication: "Steroid yes, Benadryl no."
Megaloblastic anemia · 1 of 2

Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin) Deficiency

A macrocytic anemia with a neurological tail. The drug itself is almost free of side effects — the whole exam value of this section is in what causes the deficiency (two extremely common medications) and what happens if you treat the wrong vitamin.

My confidence:

Abbreviations in this section — B12 vitamin B12, also called cobalamin; IF intrinsic factor (the stomach protein B12 must bind to be absorbed in the ileum); PPI proton pump inhibitor; Ca²⁺ calcium; IM intramuscular; SubQ subcutaneous.

Two medications cause this

The drug-induced B12 deficiencies know cold

Metformin
Affects the calcium-dependent absorption of the vitamin B12–intrinsic factor complex in the ileum. The complex forms fine; it just cannot be taken up at the end of the line.
Proton pump inhibitors
Gastric acidity is needed to release protein-bound B12 from food. No acid → B12 stays stuck to dietary protein → less available for absorption. (Same theme as oral iron: PPIs break both.)

Both are among the most-prescribed drugs in medicine, which is exactly why they show up as exam stems. A diabetic on long-term metformin with a rising mean corpuscular volume and paresthesias is not a mystery.

Available forms — over-the-counter versus prescription
CategoryFormsWhere it fits
Nonprescription vitamin B12Multivitamins (including children's chewables and liquid drops), B-complex vitamins, individual supplementsDietary insufficiency; mild drug-induced deficiency
Prescription cyanocobalaminOral, intranasal (Nascobal), and injectable (intramuscular and subcutaneous)Established deficiency; the injectable and intranasal routes bypass a broken gut entirely
Reason it out — the two rare side effects
Adverse effects with B12 replacement are rare. The uncommon ones both come from the same event — you suddenly restart a stalled bone marrow:
  • Hypokalemia — a marked increase in potassium utilization as new hematopoietic cells are built pulls potassium out of the serum and into the cells.
  • Hyperuricemia — the turnover of nucleic acids in that burst of cell production dumps urate.
The trap — never treat a B12 deficiency with folate alone
Giving folic acid to a B12-deficient patient corrects the anemia — the blood count looks treated — while the neurological damage silently progresses (subacute combined degeneration of the dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts: numbness, proprioceptive loss, ataxia, eventually irreversible). Check B12 before, or replace both. This is standard teaching layered onto the drug list because it is the one way vitamin therapy actually hurts someone.
Memory Hook — "MetForMin and the PPI Pinch B12"
Metformin pinches it at the ileum (calcium-dependent, at the end of the tube). The PPI pinches it at the stomach (no acid, no release from food). Two drugs, two ends of the gut, one vitamin. And: "B12 fixes the nerves; folate only fixes the numbers."
Megaloblastic anemia · 2 of 2

Folate-Deficiency Anemia

There are two forms of vitamin B9: folic acid and folinic acid. They are not interchangeable, and the difference — one needs an enzyme, one does not — is the entire reason leucovorin rescue works.

My confidence:

Abbreviations in this section — DHFR dihydrofolate reductase (the enzyme that activates folic acid); MTHFR methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (a gene commonly carrying activity-reducing variants); TPN total parenteral nutrition (all nutrition given intravenously); AUD alcohol use disorder; NTD neural tube defect; IM intramuscular; SubQ subcutaneous; IV intravenous.

Class blueprint

Folic acid (folate)

Availability
Over the counter as oral formulations; by prescription as oral and injectable (IM, SubQ, or IV).
Standard replacement
Folic acid 1 mg daily — in most cases sufficient to replace stores.
Malabsorption
1 to 5 mg daily may be necessary.
Anticonvulsant-induced megaloblastic anemia
Low-dose folate, 500 micrograms daily — so that the anticonvulsant does not have to be discontinued. Treat the side effect, keep the seizure control.
Chronic high-requirement states
Long-term administration may be necessary (chronic hemolysis, pregnancy, dialysis). Encourage folate-rich foods alongside.
Toxicity
None reported at replacement doses — considered nontoxic even at high doses and rapidly excreted in the urine.

Parenteral folate — rarely for the anemia, often for the alcoholic

Parenteral folic acid exists but is rarely necessary for folate-deficiency anemia itself. Where it actually gets used:

  • Total parenteral nutrition
  • "Banana bags" — the vitamin/electrolyte replacement given in chronic alcohol use disorder. Typical contents: thiamine 100 mg, folic acid 1 mg, magnesium 1–2 g, and a multivitamin, in either normal saline or dextrose in water.

The yellow color that gives the bag its name is the multivitamin, chiefly riboflavin.

Clinical pearl — why prenatal vitamins carry extra folate
Prenatal vitamins contain more folic acid than general multivitamins. Women of childbearing years must maintain adequate folic acid intake to prevent neural tube defects — specifically anencephaly and spina bifida. The timing is the point: the neural tube closes at about 4 weeks, often before a pregnancy is recognized — which is why the recommendation is to supplement before conception, not after a positive test.

Reason it out — Leucovorin Rescue
Methotrexate inhibits DHFR. So in a methotrexate-poisoned patient, giving folic acid is useless — the enzyme that would activate it is exactly the one that is blocked. Folinic acid (leucovorin) is already past that step, so it restores the folate pool without needing the blocked enzyme. That is "rescue": you are not reversing methotrexate, you are handing the cell an activated folate it can still use.
Memory Hook — "FoliNic is fiNished"
FoliNic is already activated — N for "No enzyme needed". Folic acid still needs DHFR, which methotrexate has already broken. Rescue the cell with the vitamin that does not need the broken machine.
Anemia of inflammation · chronic kidney disease · chemotherapy

Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents & the HIF Drugs

When the ingredients are all present but the build order never arrives. These drugs supply or amplify the erythropoietin signal — and they carry the heaviest boxed warnings in the whole block, because pushing hemoglobin too high kills people.

My confidence:

Abbreviations in this section — AI anemia of inflammation; CKD chronic kidney disease; ESA erythropoiesis-stimulating agent; EPO erythropoietin; Fe iron; Hgb hemoglobin; HTN hypertension (high blood pressure); BP blood pressure; SC / SubQ subcutaneous; IV intravenous; MI myocardial infarction (heart attack); DVT deep venous thrombosis; RBC red blood cell; HIF-PHI hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor; ACS acute coronary syndrome.

Anemia of inflammation — the disease before the drug

Anemia of inflammation (AI) is the newer term covering both anemia of chronic disease and anemia of critical illness; the new name reflects the inflammatory process underlying both. It is a diagnosis of exclusion. It results from chronic inflammation, infection, or malignancy and can appear as early as 1 to 2 months after disease onset.

Mechanism, in one chain: inflammation → upregulated cytokines (interleukin-6) → hepcidin ↑. Hepcidin is an iron-regulatory protein expressed in the liver and an acute phase reactant — which is why hepcidin levels track with serum ferritin. High hepcidin destroys ferroportin, so iron is sequestered in macrophages and enterocytes, leaving less available for hemoglobin synthesis. The result is iron-restricted erythropoiesis: plenty of iron in the body, none of it reachable.

Clinical pearl — why the ferritin lies
Ferritin is an acute phase reactant too. In pure iron deficiency ferritin is low. In anemia of inflammation it is normal or high even though usable iron is scarce. When both coexist (common in chronic kidney disease and inflammatory bowel disease), ferritin can look reassuring while the patient is genuinely iron-starved — which is when transferrin saturation earns its keep.
Class blueprint

Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents (ESAs)

The agents
Recombinant epoetin alfa and recombinant darbepoetin alfa. Same mechanism; darbepoetin has a longer half-life and is dosed less frequently. Methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta is the longest-acting of the group.
Pharmacologic category
Colony Stimulating Factor; Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agent (ESA); Hematopoietic Agent
Mechanism of action
ESAs induce erythropoiesis by stimulating the division and differentiation of committed erythroid progenitor cells, and induce release of reticulocytes from the bone marrow into the bloodstream, where they mature to erythrocytes. There is a dose–response relationship: reticulocyte count rises first, then hematocrit and hemoglobin.
Indications
Although sometimes used in anemia of inflammation, they are not FDA-approved for that indication. Their approved home is anemia of chronic kidney disease and anemia due to chemotherapy in cancer patients.
Route
All available ESAs may be given IV or subcutaneously. Subcutaneous epoetin has a prolonged absorption phase → extended half-life → target hemoglobin maintained at doses 15% to 30% lower than IV.
Reason it out — the hypertension caveats
Hypertension is the most common adverse effect (likely multifactorial; occurs in up to one third of erythropoietin-treated patients). But the clinical picture has nuance worth knowing:
  • Less blood pressure rise if given subcutaneously — possibly because hemoglobin rises more slowly.
  • Much of the historical hypertension risk predated current guidelines, when a higher target hemoglobin was used.
  • Risk may be independent of the target hemoglobin in some patients — though it is consistently worse when the rise is rapid.
  • Antiplatelet medications may mitigate erythropoietin-induced hypertension.

Other adverse effects

Increases in nausea, headache, fever, bone pain, and fatigue. Less common: seizures, thrombotic events, and allergic reactions such as rashes and local injection-site reactions.

US Boxed Warnings — all four, and they share one sentence

Every one of these ends in "use the lowest dose needed to avoid transfusion."

  • Cardiovascular events: ESAs increased the risk of serious cardiovascular events, myocardial infarction, stroke, venous thromboembolism, vascular access thrombosis, and death when administered to target hemoglobin levels above 11 g/dL — and provide no additional benefit there.
  • Cancer patients: shortened overall survival and/or increased tumor progression or recurrence in breast, cervical, head and neck, lymphoid, and non-small cell lung cancer. Increased thromboembolic reactions, some life-threatening. Use ESAs in cancer only for anemia from concurrent myelosuppressive chemotherapy, and discontinue when the chemotherapy course finishes. Not indicated when the anticipated outcome is curative.
  • Chronic kidney disease patients: increased death, serious cardiovascular events, and stroke when targeting hemoglobin above 11 g/dL. No optimal target, dose, or strategy has been identified in trials.
  • Perisurgery patients: increased deep venous thrombosis in patients receiving epoetin alfa for orthopedic surgery. DVT prophylaxis is strongly recommended whenever ESAs are used to reduce transfusions in surgical patients.
Warnings and precautions — disease-related concerns

Memory Hook — "Eleven is the ceiling"
Every ESA boxed warning turns on the same number: hemoglobin above 11 g/dL = harm with no benefit. And the rate rule rides alongside it: more than 1 g/dL in 2 weeks = cut the dose. Two numbers — 11 and 1-in-2 — cover most of what gets asked. Think of an ESA as a thermostat you set to "just warm enough not to transfuse," never to "comfortable."
Clinical pearl — iron first, signal second
An ESA drives the marrow to build red cells, and building red cells consumes iron fast. Give an ESA to an iron-deplete patient and you create functional iron deficiency: the order is shouted, the factory has no raw material, and the hemoglobin does not move. In practice, check and replete iron before blaming the ESA for not working — this is the most common reason for apparent ESA resistance.
Newer class

Hypoxia-Inducible Factor (HIF) Prolyl Hydroxylase Inhibitors

The FDA has approved two: vadadustat (Vafseo) and daprodustat (Jesduvroq) — note that daprodustat was withdrawn from the US market in late 2024 for commercial reasons, not safety.

Pharmacologic category
Hypoxia-inducible factor prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor (HIF-PHI)
Mechanism of action
HIF is a key regulator of the erythropoietin gene. Normally, prolyl hydroxylases tag HIF for destruction whenever oxygen is plentiful. By inhibiting HIF-prolyl hydroxylases, vadadustat raises intracellular HIF levels, activates the oxygen-sensing pathway, and stimulates erythropoiesis — it convinces the cell it is at altitude.
Indication
Oral treatment of anemia due to chronic kidney disease in adults on dialysis for at least 3 months. The oral route is the entire selling point against injected ESAs.
Adverse effects
Hypertension, diarrhea, headache, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, abdominal pain.
Boxed warning & contraindications
Boxed warning for increased risk of thrombotic events. Like the ESAs, contraindicated in uncontrolled hypertension, and in acute coronary syndrome or cerebrovascular events within 3 months. Caution in patients with malignancies — the pathway can stimulate cancer growth.
Reason it out — HIF does more than make erythropoietin
Raising HIF does not just turn up the signal; it also improves the iron supply chain. HIF-2 upregulates DCYTB and DMT1 in the enterocyte (better dietary iron uptake) and increases ferroportin surface expression and iron mobilization — so a HIF-PHI can work in patients whose problem is partly hepcidin-driven iron restriction. Note the asterisk from the original figure: hepcidin itself is not a direct HIF target; the effect is indirect, largely through erythroferrone released by the stimulated marrow.
Memory Hook — "Fake altitude in a pill"
A HIF-PHI is a mountain in a tablet: block the enzyme that says "there's plenty of oxygen," HIF survives, the erythropoietin gene switches on. Which also tells you the side effects — everything a body at altitude with thicker blood gets: hypertension and clots.

Hemoglobinopathy · one polymer, four strategies

Sickle-Cell Disease

One amino acid substitution makes hemoglobin polymerize when deoxygenated. Every therapy on this list is an attack on that polymer: dilute it with fetal hemoglobin, dilute it with donor blood, or rewrite the gene.

My confidence:

Abbreviations in this section — SCD sickle cell disease; HbF fetal hemoglobin; HbS sickle hemoglobin; HU hydroxyurea; RBC red blood cell; NO nitric oxide; DNA deoxyribonucleic acid; CRISPR/Cas9 a genome-editing technology; BCL11A the gene that switches fetal hemoglobin off after birth.

Why fetal hemoglobin is the target

Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) directly affects polymer formation. Increases in HbF correlate with decreased red cell sickling and adhesion. Patients with low HbF levels have more frequent pain and higher mortality. The threshold to remember: HbF of 20% or greater reduces the risk of acute sickle cell complications.

Prototype · fetal hemoglobin inducer

Hydroxyurea (Droxia; Hydrea)

Pharmacologic category
Antineoplastic Agent; Chemotherapeutic agent — yes, the sickle cell drug is a chemotherapy drug. That is the whole side-effect profile in one line.
Mechanism in sickle cell anemia
  • Stimulates HbF production and increases the number of HbF-containing reticulocytes and intracellular HbF.
  • The exact mechanism is unknown, but is postulated that the cytotoxic effect in the bone marrow stimulates stress erythropoiesis, triggering rapid erythroid regeneration and shifting hemoglobin production toward HbF.
  • Also: increases nitric oxide levels, reduces neutrophils and monocytes, has antioxidant properties, alters the RBC membrane, increases RBC deformability by increasing intracellular water content, and decreases RBC adhesion to the endothelium.
Mechanism as a chemotherapeutic
Antineoplastic activity comes from inhibition of DNA synthesis by blocking the conversion of ribonucleoside to deoxyribonucleotides (it inhibits ribonucleotide reductase).
Indications in SCD
Patients with frequent painful episodes, severe symptomatic anemia, acute chest syndrome, or other severe vaso-occlusive complications.
Starting dose
15 mg/kg as a single daily dose.
Hydroxyurea — two US Boxed Warnings
  • Bone marrow suppression: hydroxyurea may cause severe myelosuppression. Monitor blood counts at baseline and throughout treatment; interrupt treatment and reduce the dose as necessary.
  • Secondary malignancy: hydroxyurea is carcinogenic. Advise sun protection and monitor for malignancies.

Hydroxyurea side effects in full

The most common is bone marrow suppression → neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, anemia, and a decreased reticulocyte count. These hematologic effects usually recover within 2 weeks of discontinuation. Others: dry skin and hyperpigmentation of skin or nails. At higher doses in other populations: myelodysplasia, acute leukemia, and chronic opportunistic infection associated with T-lymphocyte abnormalities. Reproductive toxicity is also a concern.

Memory Hook — "HU: Hides the S, Hurts the marrow"
Hydroxyurea Hikes HbF (hiding HbS in a crowd of fetal hemoglobin) and Hammers the marrow. If you remember it is a chemotherapy drug moonlighting as a sickle cell drug, you can derive every side effect: low counts, skin/nail changes, secondary cancers, reproductive toxicity. And the reticulocyte count falling is expected, not reassuring.

Chronic Transfusion Therapy

  • Red cell transfusions play an important role in managing sickle cell disease. In acute illness they can be life-saving; chronic programs can prevent serious complications.
  • Primary indication for chronic transfusion: primary and secondary stroke prevention and amelioration of organ damage.
  • Goal: achieve and maintain a sickle hemoglobin (HbS) concentration below 30% of total hemoglobin for primary and secondary prevention of neurologic complications.
  • Frequency: usually every 3 to 4 weeks, adjusted to maintain the desired HbS level.
  • Risks: alloimmunization, hyperviscosity, viral transmission (so vaccinate against hepatitis A and B), volume and iron overload, and transfusion reactions.
  • Transfusional iron overload is its own complication — counsel patients to avoid excess dietary iron, and see the chelation card in the thalassemia section.
Curative · one-time

Gene Therapies for Sickle Cell Disease

Exagamglogene autotemcel (Casgevy)
The first therapy approved in the US to use CRISPR gene-editing technology to edit a patient's gene. It is prepared from the patient's own blood stem cells collected by apheresis; CRISPR/Cas9 then makes a precise edit in the gene that controls fetal hemoglobin production — the switch that normally shuts HbF off shortly after birth. After infusion the cells engraft in the marrow and make red cells containing fetal hemoglobin; above a certain level of HbF, the cells do not sickle or become sticky. This is a reprogramming of the patient's own genes — nothing foreign is added.
Levotibeglogene autotemcel (Lyfgenia)
Prepared using a lentiviral vector to insert a novel beta-globin gene into stem cells. The modified cells produce a novel hemoglobin (HbAT87Q) that inhibits HbS polymerization and sickling.
Shared approval
Both are approved for sickle cell disease in patients ≥ 12 years old with recurrent vaso-occlusive crises.
Memory Hook — "Casgevy Cuts, Lyfgenia Lends"
Casgevy = CRISPR Cuts the patient's own switch (BCL11A) so fetal hemoglobin comes back on. Lyfgenia = Lentivirus Lends a brand-new beta-globin gene. Editing versus adding — that is the only distinction you need, and both need the patient to be 12 or older with recurrent crises.

Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria (PNH)

A rare disorder — an estimated 38 cases per 1,000,000 persons — caused by a severe deficiency in the complement regulatory pathway (CD55 + CD59) on the surface of red blood cells, resulting in intravascular hemolysis: hemolytic anemia, hemoglobinuria, fatigue, shortness of breath, and abdominal pain. It is a highly prothrombotic state — thrombosis, not anemia, is what kills these patients.

The three parenteral drugs
C5 complement inhibitors — eculizumab (Soliris) and ravulizumab (Ultomiris); and the C3 inhibitor — pegcetacoplan (Empaveli).
The first oral drug
Iptacopan (Fabhalta) is the first oral FDA-approved drug for PNH.
Iptacopan mechanism
Inhibits complement factor B, which prevents conversion of C3 to its active fragment C3b, decreasing downstream C5 activity, terminal complement activation, and lysis of red blood cells. Because it acts high in the cascade it can reduce both intravascular and extravascular hemolysis — the C5 drugs only stop the intravascular half.
Iptacopan adverse effects
Headache, nasopharyngitis, nausea, diarrhea, rash, abdominal pain, and infections.
Every complement inhibitor — meningococcal disease
Blocking terminal complement removes the exact defense the body uses against encapsulated organisms. All complement inhibitors carry a boxed warning for serious, life-threatening meningococcal infection. Patients must be vaccinated against Neisseria meningitidis before starting (and are enrolled in a risk-management program). This is standard labeling for the class and is the most reliable exam question about it.
Memory Hook — "C3 catches more than C5"
The higher up the complement cascade you block, the more hemolysis you stop: C5 blockers (eculizumab, ravulizumab) stop the membrane attack complex — intravascular hemolysis only, leaving C3b-coated cells to be eaten in the spleen. C3 (pegcetacoplan) and factor B (iptacopan) block earlier, so they stop both. And the price of blocking any of it is the same: meningococcus.

Prototype · short-acting

Filgrastim

What it is
A short-acting recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor used to prevent febrile neutropenia in patients undergoing myelosuppressive chemotherapy.
Mechanism
Stimulates neutrophil production to shorten the duration of neutropenia — it does not prevent the chemotherapy from wiping out the marrow; it shortens the window during which the patient is defenseless.

Reason it out — three ways to make one protein last
  • Attach polyethylene glycol (pegylation). Hemophilia A and B

A missing factor used to mean a lifetime of infusions. Now there are four ways around it: mimic the missing factor, insert the gene, release the brakes, or lower the body's own anticoagulant until the balance tips back. Note the elegance of the last two — they work whether the missing factor is VIII or IX.

Strategy 1 · replace it

Factor replacement therapy

Infusions of lab-made (recombinant) or plasma-derived clotting factors to stop or prevent bleeds — intravenous infusions of clotting factor concentrates, replacing factor VIII in hemophilia A and factor IX in hemophilia B. Simple, effective, and the reason every other strategy on this page exists: it requires frequent venous access, and some patients develop inhibitors that neutralize it.

Strategy 2 · mimic it

Non-factor therapy — Emicizumab (Hemlibra)

Class
A factor-mimetic antibody — a bispecific monoclonal antibody.
Indication
Routine prophylaxis to prevent or reduce bleeding episodes in adults and children with hemophilia A.
Mechanism
A two-armed design that mimics factor VIII: one arm binds factor IX, the other binds factor X. It physically holds the two together, which is all factor VIII ever did. It bypasses the factor VIII deficiency by activating factor X directly, continuing the cascade without any actual factor VIII.
Why it changed practice
It is a subcutaneous antibody, not an intravenous factor — and because it is not factor VIII, anti-factor-VIII inhibitors do not neutralize it.
Memory Hook — "Emicizumab is a molecular clothespin"
Factor VIII is not an enzyme; it is a clamp that holds factor IXa next to factor X so the reaction can happen. So you do not need to rebuild factor VIII — you need anything with two hands. Emicizumab grabs IX with one arm and X with the other. Bi-specific = two arms = the clothespin.
Strategy 3 · insert the gene

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector-based gene therapy

Strategy 4 · rebalance — works in A and B

Take the brakes off instead of adding the factor

This is the conceptual leap in this section. Hemostasis is a balance between procoagulant factors and natural anticoagulants. If you cannot raise the procoagulant side, lower the anticoagulant side — and the balance tips back either way, no matter which factor is missing.

Anti-TFPI antibodies
These target Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI), a natural anticoagulant. By blocking TFPI they allow the body to generate enough thrombin to form a clot through alternative pathways.
  • Marstacimab-hncq (Hympavzi) — a TFPI antagonist for routine prophylaxis in hemophilia A or B without inhibitors. TFPI is the primary inhibitor of the extrinsic coagulation cascade.
  • Concizumab (Alhemo) — a TFPI antagonist for routine prophylaxis in hemophilia A or B with inhibitors.
Small interfering RNA (siRNA)
Instead of replacing missing clotting factors, siRNA targets the SERPINC1 gene in the liver to lower production of antithrombin, the protein that naturally stops clotting. Lowering antithrombin rebalances the coagulation system, letting the body generate enough thrombin to stop bleeding even when factor VIII or IX levels are extremely low. It works equally well for hemophilia A and B.
  • Fitusiran (Qfitlia) — an antithrombin-directed siRNA for routine prophylaxis in hemophilia A or B without inhibitors. It causes degradation of antithrombin mRNA, reducing plasma antithrombin and restoring hemostasis.
Memory Hook — "With or without inhibitors" is the tie-breaker
Three rebalancing drugs, and the exam splits them on one question — does the patient have inhibitors?
  • Concizumab (Alhemo) → WITH inhibitors. (Concizumab = Complicated patient.)
  • Marstacimab (Hympavzi) and fitusiran (Qfitlia) → WITHOUT inhibitors.
Reason it out — the shared risk of every rebalancing drug
You are deliberately making a bleeding patient more able to clot by removing a natural anticoagulant. So the predictable adverse event for anti-TFPI antibodies and for fitusiran is thrombosis — and for fitusiran specifically, because it is a liver-targeted therapy, hepatotoxicity and gallbladder events. The mechanism tells you the risk before anyone tells you the label.
Beyond the list — one classic worth knowing
Desmopressin (DDAVP) releases stored factor VIII and von Willebrand factor from endothelial cells, and is the classic treatment for mild hemophilia A and type 1 von Willebrand disease. It is useless in hemophilia B — there is no stored factor IX to release. Not on this drug list; noted because it is the standard board pairing with this material.

Indirect Thrombin Inhibitors

Class blueprint

They all work through antithrombin

None of these drugs touches a clotting factor directly. They bind antithrombin and change its shape, turning a slow natural brake into a fast one. What differs between them is which factors the accelerated antithrombin can still reach — and that single difference generates every clinical distinction below.

  • Heparin (unfractionated) is long enough to wrap around both thrombin (IIa) and factor Xa → inhibits both, roughly equally.
  • LMWH is too short to bridge to thrombin → mostly anti-Xa.
  • Fondaparinux is the minimal 5-sugar sequence → pure anti-Xa, nothing else.

Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) — a clotting emergency, not a bleeding one
Antibodies form against the heparin–platelet factor 4 (PF4) complex. Those antibodies then activate platelets. So despite the falling platelet count, the danger is thrombosis, not hemorrhage — limb-threatening and life-threatening clots.
  • Timing: platelets fall 5–10 days after exposure (sooner with recent prior exposure), typically a >50% drop from baseline.
  • Management: stop all heparin — including flushes and coated catheters — and start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, or fondaparinux).
  • Do not start warfarin alone in acute HIT: it drops protein C first and can precipitate venous limb gangrene. Bridge until the platelet count recovers.
Memory Hook — chain length decides everything
Picture the antithrombin molecule as a hand and heparin as the rope that ties it to a target. Long rope (UFH) reaches around to grab thrombin AND Xa. Short rope (LMWH) only reaches Xa. Shortest rope (fondaparinux) reaches Xa only, and nothing can untie it — no protamine. Rope length also predicts protamine's grip: full → partial → none.

Antiplatelet Drugs

Six mechanisms, one destination. A platelet can be activated through several doors — thromboxane, adenosine diphosphate, thrombin — but every path ends at the same place: GP IIb/IIIa opening up so fibrinogen can bridge one platelet to the next. Which door a drug closes determines what it is used for and how it bleeds.

COX inhibitor · the prototype

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid, ASA)

Mechanism
Irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase-1 → no thromboxane A2 → the platelet cannot recruit its neighbors.
Why "irreversible" matters
A platelet has no nucleus and cannot make new enzyme, so the effect lasts the platelet's entire 7–10 day lifespan. That is why you stop aspirin ~7 days before major surgery and why a single dose commits you for a week.
Dose paradox
Low dose is the antiplatelet dose. Higher doses also inhibit COX-2 in the endothelium, which makes prostacyclin — the platelet inhibitor — so pushing the dose can work against you.
Harms
Gastrointestinal ulceration and bleeding, tinnitus at high dose; Reye syndrome in children with viral illness.

Vorapaxar (Zontivity)
Mechanism
Blocks the thrombin receptor on the platelet — the one door the anticoagulants above never close.
Contraindication absolute
History of stroke, TIA, or intracranial hemorrhage — the intracranial bleeding risk is what defines this drug.
Note
Very long-acting; there is no practical way to reverse it.
Phosphodiesterase inhibitors
Dipyridamole · Cilostazol
Mechanism
Inhibit phosphodiesterase → cyclic AMP rises inside the platelet → the platelet stays calm. Both also cause vasodilation, which is where their side effects come from (headache, flushing).
Cilostazol niche
Intermittent claudication — it improves walking distance in peripheral arterial disease.
Cilostazol boxed warning
Contraindicated in heart failure — phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitors increased mortality in heart failure trials.
Dipyridamole niche
Rarely used alone; it also serves as a pharmacologic stress agent in cardiac imaging.
Phosphodiesterase inhibitor with COX inhibitor
Dipyridamole extended-release 200 mg / aspirin 25 mg (Aggrenox)
Why combine them
Two different doors closed at once — thromboxane and cyclic AMP.
Use
Secondary prevention of ischemic stroke, twice daily.
The complaint that stops it
Headache, from the dipyridamole vasodilation — the main reason patients abandon it.
Reason it out — arterial versus venous decides the drug
Arterial clots are platelet-rich ("white clots") — high shear, ruptured plaque. They are treated with antiplatelets: heart attack, ischemic stroke, peripheral arterial disease, stents. Venous clots are fibrin-and-red-cell-rich ("red clots") — stasis. They are treated with anticoagulants: deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and the clot in atrial fibrillation.

This single distinction answers a surprising number of "which drug" questions, and explains why aspirin is not the answer for a deep vein thrombosis.

Memory Hook — "White is platelets, Red is fibrin"
White clot = Wall of the artery = platelets = antiplatelet.
Red clot = Resting vein = fibrin = anticoagulant.
Fast blood makes white clots; slow blood makes red ones.

Fibrinolytic (Thrombolytic) Drugs

Every other drug in this block prevents a clot. These three destroy one that already exists — by converting plasminogen into plasmin, the enzyme that chews fibrin apart. That is why they save lives in a stroke and end them in a bleed.

My confidence:

Abbreviations in this section — tPA tissue plasminogen activator; IV intravenous; ICH intracranial hemorrhage; TXA tranexamic acid.

Class blueprint

All three do the same thing

They convert plasminogen → plasmin; plasmin degrades the fibrin mesh holding the clot together. Fibrin degradation products (measured as the D-dimer) are the debris. They are relatively fibrin-selective — they work best on plasminogen already bound to a clot — but "relatively" is doing a lot of work, which is why systemic bleeding is the defining risk.

The bleeding you cannot take back
The feared complication is intracranial hemorrhage. Contraindications follow directly from "you are about to dissolve every clot in the body": any prior intracranial hemorrhage, ischemic stroke within 3 months, known intracranial neoplasm or vascular malformation, suspected aortic dissection, active internal bleeding or bleeding diathesis, significant closed head trauma within 3 months, and severe uncontrolled hypertension.

If catastrophic bleeding occurs: stop the drug, give cryoprecipitate (to replace fibrinogen) and an antifibrinolytic — tranexamic acid or aminocaproic acid, which block plasminogen from becoming plasmin. That is the mirror image of these drugs, and the reason the machine diagram shows both.

Memory Hook — "-plase dissolves, TXA restores"
Everything ending in -plase is a plasminogen activator — it turns on the demolition crew. Tranexamic acid and aminocaproic acid are the crew's stop-work order. And remember the newest fact: tenecteplase — one bolus, now approved for stroke.

Boxed warning
Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents — what hemoglobin number defines the harm?
Above 11 g/dL. Targeting a hemoglobin over 11 increased myocardial infarction, stroke, venous thromboembolism, vascular access thrombosis, and death — with no additional benefit. Also: shortened survival / tumor progression in cancer; increased death and stroke in chronic kidney disease; deep vein thrombosis perisurgery. Use the lowest dose that avoids transfusion.
Boxed warning ×2
Hydroxyurea carries two boxed warnings. Both?
1. Severe myelosuppression — monitor blood counts at baseline and throughout; interrupt and dose-reduce as needed. 2. Carcinogenic / secondary malignancy — advise sun protection and monitor for malignancies. Remember it is a chemotherapy agent moonlighting as a sickle cell drug.
Emergency
Platelets fall 60% on day 7 of heparin and the patient's leg goes cold. Diagnosis and first move?
Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — antibodies to the heparin–platelet factor 4 complex activate platelets, so the danger is thrombosis, not bleeding. Stop all heparin (including flushes and coated lines) and start a non-heparin anticoagulant: argatroban, bivalirudin, or fondaparinux. Do not start warfarin alone — venous limb gangrene.
Electrolyte trap
Which IV iron causes severe hypophosphatemia, and by what mechanism?
Ferric carboxymaltose (Injectafer). It raises FGF23, which increases the urinary fractional excretion of phosphate → phosphate is dumped in the urine. May be SEVERE. Check phosphate with repeat courses; ferric derisomaltose causes notably less.
Counterintuitive
Which premedication is specifically warned against before IV iron — and what do you use instead?
Do NOT give diphenhydramine. It causes hypotension, somnolence, flushing, dizziness, irritability, nasal congestion, wheezing, and supraventricular tachycardia — which then gets misread as an iron reaction. Use methylprednisolone 125 mg IV in patients with asthma or more than one drug allergy.
Paradox
Why can warfarin cause skin necrosis in the first days, and how do you prevent it?
Protein C has a shorter half-life than factors II, IX, and X — so the natural anticoagulant disappears first, creating a transient hypercoagulable window. Worst in protein C deficiency. Prevent by bridging with a heparin until the INR has been therapeutic for at least 24 hours (typically ≥5 days of overlap).
Reversal
Name the reversal agent for: heparin · warfarin · dabigatran · a factor Xa inhibitor · fondaparinux.
Heparin → protamine sulfate (partial for low-molecular-weight heparin). Warfarin → vitamin K + 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate. Dabigatran → idarucizumab (Praxbind). Factor Xa inhibitors → andexanet alfa was the agent, but it was withdrawn in 2026, so 4F-PCC is the practical option. Fondaparinux → nothing.
Class-wide
Two boxed warnings shared by every direct oral anticoagulant?
1. Premature discontinuation increases the risk of thrombotic events — never just stop one. 2. Spinal/epidural hematoma with neuraxial anesthesia or spinal puncture, which can cause permanent paralysis.
Vaccine required
Before starting any complement inhibitor for PNH, what must happen?
Vaccinate against Neisseria meningitidis. Blocking terminal complement removes the exact defense against encapsulated organisms — boxed warning for serious, life-threatening meningococcal infection. Applies to eculizumab, ravulizumab, pegcetacoplan, and iptacopan alike.
Contraindication
Which antiplatelet is absolutely contraindicated after a stroke or TIA — and which one is contraindicated in heart failure?
Vorapaxar (PAR-1 antagonist) — absolutely contraindicated with prior stroke, TIA, or intracranial hemorrhage. Prasugrel is also contraindicated after stroke/TIA. Cilostazol carries the heart failure contraindication — phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitors increased mortality in heart failure.
Silent damage
Why is treating a B12 deficiency with folate alone dangerous?
Folate corrects the anemia so the blood count looks treated, while the neurological damage progresses — subacute combined degeneration of the dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts, eventually irreversible. Check B12 before treating a macrocytic anemia, or replace both.
Chelator
Which iron chelator requires weekly white cell counts, and why?
Deferiprone (Ferriprox) — boxed warning for agranulocytosis / neutropenia. Meanwhile deferasirox carries renal failure, hepatic failure, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage; deferoxamine demands 8–12 hour subcutaneous infusions and can cause ocular and auditory toxicity.
