How to Break Vascular Disease Down

Dr. Ciraulo (trauma and vascular surgeon) built the lecture on the NMS Surgery review book. Two ways to sort anything vascular: by anatomy (aortoiliac → femoropopliteal → below the knee) and by cause (atherosclerosis, dissection, aneurysm, embolism). The smaller and further down the vessel, the harder the fix and the worse the patency.

His "areas of focus" (end of lecture)

  • The P's of acute ischemia — "worth committing to memory. You gotta see those on board exams."
  • Claudication vs limb-threatening ischemia — crossing that line (rest pain at night, a cold painful leg) is the trigger to see a vascular surgeon.
  • Named syndromes — "every time you have a name on something, you can almost count it being a test question": Leriche, blue toe, Goldblatt, phlegmasia alba vs cerulea dolens.
  • CT diagnoses; angiograms are for procedures.
  • Aortic dissection: tearing back pain, DeBakey/Stanford types and who needs surgery.
  • The AAA surveillance chart — "you're gonna have something on this on the test."
  • Post-op aortic repair + bloody stool = ischemic colitis (the IMA question).
  • Virchow triad and DVT management.
🗺️

By anatomy

Suprainguinal: aortoiliac disease (aorta + iliacs up to the inguinal ligament). Infrainguinal: femoropopliteal and infrageniculate (tibial) disease.

🔎

By cause

Atherosclerosis (stenosis, embolization) · dissection · aneurysm · embolism / thrombosis · vasospasm and vasculitis · venous thrombosis.

Topic 1
⭐ Memorise the P's

Slides 4–10 · plaque → stenosis or embolus · vascular vs neurogenic claudication · critical limb ischemia · collaterals

Atherosclerosis, Claudication & Limb Ischemia

AbbreviationsDM = diabetes mellitus · HTN = hypertension · CLI = critical limb ischemia · CAD = coronary artery disease · SFA = superficial femoral artery · BUN = blood urea nitrogen

Pathophysiology (slide 4)

  • Atherosclerosis is the most common cause of arterial occlusive disease. Fatty streak → complex plaque → intimal ulceration or intraplaque hemorrhage.
  • Two ways it hurts you: stenosis (low flow downstream, or occlusion) and embolization — "one of the biggest problems is… not only blockage… but also embolization."
  • Risk factors: DM, tobacco, HTN, hyperlipidemia.

Three presentations

Intermittent claudication

Vascular · not limb-threatening

Muscle pain (usually calf) brought on by walking, reproducible, relieved by rest. < 5% progress to ischemia; major amputation risk ~1%/year. "The risk of losing the limb for just claudication is really nothing."

Neurogenic claudication

Spine, not vessels

History of back pain; pain radiates down the leg with paresthesia; can come on just standing. Board add-on Relieved by sitting or bending forward (lumbar flexion); pulses normal.

Critical limb ischemia

Limb-threatening

Ischemic rest pain and tissue loss (ulcer, gangrene). 30% progress to major amputation within a year; 25% die within a year, mostly from cardiovascular complications.

The P's of acute arterial insufficiency (slide 8)

🔑 "This cartoon is probably the most important thing you're gonna have"

Pain · Pallor · Paresthesia · Paralysis · Pulselessness · Poikilothermia (the limb takes room temperature: "perishingly cold"). Board exams and the vascular service next year will ask for them.

🧠 Order of loss Board add-on

Sensory loss (paresthesia) comes before motor loss (paralysis). Once the patient can't move the foot, the limb is immediately threatened, which is why intact motor and sensory function decides whether there is time for thrombolysis (Topic 5).

Evaluating a vascular patient (slide 9)

  • Detailed history and examination. They are high risk for CAD: assess cardiopulmonary status.
  • Duplex the carotids (cerebrovascular atherosclerosis travels with PAD).
  • Renal function (BUN, creatinine) before any contrast study (DSA or CTA).
  • Address the risk factors: DM, HTN, hyperlipidemia, smoking.

Collaterals: why slow is survivable and sudden is not (slide 10)

  • Collaterals grow around a slowly narrowing stenosis: "the water runs over the banks of the stream… and finds its way to reconstitute the stream below."
  • SFA occlusion → profunda femoris collaterals reconstitute the popliteal artery; geniculate collaterals around the knee.
  • Aortoiliac disease → collaterals from the iliac, lumbar and internal mammary arteries.
  • An acute thrombus or embolus gives no time for collaterals, so the same occlusion is far worse in an acute event (and in a young person with no collaterals) than in chronic disease.

Q: A 66-year-old smoker gets calf cramping after two blocks that goes away after 3 minutes standing still. A 70-year-old with chronic back pain gets burning, tingling leg pain when standing at the sink, relieved by sitting and leaning forward. Label each.

Answer

The first is vascular (intermittent) claudication: exertional, reproducible, relieved by rest. The second is neurogenic claudication from lumbar stenosis: back pain, radiating pain with paresthesia, brought on by standing.

Q: List the P's of acute arterial insufficiency.

Answer

Pain, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, pulselessness, poikilothermia.

Q: Why does an embolus to a normal femoral artery cause a more dangerous ischemic leg than a chronic SFA occlusion?

Answer

There is no time for collateral circulation to develop. In chronic disease the profunda and geniculate collaterals reconstitute flow below the block.

⭐ "Don't be tricked": CT for diagnosis, angiogram for procedures

Work-Up: ABI, CT Angiography vs Angiogram

AbbreviationsABI = ankle-brachial index · CTA = CT angiography · MRA = magnetic resonance angiography · DSA = digital subtraction angiography · PVR = pulse-volume recording · NSF = nephrogenic systemic fibrosis

Ankle-brachial index (slide 13)

Systolic BP in both arms and both ankles→ABI = ankle systolic ÷ the higher of the two brachial systolics→Correlates with functional status
  • Lecture threshold: below about 0.8–1, especially if one side differs from the other, is abnormal.
  • Board add-on Normal 1.0–1.4 · PAD ≤ 0.9 · severe / CLI-range < 0.4 · > 1.4 = non-compressible calcified arteries (diabetes, CKD) → falsely high; use toe pressures.
  • Aortoiliac claudication can have a near-normal resting ABI; claudicants with a normal resting ABI get exercise treadmill testing (slides 12, 23).

Noninvasive lab studies (slide 14)

  • Segmental pressures and waveform analysis at each level, plus duplex of the leg arteries.
  • A drop of 20 mm Hg between adjacent segments is diagnostic of a functional occlusion there.

⚠️ The trick question

"The way they trick you on exams is to say the hallmark for making this diagnosis is an aortogram. That's not true." An angiogram shows only where the dye flows. A 2 cm channel of contrast could sit inside a 6 cm aneurysm lined with thrombus. CT shows the outside diameter of the pipe and the thrombus. The angiogram gives the most accurate intraluminal measurement, which is why it is used on the table when sizing a stent graft.

Q: Arm pressures 136 and 140 mm Hg; left ankle 54 mm Hg. Calculate the left ABI and interpret it.

Answer

54 ÷ 140 (the higher arm) = 0.39 → severe disease in the critical-ischemia range.

Q: A patient with a suspected aortic aneurysm has an aortogram showing a 2.2 cm lumen. Why can't you conclude the aorta is normal size?

Answer

The angiogram shows only the lumen. Mural thrombus can fill most of a large aneurysm. CT measures the outer wall and shows the thrombus, so CT is the diagnostic test.

Q: A diabetic on dialysis has an ABI of 1.6 with absent pedal pulses. What does the number mean?

Answer

Board add-on Non-compressible, calcified tibial arteries give a falsely high ABI. Use toe-brachial index or imaging.

Topic 3
⭐ Butt claudication + impotence + weak femorals = Leriche

Slides 11–12, 16–19 · infrarenal aorta · Leriche syndrome · blue toe · medical therapy · stents · bypass

Aortoiliac Disease, Leriche & Blue Toe

AbbreviationsAIOD = aortoiliac occlusive disease · PDE3 = phosphodiesterase-3 · HMG-CoA = 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (statin target) · CHF = congestive heart failure · PTFE = polytetrafluoroethylene · ED = erectile dysfunction

Who and where (slide 11)

  • Atherosclerosis begins in the infrarenal aorta.
  • Young women who smoke with hypercholesterolemia: tend to have the worst outcomes (focal aortoiliac disease). Older men with DM and HTN: diffuse multisegment disease.
  • Hallmark: diminished or absent femoral pulses — "that's a pretty big obstruction to block off your femoral pulses."

Presentations (slide 12)

Leriche syndrome

Men · named → testable

Triad: buttock (and thigh) claudication, impotence, diminished femoral pulses. The man asking for Viagra whose "butt hurts on the ladder": "Don't just write a prescription for Viagra." Find the aortoiliac occlusion.

Blue toe syndrome

Microemboli

Small cholesterol emboli from upstream plaque; more common with aneurysmal disease. Can be the presenting sign of AIOD. "It's not a blue toe because of a problem with the knee… there's probably a problem upstream."

Buttock/thigh claudication

Proximal level

Suspect AIOD even with a near-normal ankle-brachial index at rest.

Medical therapy first (slide 16)

  • Smoking cessation — the biggest lever; most patients "don't change behavior."
  • Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin). Lecture: "aspirin's good, but in this day and age aspirin's probably not enough." Board add-on Clopidogrel is the alternative; low-dose rivaroxaban plus aspirin is used in symptomatic PAD.
  • Cilostazol: PDE3 inhibitor → smooth-muscle vasodilation + ↓ platelet aggregation. The only drug shown to improve claudication. Contraindicated in heart failure.
  • Statins for everyone, even with acceptable lipids (extra cardiovascular protection).
  • Graded exercise programme: very effective; promotes collaterals (angiogenesis) and relieves symptoms.
⚠️ Recording slip

Answering a student, the recording says "the first line of treatment is going to be anticoagulation" for early claudication. The slides say antiplatelet therapy (aspirin) for claudication; IV heparin anticoagulation is the first step in acute/critical limb ischemia (slide 31). Use the slides.

Intervention (slides 17–19)

Indications: lifestyle-limiting claudication, ischemic rest pain, or tissue loss.

🧠 Why long grafts fail

"The longer a pathway is, the more likely… turbulence and thrombosis. So you want short grafts." That's why the long extra-anatomic axillobifemoral graft has the lowest patency and is reserved for patients who can't tolerate an aortic operation (or have an infected aortic field).

Q: A 58-year-old man has pain in both buttocks climbing a ladder, new erectile dysfunction, and weak femoral pulses. Name the syndrome and the level of disease.

Answer

Leriche syndrome: aortoiliac occlusive disease at the aortic bifurcation.

Q: A claudicant with an ejection fraction of 25% asks for "the walking pill." Why not cilostazol?

Answer

Cilostazol (a PDE3 inhibitor) is contraindicated in heart failure. Use exercise therapy, smoking cessation, antiplatelet therapy and a statin.

Q: A patient with a painful purple toe has a normal popliteal and pedal pulse. Where do you look?

Answer

Upstream: blue toe syndrome is cholesterol microembolization from proximal plaque or an aneurysm (aortoiliac).

Topic 4
⭐ Rest pain at night, relieved by dangling the leg

Slides 20–26 · SFA disease · critical limb ischemia · endarterectomy vs bypass · tibial disease in diabetes and dialysis patients

Femoropopliteal & Tibial Disease

AbbreviationsSFA = superficial femoral artery · CFA = common femoral artery · CLI = critical limb ischemia · ABI = ankle-brachial index · PVR = pulse-volume recording · GSV = great saphenous vein · DSA = digital subtraction angiography · ESRD = end-stage renal disease · DM = diabetes mellitus

Femoropopliteal disease

  • Most often in the SFA; "probably one of the more frequent ones… also one of the easier ones to handle." Severity ranges from flow-limiting stenosis to complete occlusion of the SFA and popliteal.
  • Causes: atherosclerosis (most important); also popliteal entrapment, dissection, embolization, aneurysmal degeneration.
  • Most are asymptomatic: the profunda femoris feeds the thigh muscles and, through collaterals, can sustain near-normal distal flow.
  • Claudication: crampy calf pain with walking. Rest pain, ulcers and gangrene signal imminent limb loss.
  • Exam: palpable femoral pulses but diminished popliteal and pedal pulses (contrast aortoiliac disease, where the femorals are weak).
🩺 Rest pain — the line you don't want to cross

"I go to bed, I lay down, and things are not getting better… I'm having nocturnal pain." Typical rest pain is in the forefoot at night and is relieved by dangling the leg off the bed (gravity helps perfusion). With rubor, swelling and redness, that's limb-threatening ischemia: refer to vascular surgery.

Tests (slide 23)

  • ABI and segmental pressures (a drop > 20 mm Hg localises the lesion); Doppler waveforms and PVR.
  • Exercise treadmill testing for claudication with a normal resting ABI.
  • CTA of abdomen and pelvis with runoff: near-equal information to DSA with 3D reconstruction. DSA is used during the procedure.

Treatment (slide 24)

  • Best medical therapy: smoking cessation, lipid control, cilostazol. "Stop smoking, stop eating French fries… go on antiplatelet therapy."
  • Angioplasty and stenting: ~80% patency over 3 years; better above the knee. Stents can be re-stented ("a stent in a stent"), but eventually you run out and move to bypass.
  • Endarterectomy of the common femoral and profunda with patch angioplasty for focal lesions. The profunda matters for thigh and buttock claudication, and it's hard to reach (it dives behind the hamstrings).
  • Bypass: femoropopliteal above- or below-knee with great saphenous vein → 70–90% 5-year primary patency. Autogenous vein is the preferred conduit.

Infrageniculate (tibial) disease (slides 25–26)

  • Usually multisegment, in high-risk patients; typical of DM and ESRD. They present with foot ulcers or gangrene.
  • Vessels are 2–3 mm: "an area that has poorer outcomes." Smaller stents have shorter longevity.
  • For rest pain or tissue loss, open bypass is the procedure of choice; endovascular treatment can achieve wound healing. The goal is in-line flow to the foot, targeting any tibial (anterior tibial, posterior tibial, peroneal) or pedal artery; DSA identifies inflow and outflow.
  • Runoff: the posterior tibial tends to be the most robust (runs behind the medial malleolus into the arch). "If we have at least two runoffs, we're pretty happy. One runoff, you kinda get worried."
  • "It's not so much about life or death like aortoiliac disease, it's more about limb loss and tissue salvage."

Q: A smoker has calf claudication, strong femoral pulses and absent popliteal and pedal pulses. Where is the lesion?

Answer

Superficial femoral artery (femoropopliteal disease). In aortoiliac disease the femoral pulses themselves are weak.

Q: A diabetic on dialysis has a non-healing toe ulcer and tibial occlusions with a patent pedal artery. Which treatment does the lecture call the procedure of choice?

Answer

Open bypass to a tibial or pedal target to restore in-line flow to the foot (with autogenous vein as the preferred conduit).

Topic 5
⭐ Heparin first; then open the artery; then watch the compartments

Slides 27–34 · embolism vs thrombosis · heparin · thrombolysis vs Fogarty · reperfusion and compartment syndrome · amputation levels

Acute Limb Ischemia, Compartment Syndrome & Amputation

AbbreviationsAF = atrial fibrillation · MI = myocardial infarction · IV = intravenous · tPA = tissue plasminogen activator · CTA = CT angiography · BKA / AKA = below- / above-knee amputation · ΔP = diastolic pressure minus compartment pressure

Causes (slides 27–28)

🎯

Embolism

Debris (thrombus, lipid, tumor) breaks off upstream. Most are cardiac: left atrial mural thrombus in AF ("the biggest thing we have"), ventricular thrombus after MI, rheumatic valve disease, prosthetic valve vegetations, atrial myxoma. Non-cardiac: atherosclerotic or aneurysmal sources (popliteal aneurysm), thoracic outlet syndrome, iatrogenic atheroemboli after catheter procedures.

🧱

Thrombosis

In situ on an atherosclerotic plaque; hypercoagulable states (even without atherosclerosis); extension of an aortic or arterial dissection; acute occlusion of a previously patent bypass graft. A thrombus either breaks off or grows, so it always needs a plan, even if that plan is monitoring.

  • Symptoms depend on the size of the artery and the collaterals.
  • Macroemboli lodge at bifurcations. Board add-on The common femoral bifurcation is the commonest site.
  • Microemboli go to the tibial or digital vessels → blue toe syndrome.
  • Evaluation: prompt exam, CTA with runoff, duplex, on-table angiography to direct therapy.
🦴 Aside: fat embolism

A non-vascular embolus after long-bone fractures. Lecture: respiratory insufficiency with a broken femur → look for petechiae (the recording says "splinters" in the fingernails). "The good news is you made the diagnosis, the bad news is you can't fix it": treatment is supportive. Board add-on Classic triad 24–72 h after the fracture: hypoxemia, confusion, petechial rash (axillae, conjunctivae, chest).

Treatment (slide 31)

  1. Anticoagulate: IV heparin is the drug of choice"Always the first step in critical limb ischemia." It stops propagation while you plan.
  2. Percutaneous thrombectomy / catheter-directed thrombolysisPreferred when motor and sensory function are intact (there's time). Useful in the frail 90-year-old who can't have general anesthesia.
  3. Open thrombectomy / embolectomyA Fogarty balloon catheter is passed through the embolus, the balloon inflated, and the clot pulled out. Combined open + endovascular (angioplasty, stent) is common.
  4. Then find the source"Where did it come from and how do we prevent it from happening again?" (echo, rhythm monitoring, long-term anticoagulation for AF).
☐ Absolute contraindications to thrombolysis (slide 31)

Stroke or brain surgery within 2 months · major surgery within 2 weeks · significant bleeding risk. The lecture's example: give tPA to a patient with peptic ulcer disease and "now we've got a massive GI bleed."

Reperfusion and compartment syndrome (slide 32)

  • Timing: past about 6–8 hours without flow (lecture) "it's probably going to come off." Board add-on Irreversible muscle and nerve necrosis after ~4–6 h of compartment ischemia.
  • Mechanism: reperfusion injury: "like putting peroxide right into the bloodstream." Oxygen free radicals damage capillary membranes → muscle swells inside its fascia → pressure exceeds perfusion → ischemia again.
  • Signs: pain on passive stretch, tense calf tenderness, numbness in the first web space (deep peroneal nerve, anterior compartment).
  • Treatment: fasciotomy, limb-saving, decided on clinical findings. A Stryker device measures compartment pressure. Board add-on Pressure ≥ 30 mm Hg or ΔP (diastolic − compartment) ≤ 30 mm Hg.
  • Check the patient every couple of hours after revascularisation. Gray, dead muscle at fasciotomy = "you waited too long."

Amputation (slides 33–34)

  • Indications: non-ambulatory bed-bound patients, non-reconstructable disease, after weighing risks and benefits. "Life over limb."
  • Goals: remove all infected and necrotic tissue at the level that maximises rehabilitation; preoperative functional status matters.
  • Levels: toe · transmetatarsal · below-knee (~10 cm below the tibial tuberosity, long posterior gastrocnemius flap, bevelled tibia) · above-knee.
LevelExtra energy to walk (slides)Recording
Below-knee10–40%"About 35–45%"

A frail patient with CAD or COPD often can't make up that energy cost, which is why the conversation before amputation matters: "they're expecting, 'Oh, I got my leg taken off, I'm good to go.' Well, that's not necessarily the case."

Q: A 74-year-old with atrial fibrillation has a sudden cold, pale, pulseless right leg; sensation is reduced but she can wiggle her toes. First drug, and is thrombolysis an option?

Answer

IV heparin immediately. With motor function intact, catheter-directed thrombolysis or percutaneous thrombectomy is preferred if there's no contraindication; otherwise Fogarty embolectomy. Then treat the source (anticoagulation for AF).

Q: Six hours after femoral embolectomy the calf is tense, passive dorsiflexion of the toes is excruciating, and the first web space is numb. Diagnosis and treatment?

Answer

Compartment syndrome from reperfusion injury; emergency fasciotomy.

Q: Which patient should not receive tPA for acute limb ischemia: one with a stroke 6 weeks ago, or one with a knee arthroscopy 5 weeks ago?

Answer

The stroke 6 weeks ago (within 2 months is an absolute contraindication). Major surgery is a contraindication only within 2 weeks.

Slides 35–40 · bifurcation disease · amaurosis fugax and Hollenhorst plaques · TIA vs RIND vs stroke · duplex · endarterectomy vs stent

Extracranial Cerebrovascular (Carotid) Disease

AbbreviationsTIA = transient ischemic attack · RIND = reversible ischemic neurologic deficit · ICA = internal carotid artery · CEA = carotid endarterectomy · CN = cranial nerve · HTN / DM = hypertension / diabetes mellitus
  • Stroke: the 3rd leading cause of death in the United States (slide). Disease sits at the carotid bifurcation.
  • Asymptomatic: a carotid bruit.
  • Symptomatic: amaurosis fugax (transient monocular blindness, "a shade coming down") from emboli to the retinal artery; Hollenhorst plaques (cholesterol emboli) on fundoscopy.
  • Falls and new clumsiness: "Don't blow off someone… who feels a little clumsy." Every fall victim on his service gets a work-up for an embolic or cardiac cause (echo for thrombus and EF, 24-h rhythm monitoring).

Definitions (slide 37)

TermLecture definition
TIASudden neurological event lasting < 24 h
RINDDeficit lasting > 24 h but < 1 week
StrokeInfarction of brain tissue (permanent)
Vertebrobasilar insufficiencyVisual changes, ataxia, gait disturbance, vertigo (posterior circulation)

Board add-on Current definitions are tissue-based: a TIA has no infarct on imaging regardless of duration (most last < 1 h). RIND is an older term.

Work-up (slide 38)

  • CT first; MRI is more sensitive for early ischemia.
  • Carotid duplex: the primary screening test for grading stenosis.
  • Cerebral angiography defines the lesion and is used during treatment.
  • Echocardiography for a cardiac embolic source.
  • Acute stroke: endovascular neurosurgeons can retrieve clot, but the window is about 6 h in the lecture: "anything past that, the risk of… significant resolution… goes way down."

Management (slides 39–40)

  • Everyone: aspirin, statin, control HTN and DM, smoking cessation.
  • Surgery depends on degree of stenosis and symptoms:
StenosisBenefit of surgery
Asymptomatic60–99%5.9% absolute stroke-risk reduction at 5 years
Symptomatic> 70%2-year stroke risk 26% → 9%

Take-home: "If you have a 70% stenosis, you probably are going to be getting teed up for an intervention." Contraindications: disabling stroke, an occluded internal carotid, short life expectancy.

🔪

Carotid endarterectomy

Open the carotid, "feather out" the plaque from the internal, external and common carotid, patch closure. A shunt maintains brain perfusion during the clamp. Complications: TIA, bleeding, cranial nerve injury — hypoglossal (XII) and vagus (X).

🧵

Carotid angioplasty and stent

For a lesion inaccessible at the skull base or a patient too sick for surgery. Done awake with an embolic-protection/aspiration device. "Probably eventually will be the way we're gonna go, but right now it's not quite there yet."

Q: A 68-year-old has two episodes of painless monocular vision loss "like a curtain" lasting minutes. Fundoscopy shows a bright yellow plaque in a retinal arteriole. Name the finding and the first imaging test.

Answer

Amaurosis fugax with a Hollenhorst plaque (cholesterol embolus); carotid duplex ultrasound.

Q: After a left carotid endarterectomy the patient's tongue deviates to the left. Which nerve?

Answer

Left hypoglossal nerve (CN XII); the tongue deviates toward the injured side. Hoarseness would point to the vagus/recurrent laryngeal nerve.

Q: A patient had a TIA with a 30% stenosis on that side and a 100% occlusion on the other. Is endarterectomy indicated?

Answer

No. The symptomatic side is < 70%, and an occluded internal carotid is a contraindication to surgery. Medical therapy.

⭐ Tearing back pain → CT, not chiropractic

Slides 41–46 · intimal tear and false lumen · DeBakey and Stanford · risks · CTA / TEE · open repair vs blood-pressure control vs TEVAR

Aortic Dissection

AbbreviationsCTA = CT angiography · TEE = transesophageal echocardiogram · TEVAR = thoracic endovascular aortic repair · HTN = hypertension · BP = blood pressure · CSF = cerebrospinal fluid
  • A tear in the intima lets blood run between the layers, creating a false lumen behind an intimal flap. "I think more lethal than atherosclerotic disease."
  • Causes: hypertension lifting the intima behind a plaque; blunt thoracic trauma.
  • Rupture is rare; the usual harm is ischemia downstream (branches cut off by the flap, including the renals).
  • Timing: acute < 2 weeks (complicated = rupture, impending rupture, end-organ failure; uncomplicated = none) · chronic > 2 weeks.

Classification (slides 43–44)

He prefers DeBakey ("that's how I was taught"), but "it doesn't really matter as long as you know what the words mean and what you have to do." Michael DeBakey pioneered cardiovascular surgery and operated into his 90s.

Who and how it presents (slide 45)

  • Risks: HTN, aortic wall abnormalities, bicuspid aortic valve, Marfan syndrome, cocaine (the most dramatic hypertensive crisis).
  • "Worst ever" pain, classically tearing, in the back of the chest: "someone's trying to rip my back apart." Cardiac pain is "an elephant sitting on my chest."
  • End-organ hypoperfusion from branch occlusion.
⚠️ The ED case he closed with

A hypertensive man: "the worst pain in my back… I can't seem to get comfortable." "It's not time to break out the manipulation box. That's the time to get a CAT scan." Possible dissection → CTA.

Diagnosis and management (slide 46)

  • CTA of chest and abdomen; TEE (board: when unstable).
  • Type A → emergent open repair.
  • Uncomplicated type B → medical BP control; "if you control the hypertension, sometimes these dissections will heal." Failure or complications → TEVAR (preferred).
  • Board add-on Start an IV β-blocker first (esmolol, labetalol) to a heart rate ≤ 60 and systolic BP 100–120 before any vasodilator (nitroprusside), to avoid reflex tachycardia that raises aortic shear stress. Classic x-ray sign: widened mediastinum.
🧠 The complication of clamping the descending aorta: paraplegia

"What gets supplied off the aorta?" The artery of Adamkiewicz (spinal cord). The longer the clamp, the higher the risk of spinal cord ischemia. Modern tricks: bypass the aorta during the repair, and place a lumbar drain to remove CSF — the ischemic cord swells, and draining CSF protects its perfusion. The recording places the artery around T4–T8; Board add-on it most often arises at T9–T12.

Q: CTA shows a dissection starting just beyond the left subclavian artery and extending to the iliacs; the patient is stable without malperfusion. Classify and manage.

Answer

DeBakey III / Stanford B, uncomplicated → medical BP control (IV β-blocker first). TEVAR if it becomes complicated or fails medical therapy.

Q: A dissection involves only the ascending aorta. DeBakey type, Stanford type, and management?

Answer

DeBakey II = Stanford A → emergent open repair.

Q: After descending thoracic aortic repair the patient can't move his legs. Mechanism, and what is done to prevent it?

Answer

Spinal cord ischemia (loss of flow through the artery of Adamkiewicz during cross-clamping). Prevention: shorten clamp time or bypass the aorta, and lumbar CSF drainage.

⭐ "Memorize that chart"

Slides 47–56 · fusiform vs saccular · AAA risk, rupture, surveillance and repair · IMA and ischemic colitis · graft infection · splenic and popliteal aneurysms

Aneurysms: AAA, Splenic, Popliteal

AbbreviationsAAA = abdominal aortic aneurysm · US = ultrasound · CT = computed tomography · EVAR = endovascular aneurysm repair · IMA / SMA = inferior / superior mesenteric artery · COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease · HTN = hypertension

Basics (slide 48)

  • Dilatation of an artery anywhere in the body, named by location (e.g. infrarenal aortic).
  • Fusiform = the whole circumference · saccular = an outpouching of one wall.
  • Causes: inflammatory, infectious, congenital, degenerative, traumatic.

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (slides 49–51)

Definition

Most common type

Infrarenal, degenerative, fusiform, > 3 cm. Grows 0.2–0.3 cm/year.

Risk of having one

Same as atherosclerosis

Advanced age, male, smoking, family history, HTN.

Risk of rupture

Note: female

HTN, COPD, female sex, smoking, steroid use. > 5.5 cm: 3–15% rupture risk (slide).

Complications

Four

Rupture · embolization · dissection · fistulization (aortoduodenal / aortoenteric).

  • Mostly asymptomatic. Symptoms: abdominal or back pain, early satiety, hydronephrosis, venous thrombosis; renal insufficiency if the renals are involved. Rupture: severe back pain (and shock).
  • Screening (slide): family history; men 65–70. Board add-on USPSTF: one-time US in men 65–75 who have ever smoked.
  • Imaging: x-ray may show an eggshell calcified rim · ultrasound = screening · CT with contrast = gold standard. Not angiography: thrombus hides the real diameter.

The surveillance chart — "you're gonna have something on this on the test"

🔑 Indications for surgery (slides 50–51)

Symptomatic (any size) · > 5.5 cm · growth > 5 mm in 6 months · eccentric or saccular shape. A non-compliant patient who won't change behaviour may be operated on sooner. "That's the kind of graph you want to clip out and put in the patient's chart" if you follow them in primary care.

Diameter, then tick anything that applies. Follows the lecture chart.

Repair (slide 52)

  • Endovascular (EVAR) needs suitable anatomy: aortic neck > 15 mm long, angle < 60°, access arteries big enough for the delivery system, calcification < 50% of the circumference. Fenestrated and branched grafts keep the renals and SMA open.
  • Open: transabdominal or retroperitoneal. Today mostly for patients who can't have a stent graft.

Complications of repair (slides 53–54)

  • Acute renal failure · acute leg ischemia · ischemic colitis · spinal cord ischemia · sexual dysfunction · pelvic ischemia · endograft migration or leak.
  • Aortic graft infection: dull abdominal pain, fever, leukocytosis → CT → antibiotics, graft removal, extra-anatomic bypass (axillobifemoral if infrarenal). "Graft infections basically mean you take the damn graft out."
  • Aortoenteric fistula: a known aneurysm or graft + GI bleeding ("like a hose") → endoscopy → graft removal and extra-anatomic bypass.
⚠️ The classic ICU question: the IMA

The night after aortic repair: unstable, on norepinephrine, then bloody diarrhea ("why is he pooping blood?") in the first 12–24 h → ischemic colitis of the sigmoid; confirm with (flexible) sigmoidoscopy.

The IMA decision at surgery: pulsatile back-bleeding from the cut IMA = good collaterals from the SMA → tie it off. Weak trickle of back-bleeding = the colon depends on it → re-implant the IMA. A thrombosed IMA was never contributing.

Other aneurysms (slides 55–56)

🤰

Splenic artery aneurysm

Risk: female, multiple pregnancies, HTN. Usually asymptomatic; "double rupture" (into the lesser sac, then free into the abdomen). Famous for rupturing in pregnancy (mother codes after delivery with a distended abdomen). Repair if > 2 cm, symptomatic, or in a woman of child-bearing age (treat before pregnancy): stent graft or embolization, or open ligation.

🦵

Popliteal aneurysm

Primarily men; often a pulsatile mass behind the knee on exam. Symptoms come from embolization to the feet or thrombosis, not rupture. Repair if > 2 cm or symptomatic: ligation with bypass or interposition graft, endovascular repair, or thrombolysis followed by repair.

Board add-on The slide says 2.5% are bilateral. Board sources say popliteal aneurysms are bilateral in about half of patients and about a third have an AAA, so image the other knee and the aorta.

Q: An asymptomatic 70-year-old man has a 4.8 cm AAA. Plan?

Answer

Ultrasound surveillance every 3 months (4.5–5.0 cm band), risk-factor control (smoking!).

Q: A 5.2 cm AAA in a 68-year-old woman whose brother had a ruptured AAA. Repair or watch?

Answer

Repair: in the 5.0–5.5 cm band, female sex and a familial aneurysm are on the chart's list of reasons to operate.

Q: Twenty hours after open AAA repair the patient passes bloody stool. Diagnosis, test, and what intraoperative finding should have prompted IMA re-implantation?

Answer

Ischemic colitis (sigmoid) → sigmoidoscopy. Re-implant the IMA when its back-bleeding is weak (poor SMA collaterals); pulsatile back-bleeding means it can be ligated.

Q: Years after an aortic graft, a patient has a small hematemesis followed hours later by massive GI bleeding. Diagnosis?

Answer

Aortoenteric fistula (the small "herald bleed" precedes the exsanguinating one). Endoscopy, then graft removal and extra-anatomic bypass.

⭐ Elderly + abdominal pain + atrial fibrillation = a 911 event

Slides 57–64 · anatomy and collaterals · acute: embolus, thrombosis, NOMI, venous · chronic: intestinal angina

Mesenteric Ischemia

AbbreviationsSMA / IMA = superior / inferior mesenteric artery · SMV = superior mesenteric vein · AF = atrial fibrillation · NOMI = non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia · CTA = CT angiography
  • Supply: celiac, SMA, IMA; collaterals through the pancreaticoduodenal arcade (celiac ↔ SMA) and the marginal artery (SMA ↔ IMA).
  • "Probably one of the most lethal things in the world… because people don't pay attention to it." Granny with poor appetite and belly pain → acute abdomen with dead bowel. Take abdominal pain in elderly patients seriously.

Acute mesenteric ischemia (slides 59–61)

  • Diagnosis: high index of suspicion. X-ray: ileus, pneumatosis · duplex: SMA stenosis · CTA: bowel plus mesenteric vessels (the clot "shows up really nicely, especially on the sagittal view") · mesenteric angiogram with intervention.
  • Board add-on Classic sign: pain out of proportion to the exam; lactate rises late.
  • "When in doubt, operate… better to have a negative laparotomy than to go in and see this." About 100 cm of small bowel is needed to survive (recording).

Chronic mesenteric ischemia (slides 62–64)

  • Slow atherosclerotic stenosis. Blood flow normally rises 30–90 minutes after a meal; a narrowed vessel can't meet that demand.
  • Middle-aged women · postprandial abdominal pain ("intestinal angina") · weight loss (Board add-on from food fear).
  • Diagnosis: mesenteric duplex (~80% sensitivity and specificity) · CTA = diagnostic modality of choice · mesenteric angiogram = gold standard, used with endovascular treatment.
  • Treatment: revascularize to prevent bowel infarction (endovascular, or open bypass with vein or prosthetic graft). "Very treatable."

Q: An 82-year-old with atrial fibrillation (off anticoagulation) has sudden severe abdominal pain, but the abdomen is soft. Most likely diagnosis and test?

Answer

Acute mesenteric ischemia from an SMA embolus; CT angiography of the abdomen, then embolectomy and lifelong anticoagulation.

Q: A 55-year-old woman who smokes has pain 30 minutes after every meal and has lost 9 kg because she avoids eating. Diagnosis?

Answer

Chronic mesenteric ischemia (intestinal angina). CTA, then revascularization.

⭐ "If you do nothing else for kidneys, learn that word: Goldblatt"

Slides 65–69 · renovascular hypertension · atherosclerosis vs fibromuscular dysplasia · functional and anatomic tests · treatment

Renal Artery Stenosis (Goldblatt)

AbbreviationsRAS = renal artery stenosis · FMD = fibromuscular dysplasia · HTN = hypertension · RAAS = renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system · CTA / MRA = CT / MR angiography · ACE = angiotensin-converting enzyme
Renal artery stenosis→kidney senses low flow→↑ renin → ↑ angiotensin II (vasoconstriction)→adrenal ↑ aldosterone → Na⁺ and water reabsorption→↑ BP = Goldblatt (renovascular) hypertension

When to suspect it (slide 66)

  • Chronic HTN refractory to medication.
  • Pulmonary edema out of proportion to left-heart failure (Board add-on "flash" pulmonary edema).
  • HTN in children and young people: "a young person with hypertension, start thinking about the kidney. Right away." His example: a young man after hitting a tree, with a renal artery dissection.

Causes (slide 67)

🧓

Atherosclerosis

The most common cause overall. Board add-on Older men, ostial lesions, with other vascular disease.

👩

Fibromuscular dysplasia

Young women, right > left, 75% cure of HTN with repair. Board add-on "String of beads" on angiography, mid/distal artery; angioplasty without a stent.

💥

Other

Trauma, dissection (most common in the young, per the recording), aneurysm. A renal artery cut off by an aortic dissection is usually lost.

Tests (slide 68)

TypeTestNotes
FunctionalCaptopril scintigraphySensitivity 75%, specificity 90%; the ACE inhibitor drops filtration in the stenotic kidney
Renal vein renin samplingCatheter samples from both renal veins; the lecturer's preferred test ("probably the best way")
AnatomicDuplex US · CTA and MRA · renal angiography—

Treatment (slide 69): percutaneous angioplasty and stent · aortorenal bypass · renal artery thromboendarterectomy · nephrectomy. In practice: "everyone with a Goldblatt kidney I've ever dealt with, I just took the kidney out." Board add-on ACE inhibitors/ARBs control the BP but can precipitate renal failure with bilateral stenosis; watch creatinine.

Q: A 28-year-old woman has new hypertension and an epigastric bruit. Most likely cause?

Answer

Fibromuscular dysplasia of the renal artery (young women, right more than left; repair cures HTN in ~75%).

Q: Explain Goldblatt hypertension in one chain.

Answer

Renal artery stenosis → ↓ perfusion sensed → ↑ renin → angiotensin II (vasoconstriction) and aldosterone (Na⁺/water retention) → hypertension.

Topic 11
⭐ Buerger: "Stop smoking or your hand's coming off"

Slides 70–74 · Raynaud phenomenon · giant cell arteritis · Takayasu disease · thromboangiitis obliterans

Raynaud, Vasculitis & Buerger Disease

AbbreviationsCCB = calcium channel blocker · GCA = giant cell arteritis · TAO = thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease) · DSA = digital subtraction angiography · ESR = erythrocyte sedimentation rate

Raynaud phenomenon

Vasospasm · cold or emotion

Episodic exaggerated vasospasm. Colour sequence: pallor → cyanosis → rubor (white, blue, red). Primary: no underlying disease. Secondary: systemic or autoimmune disease (Board add-on scleroderma, SLE). Frostbite can leave permanent cold sensitivity.

Treat: avoid cold (heated gloves), calcium channel blockers (Board add-on nifedipine); sympathectomy rarely.

Giant cell (temporal) arteritis

Large-vessel vasculitis · older

Fever, fatigue, jaw claudication, headache, blurred vision. Temporal artery biopsy → corticosteroids. Board add-on Age > 50, high ESR, polymyalgia rheumatica; start steroids before the biopsy result if vision is threatened (blindness from ophthalmic artery involvement).

Takayasu disease

Large-vessel vasculitis · young women

Aorta and its branches plus pulmonary vessels; young females. Fever, myalgia, weight loss; pain and aneurysmal change. Diagnosis: DSA (lumpy, irregular "cobblestone" aorta on CTA with 3D reconstruction). Steroids; consider revascularization. Board add-on "Pulseless disease": weak arm pulses and BP difference between arms; often Asian women < 40.

Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger)

Small and medium arteries AND veins

Male smokers; normal proximal pulses with absent distal pulses; claudication (foot/hand) and paresthesia. Angiogram: corkscrew collaterals around the occlusions. Treatment: smoking cessation — "you don't stop smoking, your hand's coming off, pick one." Board add-on Also migratory superficial thrombophlebitis and Raynaud.

Q: A 35-year-old man who smokes two packs a day has a painful ulcer on his fingertip, normal radial pulses, and corkscrew collaterals on hand angiography. Diagnosis and the only treatment that works?

Answer

Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease); complete smoking cessation.

Q: Large-vessel vasculitis in a 72-year-old with jaw claudication vs in a 25-year-old woman with unequal arm pulses: name each and the shared treatment.

Answer

Giant cell arteritis (temporal artery biopsy) vs Takayasu arteritis (angiography); both are treated with corticosteroids.

Topic 12
⭐ Virchow triad "will be something you're tested on"

Slides 75–81 · epidemiology · Virchow triad · signs · duplex vs D-dimer · anticoagulation and duration

Deep Venous Thrombosis: Diagnosis & Treatment

AbbreviationsDVT = deep venous thrombosis · PE = pulmonary embolism · US = ultrasound · MRV = magnetic resonance venography · LMWH = low-molecular-weight heparin · UFH = unfractionated heparin · INR = international normalized ratio · DOAC = direct oral anticoagulant · Xa / IIa = activated factor X / thrombin

Pearls (slide 76)

  • ~1 million DVTs a year in the US. 30% recur within 10 years ("your veins have had damage done to them").
  • Up to 50% are asymptomatic.
  • Untreated proximal DVT → 30–50% risk of PE and 12% mortality. "Pulmonary embolus… that's the one that kills you."
  • Without prophylaxis: < 20% of general surgery patients · 25% elective neurosurgery · 50–60% orthopedic surgery. Long flights: keep moving your legs.

Virchow triad (slide 77)

Stasis / turbulence

Circulatory

Immobility, venous obstruction, varicose veins, atrial fibrillation, LV dysfunction, long travel.

Hypercoagulability

Blood

Major surgery or trauma, malignancy, pregnancy and postpartum, inherited thrombophilia, estrogen therapy, inflammatory disease, sepsis, dehydration.

Endothelial injury

Vessel wall

Trauma, surgery, indwelling catheters, venipuncture, thrombophlebitis, prior DVT.

Signs (slide 78)

Leg pain, pain on passive dorsiflexion (Homans sign), edema, erythema, warmth, prominent superficial veins, peripheral cyanosis. Patients "don't want to bend their ankle."

Tests (slide 79)

TestRole
Duplex ultrasoundThe test. "If you think you've got a problem… let's get a duplex ultrasound."
D-dimerFibrin degradation product; nonspecific. Slide: may be used as a screening test. Recording: "useless… don't even order it."
MR venographyHighest sensitivity and specificity for iliac, pelvic and central vein thrombosis
Contrast venogramHistorical interest
🧠 Reconciling the D-dimer Board add-on

D-dimer is useful only for ruling out: a negative result with low pretest probability (Wells score) excludes DVT. It's positive after surgery, trauma, pregnancy, cancer and in most inpatients, which is why a surgeon finds it useless. If probability is moderate or high, go straight to duplex.

Initial treatment (slides 80–81)

  • Acute DVT → anticoagulation. Goal: prevent PE and propagation.
  • Agents: LMWH and UFH (potentiate antithrombin III → inhibit Xa and thrombin) · fondaparinux (Xa) · warfarin (vitamin K–dependent factors II, VII, IX, X).
  • Overlap heparin with warfarin for 5–10 days; warfarin alone once the INR is 2.0–3.0 for at least 24 h.
  • 3 months + compression: first DVT, proximal DVT, surgery-provoked, or a transient non-surgical risk factor.
  • Longer: unprovoked, recurrent, prior PE, or active cancer (use LMWH).
  • Bleeding is the cost of every anticoagulant (LMWH: postoperative bleeding 6%, major hemorrhage 3%, wound hematoma 4%). Hard on a trauma service with subdurals.
  • Board add-on DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban) are now first-line for most DVTs, including many cancer patients.

Q: Name the Virchow triad and give one example of each.

Answer

Stasis (immobility, long flight) · hypercoagulability (cancer, pregnancy, estrogen) · endothelial injury (surgery, trauma, central line).

Q: A 45-year-old has a first DVT 3 weeks after knee surgery. How long is anticoagulation, and when can heparin stop if warfarin is used?

Answer

3 months (provoked by surgery), with compression. Stop heparin after 5–10 days of overlap once the INR is 2–3 for ≥ 24 h.

Q: A patient with metastatic pancreatic cancer has a DVT. Agent and duration per the slide?

Answer

LMWH, continued longer than 3 months (as long as the cancer is active).

Topic 13
⭐ Alba (white) vs cerulea (blue) dolens

Slides 82–87 · IVC filters · thrombolysis · prevention · heparin-induced thrombocytopenia · phlegmasia · post-thrombotic syndrome

IVC Filters, Prevention, HIT & Complications

AbbreviationsIVC = inferior vena cava · CDT = catheter-directed thrombolysis · tPA = tissue plasminogen activator · IPC = intermittent pneumatic compression · VTE = venous thromboembolism · HIT = heparin-induced thrombocytopenia · PF4 = platelet factor 4 · PTS = post-thrombotic syndrome

IVC filters (slide 82)

  • Indication: DVT when anticoagulation is contraindicated (e.g. a trauma patient with a brain bleed). Place one immediately to prevent PE.
  • "An umbrella with all the material taken off." A big clot hits the filter and fragments; small pieces reach the lungs, but not a fatal one. Older stainless-steel filters were thrombogenic; titanium is used now.
  • Prefer a retrievable filter: placed through the groin (or neck), retrieved through the neck with a snare on its hook once the threat has passed (e.g. ~6 months with a negative duplex).
  • Complications: migration, erosion into the duodenum or aorta.
  • Before lysing a large iliac clot he places a filter first, from above.

Additional therapy (slide 83)

Goal: remove the thrombus to preserve valve function and prevent long-term sequelae. Catheter-directed thrombolysis with tPA, pharmacomechanical thrombolysis (75–90% rapid recanalization), or operative balloon-catheter thrombectomy.

Prevention (slide 84)

  • Leg elevation: ↓ swelling and venous pressure.
  • Graduated compression stockings: ↓ vein cross-section → ↑ flow velocity.
  • Intermittent pneumatic compression: ↑ venous flow and stimulates fibrinolysis ("it's not mechanical, it squeezes the blood upstream").
  • Foot compression devices: high-risk patients when anticoagulants and IPCs are contraindicated.
  • Pharmacologic: LMWH, UFH or fondaparinux — the standard of care in surgical and medical patients.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (slide 85)

Heparin binds PF4→IgG against the heparin–PF4 complex→immune complexes activate platelets via Fc receptors→thrombocytopenia and thrombosis
  • Clinical: thrombocytopenia, thrombosis, or both during or right after heparin.
  • Lab: heparin-induced platelet aggregation, heparin (PF4) antibody test, serotonin release assay.
  • Treatment: stop all heparin (including flushes and LMWH) and start a direct thrombin inhibitor: argatroban, lepirudin or bivalirudin.
  • Board add-on Typically 5–10 days after starting heparin, with a > 50% platelet drop (4Ts score). Don't start warfarin until platelets recover (venous limb gangrene).

Acute complications (slide 86)

🥛

Phlegmasia alba dolens ("milk leg")

Massive DVT; non-ischemic limb with massive pitting edema and blanching (white) from arterial compromise/spasm.

💙

Phlegmasia cerulea dolens

The reversible phase of ischemic venous occlusion: a painful, swollen, blue leg with a predisposition to limb loss.

⚫

Venous gangrene

The irreversible phase of ischemic venous occlusion.

⚠️ Recording slip

The recording says "if you have an arterial occlusion, you will have milk leg" and calls cerulea "venous thrombosis." Both phlegmasias are venous (massive iliofemoral DVT). In alba dolens the leg is white because of edema and secondary arterial spasm/compromise; cerulea dolens is the more extensive, blue, ischemic stage.

Chronic complication: post-thrombotic syndrome (slide 87)

  • Venous hypertension and valve dysfunction: swelling, fatigue or pain, venous claudication, pigmentation (lipodermatosclerosis; the slide says "dermatoliposclerosis"), eczema, erythema, ulceration.
  • Therapy: elevation at rest and night, compression ("probably one of the most important things"), pain control, wound care, and iliac vein angioplasty/stenting (iliac stenosis is present in 50–75% of PTS patients).

Q: A trauma patient with a subdural hematoma develops a femoral DVT. Treatment?

Answer

Anticoagulation is contraindicated → retrievable IVC filter; remove it when anticoagulation becomes possible or the risk has passed.

Q: Day 7 of heparin after hip surgery: platelets fall from 260,000 to 90,000 and a new arterial thrombus appears. Diagnosis and next step?

Answer

HIT (IgG against heparin–PF4). Stop all heparin and start a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban or bivalirudin); confirm with the serotonin release assay.

Q: A patient with an iliofemoral DVT develops a massively swollen, painful, purple-blue leg. Name it and why it matters.

Answer

Phlegmasia cerulea dolens: the reversible ischemic phase of venous occlusion; without urgent clot removal it can progress to venous gangrene and limb loss.

OCCLUSIVE DISEASE

Claudication vs CLI

  • Claudication: reproducible calf pain, rest relieves; < 5% → ischemia, ~1%/yr amputation
  • Neurogenic: back pain, radiating + paresthesia, on standing
  • CLI: rest pain (night, relieved by dangling), tissue loss; 30% amputation and 25% death at 1 yr
  • P's: pain, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, pulselessness, poikilothermia
  • Acute occlusion = no time for collaterals (profunda → popliteal; geniculate)

Work-up

  • ABI = ankle ÷ higher arm; ≤ 0.9 PAD, < 0.4 severe, > 1.4 calcified (board)
  • Segmental drop ≥ 20 mm Hg localises · treadmill if resting ABI normal
  • CT(A) diagnoses; angiogram (DSA) = gold standard lumen, used on the table; MRA: cost, gadolinium NSF

Aortoiliac · fem-pop · tibial

  • AIOD starts infrarenal; hallmark ↓ femoral pulses
  • Leriche: buttock claudication + impotence + ↓ femorals
  • Blue toe: cholesterol microemboli — look upstream
  • Rx: stop smoking, aspirin, statin, exercise, cilostazol (PDE3; CI in HF)
  • Operate for lifestyle-limiting claudication, rest pain, tissue loss
  • Aortobifem 90/75% (5/10 y) · fem-fem 75% · axillobifem 60–85% (long = worse)
  • Fem-pop: SFA; femorals palpable, popliteal/pedal weak · GSV bypass 70–90%
  • Tibial: DM/ESRD, ulcers; open bypass for rest pain/tissue loss

Acute limb ischemia

  • Emboli: AF left atrium, post-MI LV, valves, myxoma · lodge at bifurcations
  • IV heparin first → CDT if motor/sensory intact → Fogarty
  • No lysis: stroke < 2 mo, major surgery < 2 wk, bleeding risk
  • Reperfusion → compartment syndrome: pain on passive stretch, 1st web space numb → fasciotomy
  • BKA 10 cm below tibial tuberosity; energy +10–40% (AKA +50–70%)
CAROTID · AORTA

Carotid

  • Bifurcation · bruit · amaurosis fugax + Hollenhorst plaque
  • TIA < 24 h · RIND > 24 h < 1 wk · stroke = infarct
  • Duplex screens/grades · MRI for early ischemia · echo for cardiac source
  • Asymptomatic 60–99%: 5.9% ↓ at 5 y · symptomatic > 70%: 26 → 9% at 2 y
  • CI: disabling stroke, occluded ICA, short life expectancy
  • CEA + patch (shunt) → CN XII and X injury · stent if skull base / sick

Aortic dissection

  • Intimal tear → false lumen; HTN, Marfan, bicuspid, cocaine, trauma
  • Tearing back pain; end-organ ischemia > rupture
  • DeBakey I (60%) root → down · II (10–15%) ascending · III (25–30%) distal to L subclavian
  • Stanford A (I+II) → open repair · B → BP control, TEVAR if fails
  • CTA; TEE if unstable · board: IV β-blocker first
  • Clamp → spinal cord ischemia (Adamkiewicz) → CSF drain

AAA

  • > 3 cm, infrarenal fusiform; grows 0.2–0.3 cm/yr
  • Risk: age, male, smoking, FHx, HTN · rupture: female, COPD, HTN, smoking, steroids
  • Screen men 65–70 / FHx · US screens · CT gold standard · x-ray eggshell
  • < 4.5 q6mo · 4.5–5 q3mo · 5–5.5 repair if female/familial/fast · > 5.5 repair
  • Also repair: symptomatic, > 5 mm/6 mo, saccular
  • EVAR: neck > 15 mm, angle < 60°, Ca < 50%
  • Post-op bloody stool → ischemic colitis; re-implant IMA if weak back-bleeding
  • Graft infection / aortoenteric fistula → remove graft + extra-anatomic bypass
  • Splenic: women, pregnancy rupture; repair > 2 cm or child-bearing age · Popliteal: men, emboli/thrombosis; repair > 2 cm
VISCERA · VASCULITIS · VEINS

Mesenteric

  • Elderly + AF + abdominal pain = SMA embolus (past jejunal branches) → CTA
  • Embolus: embolectomy + lifelong anticoag · thrombosis (20%): bypass · NOMI: rest, fluids, antibiotics · SMV: anticoag · dead bowel: resect
  • Chronic: middle-aged women, pain 30–90 min after meals, weight loss; CTA → revascularise

Renal (Goldblatt)

  • Stenosis → renin → AT II + aldosterone → HTN
  • Suspect: refractory HTN, flash pulmonary edema, HTN in the young
  • FMD: young women, R > L, 75% cure · atherosclerosis in older
  • Captopril scan (75/90) · renal vein renin · duplex, CTA/MRA · stent, bypass, nephrectomy

Vasospasm · vasculitis

  • Raynaud: white → blue → red; CCB, sympathectomy
  • GCA: jaw claudication; temporal artery biopsy; steroids
  • Takayasu: young women, aorta + pulmonary; DSA; steroids
  • Buerger: male smokers, distal arteries + veins, corkscrew collaterals; quit smoking

DVT

  • Virchow: stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury
  • Untreated proximal → 30–50% PE, 12% mortality · 30% recur in 10 y
  • Homans sign · duplex US · D-dimer nonspecific (rule-out only) · MRV for iliac/pelvic
  • LMWH/UFH/fondaparinux; warfarin overlap 5–10 d, INR 2–3 × 24 h
  • 3 mo if provoked/first · longer if unprovoked, recurrent, PE, cancer (LMWH)
  • IVC filter if anticoag contraindicated (retrievable)
  • Prevent: IPC (↑ fibrinolysis), stockings, LMWH/UFH
  • HIT: anti-heparin–PF4 IgG → stop heparin, argatroban/bivalirudin
  • Alba dolens = white milk leg · cerulea = blue, threatened · then venous gangrene · PTS: compression, iliac stent
