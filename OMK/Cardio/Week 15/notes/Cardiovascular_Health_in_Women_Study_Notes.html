Women's Heart Health

Heart disease is the leading killer of women. This lecture covers how common it is, the traditional risk factors every woman shares with men, the risk enhancers unique to women (early menarche, premenstrual syndrome, pregnancy complications, menopause), and when menopausal hormone therapy helps or harms.

Why Women's Heart Health

Women share every traditional cardiac risk factor with men, but they also carry female-specific risk enhancers from their reproductive history: menarche, premenstrual syndrome, pregnancy complications and menopause. The lecture argues these are "only recently" being considered and that heart health matters across the whole lifespan, including the reproductive years and the menopause transition.

The three objectives

  1. The significance of heart disease in womenHow common and how deadly it is (Topic 1).
  2. Risk factors unique to womenMenarche, premenstrual syndrome, pregnancy complications (Topics 2–4).
  3. Heart health across the lifespanEspecially the reproductive years and the menopause transition, and the role of hormone therapy (Topics 5–6).

What "cardiovascular disease" means here (slide 3)

  • Coronary artery disease (the most prevalent, and the focus) · cerebrovascular disease (stroke, TIA) · high blood pressure · congestive heart failure.
  • Valvular disease, arrhythmias and congenital heart disease are also CVD but are set aside for this talk.
Objective 1 · Topic 1
⭐ Leading cause of death in US women — 1 in 3

Slides 5–9 · epidemiology · two myths · young women · prevalence by age and sex

Epidemiology & Myths

AbbreviationsCVD = cardiovascular disease · MI = myocardial infarction · CDC = Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

Leading killer

US women

CVD is the leading cause of death in US women: 1 in 3 deaths (CDC), about one death per minute.

Burden

Living with CVD

More than 60 million US women are living with CVD.

Outcomes

Worse than men

Women are more likely to die from heart disease than men, especially after menopause. Under 65, acute-MI mortality in women is almost twice that of men.

Young women

Not only after menopause

> 10,000 US women under 45 have an MI each year. Between 45 and 64, 1 in 9 women develop symptoms of CVD.

❌

Myth 1

"Heart disease is a man's disease." It's the leading cause of death in women.

❌

Myth 2

"Heart attacks occur in women after menopause." More than 10,000 women under 45 have one every year.

Prevalence by age and sex (slide 8)

Men have more CVD at younger ages (20–39: 14.2% vs 9.7%), the sexes are nearly equal at 60–79, and women overtake men at 80+ (86.7% vs 80.1%).

🩺 Some good news (slide 9)

Mortality from acute MI fell by as much as 50% in the 1990s and 2000s, though CVD is still the leading cause of death in most developed countries.

Q: A patient says heart attacks only happen to women after menopause. Correct her with two numbers from the lecture.

Answer

More than 10,000 US women under 45 have an MI each year, and under 65 a woman's MI mortality is almost twice a man's. CVD causes 1 in 3 deaths in women.

Q: At what age does CVD prevalence in women overtake men?

Answer

80 and over (86.7% vs 80.1%); men lead in younger groups.

Objective 2 · Topic 2
⭐ Women: age counts as a risk factor from 50

Slides 10–11 · the traditional list · the female-specific enhancers · how women's heart disease presents

Traditional Risk Factors (and How Women Differ)

AbbreviationsCAD = coronary artery disease · MI = myocardial infarction · SCAD = spontaneous coronary artery dissection · MINOCA = MI with non-obstructive coronary arteries · ASCVD = atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease
📋

Traditional (slide 10)

Age (men > 40, women > 50) · family history · smoking · hypertension · high cholesterol · diabetes · physical inactivity · obesity.

♀️

Female-specific risk enhancers (slide 11)

Age at menarche · postmenopausal status · pregnancy-related complications (plus premenstrual syndrome, slide 13).

Where women's heart disease looks different Board add-on

  • Diabetes and smoking raise coronary risk proportionally more in women than in men.
  • Women more often have atypical or accompanying symptoms of MI (dyspnea, fatigue, nausea, jaw/back pain) and are diagnosed and treated later.
  • Causes that cluster in women: SCAD (young women, peripartum, fibromuscular dysplasia), Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy (postmenopausal women after emotional stress), MINOCA and coronary microvascular dysfunction.
  • Autoimmune disease (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis) and pregnancy complications are listed as ASCVD risk enhancers in prevention guidelines.

Q: From what age does the lecture count age itself as a coronary risk factor in women, compared with men?

Answer

Women over 50; men over 40.

Q: Name the three female-specific risk enhancers on slide 11.

Answer

Age at menarche, postmenopausal status, pregnancy-related complications.

Objective 2 · Topic 3
⭐ Menarche ≤ 10 → RR 1.27 for CHD

Slides 12–13 · age at menarche (1.2 million women) · premenstrual syndrome and hypertension (Nurses' Health Study II)

Menarche & Premenstrual Syndrome

AbbreviationsCHD = coronary heart disease · CVD = cardiovascular disease · PMS = premenstrual syndrome · RR / HR = relative risk / hazard ratio · CI = confidence interval

Age at menarche (Canoy, Circulation 2015)

  • 1.2 million women (mean age 56) without known CVD at baseline.
  • Most common menarche age: 13 (25%). 4% reported menarche at 10 or younger.
  • Early menarche (≤ 10 years) → RR 1.27 (95% CI 1.22–1.31) for developing CHD.
  • The curve is U-shaped: lowest at 13, rising for both early and late (≥ 17, RR 1.23) menarche.

Premenstrual syndrome (Bertone-Johnson, Am J Epidemiol 2015)

  • PMS: physical and emotional symptoms in the days before menses.
  • Women with PMS were more likely to develop hypertension: adjusted HR 1.4 (1.2–1.6).
  • For hypertension before age 40, PMS tripled the risk: HR 3.3 (1.7–6.5).
  • The study suggests a modifiable link (risk attenuated with high thiamine and riboflavin intake).

Q: Describe the relationship between age at menarche and coronary heart disease.

Answer

U-shaped: lowest risk at 13; menarche at ≤ 10 → RR 1.27, and late menarche (≥ 17) also raises risk.

Q: Which cardiovascular outcome was linked to premenstrual syndrome, and in which group was the association strongest?

Answer

Hypertension (HR 1.4), strongest for hypertension before age 40 (HR 3.3).

Objective 2 · Topic 4
⭐ Pregnancy is a cardiovascular "stress test"

Slides 14–21 · hypertension and diabetes of pregnancy · preeclampsia · pregnancy loss · preterm birth · the 50-year cohort

Pregnancy Complications as Risk Enhancers

AbbreviationsCVD = cardiovascular disease · CHD = coronary heart disease · HF = heart failure · GDM = gestational diabetes mellitus · DVT = deep venous thrombosis · TIA = transient ischemic attack · PTD = preterm delivery · SGA = small for gestational age · MI = myocardial infarction · OR / HR = odds / hazard ratio

The complications that predict later CVD (slide 14)

  • Hypertension and diabetes that develop during pregnancy
  • Spontaneous pregnancy loss
  • Preterm birth
  • Others: pre-existing hypertension, glycosuria, preeclampsia after 34 weeks, a dropping hemoglobin in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters.
🧠 Why pregnancy predicts heart disease Board add-on

Pregnancy raises cardiac output and insulin resistance and changes the vasculature. A woman who develops hypertension or diabetes under that stress is showing an underlying susceptibility that resurfaces later. Guidelines therefore list preeclampsia and other adverse pregnancy outcomes as ASCVD risk enhancers: ask about them in every woman's history.

Hypertension and diabetes in pregnancy (slides 15–16)

  • Preeclampsia: a multisystem, placentally mediated disease, usually after 32 weeks, with hypertension and proteinuria, often fetal growth restriction. Affects 2–8% of pregnancies. A history of preeclampsia raises future cardiovascular risk (Ahmed, JACC 2014). Board add-on Roughly doubles later CHD and stroke, and about quadruples later hypertension.
  • The year after delivery (Savitz, New York City 1995–2004): preeclampsia predicted heart failure (OR ~4.1), stroke/TIA (2.8), CHD (3.1) and DVT; gestational hypertension predicted heart failure (2.6); multiple gestation predicted heart failure (4.5) and CHD (4.5); gestational diabetes predicted type 2 diabetes (OR ~23).
  • Absolute rates in that first year were low (per 100,000 births: heart failure 30, type 2 diabetes 25, DVT 23.3, stroke/TIA 14.8, CHD 9.5).

Pregnancy loss (slides 17–18)

  • Meta-analysis (Oliver-Williams): miscarriage → OR ~1.45 for CHD; recurrent miscarriage → OR ~2.
  • Postmenopausal women (11,518 women, ~11 years): any miscarriage HR 1.42 · stillbirth HR 2.65 · more than three miscarriages HR 8.90 for MI.

Preterm birth (slide 19)

Women with a preterm delivery had a higher cumulative hazard of cardiovascular hospitalisation over about 20 years (Kessous 2013).

The 50-year cohort (slides 20–21, Cirillo & Cohn, Circulation 2015)

14,062 women (median age 26 at enrollment) followed for CVD death into their 60s. Adjusted hazard ratios:

  • Combinations multiply risk: preterm birth plus hemorrhage, gestational hypertension or pre-existing hypertension → 4–7× CVD death; preeclampsia plus pre-existing hypertension → nearly 6×.
  • Two new markers: glycosuria and hemoglobin decline.
  • The authors' point: obstetricians serve as primary care physicians for many young women and can use these complications to identify high-risk women early.

Q: A 38-year-old woman's history includes preeclampsia in two pregnancies. Why does this matter at her wellness visit?

Answer

Preeclampsia is a female-specific CVD risk enhancer (later heart failure, CHD, stroke, hypertension). Screen and treat her traditional risk factors earlier and more aggressively.

Q: In postmenopausal women, which pregnancy-loss history carried the highest MI risk?

Answer

More than three miscarriages (HR 8.90); stillbirth HR 2.65; any miscarriage HR 1.42.

Q: Name the two "new" pregnancy markers of later CVD death from the 50-year cohort.

Answer

Glycosuria (HR 4.2) and a hemoglobin decline in the 2nd–3rd trimester (HR 1.7).

Objective 3 · Topic 5
⭐ Postmenopausal status = same weight as male sex

Slides 22–26 · menopause as a risk factor · early menopause · two paradigms · what estrogen protects · what its loss does

Menopause & the Loss of Estrogen

AbbreviationsCVD = cardiovascular disease · ACC/AHA = American College of Cardiology / American Heart Association · E2 = estradiol · WHI = Women's Health Initiative · HERS = Heart and Estrogen/progestin Replacement Study · eNOS = endothelial nitric oxide synthase · VEGF = vascular endothelial growth factor · ROS = reactive oxygen species · FAO = fatty-acid oxidation · MMP = matrix metalloproteinase · IRS = insulin resistance syndrome · HRV = heart rate variability

The facts (slide 22)

  • CVD is unusual in premenopausal women.
  • ACC/AHA guidelines treat postmenopausal status as a risk factor with the same weight as male sex.
  • Early natural menopause (≤ 44 years) is associated with more CVD. Whether surgical vs natural menopause differs is conflicting.
  • But menopause hasn't been proven causal: postmenopausal women who develop CVD carry more traditional risk factors. Consistent with that, hormone therapy gave no benefit in WHI (mostly primary prevention) or HERS (secondary prevention).

Two paradigms (slide 23)

🔀

Traditional

Risk is minimal or absent before menopause, and menopause switches it on.

📈

Alternative

Risk is rising across the lifespan (including the reproductive years); menopause modulates it up or down rather than starting it.

What estrogen does, and what its loss does (slides 24–26)

TargetProtective role of E2After the E2 decline
Fibrosis↓ fibroblast migration and proliferation, ↓ collagen deposition (MMP, angiotensin II)↑ extracellular matrix
Vasculature↑ angiogenesis (VEGF), ↑ vasodilation (eNOS)Impaired hypoxia–angiogenesis coupling; plaque composition changes
MitochondriaPro-survival, ↑ fatty-acid oxidation, smaller infarcts, ↓ ROS—
Oxidative stressAnti-oxidant, ↑ cell survival↑ reactive oxygen species
  • Menopause (with genes, early programming, lifestyle and aging) drives ↑ sympathetic activity, ↓ parasympathetic tone and the insulin-resistance syndrome: insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, glucose intolerance, ↑ blood pressure, ↑ visceral fat.
  • These feed ↑ inflammation, ↓ heart rate variability, coagulopathy, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, sleep disturbance and depressive symptoms → ↑ CVD.
  • Board add-on Typical lipid shift after menopause: ↑ LDL and triglycerides, ↓ HDL.

Q: How do ACC/AHA guidelines weight postmenopausal status as a risk factor?

Answer

With the same weight as male sex.

Q: If estrogen is protective, why don't the slides call menopause a proven cause of CVD?

Answer

The association is observational: postmenopausal women who develop CVD carry more traditional risk factors, and replacing estrogen gave no cardiovascular benefit in WHI or HERS.

Objective 3 · Topic 6
⭐ The timing hypothesis: < 10 years and < 60 years old

Slides 27–33 · control the traditional risk factors · WHI and HERS · timing hypothesis · KEEPS and ELITE · biomarkers · who can take MHT

Prevention & Menopausal Hormone Therapy

AbbreviationsMHT = menopausal hormone therapy · WHI = Women's Health Initiative · HERS = Heart and Estrogen/progestin Replacement Study · KEEPS = Kronos Early Estrogen Prevention Study · ELITE = Early versus Late Intervention Trial with Estradiol · CIMT = carotid intima-media thickness · ASCVD = atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease · TIA = transient ischemic attack · VTE = venous thromboembolism

First: control the traditional risk factors (slide 27)

Good BP control · good cholesterol numbers · healthy diet · quit smoking · lose excess weight · exercise at least 30 minutes a day, most days.

Hormone therapy: the story so far (slides 28–31)

  1. The hypothesisWomen may spend up to 40% of their lives after menopause, and low ovarian estrogen was thought to explain the cardiometabolic changes.
  2. WHI and HERSEstrogen therapy did not reduce cardiovascular events and may have increased them. Board add-on WHI estrogen + progestin arm: more CHD, stroke, VTE and breast cancer.
  3. The catch: timingWomen in WHI and HERS were more than 10 years past menopause. Started < 10 years after menopause and before age 60, MHT appears to cut CHD risk by ~50% and all-cause mortality by ~30%. In older women far from menopause it has no effect on CHD or mortality.
  4. Trials of the timing hypothesisKEEPS: no CIMT increase with MHT in women < 3 years postmenopause. ELITE: low-dose oral estradiol slowed CIMT progression in women < 6 years postmenopause, but not in those > 10 years.
  5. Biomarkers (Lau)71 cardiovascular biomarkers: postmenopausal profiles resemble men's, and MHT restored the premenopausal profile.

Who can take it (slide 32)

🔴

Higher risk — avoid MHT

Known ASCVD/CAD/PAD · prior VTE or PE · stroke/TIA or MI · known clotting disorder · breast cancer · 10-year ASCVD risk ≥ 7.5%.

🟡

Caution

Diabetes · smoking · uncontrolled hypertension · obesity, sedentary or immobile · SLE, RA, migraine with aura · high TG or uncontrolled cholesterol · 10-year risk 5–7.4%.

🟢

Lower risk — acceptable

Recent menopause, normal weight and BP, active · 10-year risk < 5%.

🔑 Conclusions (slide 33)

Heart disease in women is a complex, very prevalent story. Address the traditional risk factors; hormonal status and reproductive history are unique risk factors only recently considered; and MHT may have a role in younger women within 10 years of the menopause transition.

Q: Why did WHI and HERS find no cardiovascular benefit from hormone therapy?

Answer

Participants were mostly more than 10 years past menopause. Per the timing hypothesis, MHT started < 10 years after menopause and before 60 appears to lower CHD (~50%) and mortality (~30%), while late initiation has no benefit.

Q: A 51-year-old, 1 year past menopause, with migraine with aura and a 10-year ASCVD risk of 6% asks about MHT. Traffic-light category?

Answer

Yellow — caution (migraine with aura; 10-year risk 5–7.4%).

Q: What did ELITE show?

Answer

Low-dose oral estradiol slowed carotid intima-media thickening in women < 6 years postmenopause but not in women > 10 years postmenopause.

🎯 Cardiovascular Health in Women — High-Yield One-Pager

Amina Qazi, DO, FACC · OMK 2 Cardio · Objective 1 significance → Objective 2 female-specific risk → Objective 3 lifespan, menopause and hormone therapy

SIGNIFICANCE

Numbers

  • CVD = leading cause of death in US women: 1 in 3 deaths, ~1 per minute; > 60 million living with CVD
  • > 10,000 women < 45 have an MI each year · under 65, MI mortality ~2× men's
  • 45–64: 1 in 9 women develop CVD symptoms
  • Women overtake men in prevalence at 80+ (86.7% vs 80.1%)
  • Acute-MI mortality fell ~50% in the 1990s–2000s

Myths

  • "A man's disease" ✗ · "Only after menopause" ✗

Risk factors

  • Traditional: age (women > 50, men > 40), family history, smoking, HTN, cholesterol, diabetes, inactivity, obesity
  • Female-specific: age at menarche · postmenopausal status · pregnancy complications (+ PMS)
  • Board: atypical symptoms; SCAD, Takotsubo, MINOCA, microvascular disease
REPRODUCTIVE YEARS

Menarche · PMS

  • U-shaped; nadir at 13 · ≤ 10 → RR 1.27 for CHD · ≥ 17 also ↑
  • PMS → hypertension: HR 1.4; HR 3.3 before age 40

Pregnancy complications

  • HTN and diabetes of pregnancy · pregnancy loss · preterm birth · pre-existing HTN · glycosuria · preeclampsia after 34 wk · Hb drop in 2nd–3rd trimester
  • Preeclampsia: placental, > 32 wk, HTN + proteinuria, 2–8% of pregnancies → later HF, CHD, stroke
  • Year after delivery: preeclampsia → HF OR ~4; GDM → type 2 diabetes OR ~23
  • Miscarriage OR ~1.45 · recurrent ~2 · MI: stillbirth HR 2.65, > 3 miscarriages HR 8.9
  • Preterm delivery → ↑ CV hospitalisation
  • 50-yr cohort, CVD death: glycosuria 4.2 · early preeclampsia 3.6 · pre-existing HTN 3.5 · PTD 2.1 · late preeclampsia 2.0 · Hb decline 1.7 · combos 4–7×
MENOPAUSE & MHT

Menopause

  • CVD unusual premenopause · postmenopause = same weight as male sex (ACC/AHA)
  • Early menopause ≤ 44 → ↑ CVD; surgical vs natural conflicting
  • Not proven causal (more risk factors; no benefit in WHI/HERS)
  • E2: ↓ fibrosis, ↑ VEGF angiogenesis, ↑ eNOS vasodilation, mitochondrial and anti-oxidant protection
  • Loss → ↑ sympathetic tone + insulin-resistance syndrome (dyslipidemia, glucose intolerance, ↑ BP, visceral fat)

Hormone therapy

  • WHI/HERS: no CV benefit, maybe harm — women > 10 y past menopause
  • Timing: < 10 y and < 60 → CHD ↓ ~50%, mortality ↓ ~30%; late → no effect
  • KEEPS: no CIMT rise (< 3 y) · ELITE: slowed CIMT < 6 y, not > 10 y
  • Lau: postmenopausal biomarkers look like men's; MHT restores premenopausal profile
  • 🔴 ASCVD, VTE, stroke/MI, clotting disorder, breast cancer, risk ≥ 7.5% · 🟡 DM, smoking, uncontrolled HTN, obesity, SLE/RA/migraine with aura, risk 5–7.4% · 🟢 < 5%
  • Everyone: BP, cholesterol, diet, no smoking, weight, ≥ 30 min exercise most days
