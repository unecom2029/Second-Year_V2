Orientation

One Diagram Explains It All

Dr. Qazi's goal for this session: understand the cardiac cycle well enough to read the Wiggers diagram. That one figure stacks the pressure tracings, the left ventricular volume curve, the ECG and the heart sounds on a shared time axis. Once you can read the normal diagram, each valve lesion is just a variation on it. Stenosis shows up as a pressure gradient, and regurgitation shows up as extra volume.

🎙️ From the recording: how she wants you to think

Blood flows from high pressure to low pressure. A valve opens when the chamber before it has the higher pressure, and it closes when the chamber after it overtakes. If you hold onto that one rule, every event on the diagram follows. She also said bluntly: on boards, "if you overthink the question you're gonna get it wrong." Learn the buzzwords for each lesion and trust them.

LO 1 & 3 · Topic 1
⭐ Valves open and close only because of pressure differences

systole vs diastole · the pressure rule · electrical before mechanical

Systole, Diastole & the Valve Rule

AbbreviationsLA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LAP / LVP / AoP = left atrial / left ventricular / aortic pressure · AV valves = atrioventricular valves (mitral, tricuspid) · semilunar valves = aortic, pulmonic · ECG = electrocardiogram · S1 / S2 = first / second heart sound
💥

Systole

Ventricular contraction and ejection. Starts at S1 (AV valves close) and ends at S2 (semilunar valves close). Made up of isovolumetric contraction, rapid ejection and reduced ejection.

🫗

Diastole

Ventricular relaxation and filling. Starts at S2. Made up of isovolumetric relaxation, rapid filling, diastasis (slow filling) and finally atrial systole (the "atrial kick").

The valve rule

  • For a valve to open, the pressure in the chamber before it (proximal) must be higher than the chamber after it (distal).
  • For a valve to close, pressure in the distal chamber must rise above the proximal chamber.
  • That gives four crossover points on the left side of the heart:
Pressure crossoverValve eventWhat you hear / see
LVP rises above LAPMitral valve closesS1; start of isovolumetric contraction
LVP rises above AoP (~80 mm Hg)Aortic valve opensEjection begins (silent in a normal valve)
LVP falls below AoPAortic valve closesS2 and the dicrotic notch; start of isovolumetric relaxation
LVP falls below LAPMitral valve opensFilling begins (silent normally; an opening snap in mitral stenosis)

Electrical before mechanical

  • P wave (atrial depolarization) comes just before atrial contraction.
  • QRS complex (ventricular depolarization) comes just before ventricular contraction.
  • T wave = ventricular repolarization, which happens during late ejection, just before relaxation.
  • Volume: LV volume falls during ejection and rises during filling. It is flat during both isovolumetric phases.
🧠 Why "isovolumetric"

In both isovolumetric phases all four valves are closed, so blood has nowhere to go and the volume can't change. Only the pressure moves: it rises in isovolumetric contraction and falls in isovolumetric relaxation. Board add-on There is never a moment when all the valves are open (AMBOSS).

Q: What has to happen to the pressures for the aortic valve to open, and what closes it again?

Answer

It opens when LV pressure exceeds aortic pressure (~80 mm Hg). It closes when LV pressure falls below aortic pressure at the end of ejection, producing S2 and the dicrotic notch.

Q: During which two phases are all four valves closed, and what changes in each?

Answer

Isovolumetric contraction, where pressure rises at constant volume, and isovolumetric relaxation, where pressure falls at constant volume.

Reading the Wiggers Diagram

AbbreviationsAo = aorta · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · EDV / ESV = end-diastolic / end-systolic volume · SV = stroke volume · ECG = electrocardiogram · S1–S3 = heart sounds · JVP = jugular venous pulse

The Wiggers diagram is one graph that combines the ECG, the aortic, LV and LA pressures, the LV volume and the heart sounds on one time axis. It shows how the electrical signal drives the mechanical events, and it is the reference "normal" that every lesion in Topics 6–9 is compared against.

Line by line

Aortic pressure

Grey top curve · ~80 to ~120 mm Hg

Rises during ejection because the stroke volume is pushed into it, then drifts down as blood runs off into the systemic circulation. The dicrotic notch marks aortic valve closure.

LV pressure

Blue · ~0–10 → ~120 mm Hg

Near zero through diastole. It shoots up in isovolumetric contraction, tracks right on top of aortic pressure during ejection (no gradient across a normal valve), and plunges in isovolumetric relaxation.

LA pressure

Grey bottom curve · ~5–10 mm Hg

Three small waves: a, c and v (below). In diastole, while the mitral valve is open, LA and LV pressures sit almost on top of each other.

LV volume

Red · EDV ~130 → ESV ~50 mL

Flat at EDV during isovolumetric contraction, falls steeply then slowly during ejection, flat at ESV during isovolumetric relaxation, then rapid filling, diastasis and the atrial kick. EDV − ESV = stroke volume.

ECG

Navy

P just before atrial contraction · QRS just before ventricular contraction · T = repolarization late in ejection.

Phonocardiogram

Grey bottom trace

S1 = mitral and tricuspid closure · S2 = aortic and pulmonic closure · S3 in early diastole during rapid filling.

The atrial pressure waves

WaveWhat causes itTiming
aAtrial contraction (atrial systole). Pressure rises as the atrium squeezes, then falls as blood empties into the LVRight after the P wave, end of diastole
cThe ventricle contracts against the closed mitral valve and bows the valve back into the LA, which briefly raises LA pressureStart of ventricular systole (lecturer: during early, rapid ejection)
vAtrial filling ("atrial diastole"). Pulmonary venous blood fills the LA against the closed mitral valve, so volume and pressure rise togetherLate systole, peaking just before the mitral valve opens
⚠️ Recording vs textbook: timing of the c wave

Dr. Qazi placed the c wave during rapid ejection, with the aortic valve already open. UWorld and AMBOSS place it at the start of ventricular systole, during isovolumetric contraction, when the AV valve closes and bulges back into the atrium. On the slide 11 diagram the c wave sits right at the boundary between the two. For boards, say "AV valve bulging at the onset of systole." Both versions agree on the mechanism.

🧠 Same waves, right side Board add-on

The jugular venous pulse shows the same a, c and v waves from the right atrium. Two board favourites: atrial fibrillation removes the a wave (no organized atrial contraction), and tricuspid regurgitation gives a giant v wave, just as mitral regurgitation does on the left (Topic 8). The dips are named too: the x descent (atrial relaxation) follows a and c, and the y descent (atrium empties when the AV valve opens) follows v.

The dicrotic notch

  • It lines up with aortic valve closure (S2).
  • It has two causes. The valve snaps shut, and the stretched aorta recoils elastically, giving a small rebound in pressure as blood runs off into the systemic circulation.

Q: On a left atrial pressure tracing, what produces the a wave, the c wave and the v wave?

Answer

a = atrial contraction (after the P wave). c = the closed mitral valve bulging back into the atrium as the ventricle starts to contract. v = atrial filling from the pulmonary veins against the closed mitral valve.

Q: What event does the dicrotic notch mark, and why is there a small pressure rebound after it?

Answer

Aortic valve closure (S2). The rebound comes from the valve snapping shut plus the elastic recoil of the stretched aorta.

Q: On the normal diagram, the LV volume is 130 mL at the end of diastole and 50 mL at the end of systole. What is the stroke volume?

Answer

80 mL (EDV − ESV), which gives an ejection fraction of about 62%.

🔑 The atrial kick

Atrial systole adds the last part of LV filling, which the lecturer put at about 20–25% of cardiac output (roughly a quarter to a third of LV volume). Atrial fibrillation loses the atrial kick, and that matters most in stiff or obstructed hearts such as aortic stenosis, mitral stenosis and hypertrophy. Board add-on Most textbooks give ~20% of filling at rest, rising in older or stiff ventricles.

🧠 Diastole shortens first Board add-on

When heart rate rises, diastole shortens much more than systole, and diastasis is the first part to go. Coronary arteries fill mainly in diastole, which is why tachycardia provokes angina in coronary disease, and why slowing the heart helps mitral stenosis (more time to empty the atrium).

Q: Put the three parts of ventricular filling in order and say which one is lost in atrial fibrillation.

Answer

Rapid filling → diastasis → atrial systole. Atrial fibrillation removes the atrial kick (and the a wave).

Q: In which phase does most ventricular filling occur, and which heart sound can appear then?

Answer

Rapid filling (early diastole, right after the mitral valve opens). An S3 occurs here when the ventricle is volume-overloaded.

Q: During reduced ejection, which ECG wave is inscribed and which atrial wave is building?

Answer

The T wave (ventricular repolarization), while the v wave builds as the LA fills against the closed mitral valve.

🌊

S3: a VOLUME problem

"You're trying to fill the ventricle in the context of a lot of volume." It's a marker of volume overload on exam, typically in HFrEF / dilated cardiomyopathy, and in mitral and aortic regurgitation. Board add-on Cadence "Ken-tuck-y" (S1–S2–S3).

🧱

S4: a PRESSURE / stiffness problem

"You're trying to fill the ventricle in the context of high pressure." The hypertrophied ventricle can't stretch, so it fills poorly. Think LVH, aortic stenosis, hypertensive heart disease. Board add-on Cadence "Ten-nes-see" (S4–S1–S2).

⚠️ Recording slip: where the S4 falls

In the recording the lecturer said S4 is "just before S2." It is just before S1, in late diastole, because it comes from atrial contraction (look at the S4 tick on the lecture figure, slide 12, sitting right before S1). Her mechanism (the atrial kick into a non-compliant ventricle) is correct.

Q: A 68-year-old with long-standing hypertension has an extra sound just before S1. What is it and why does it happen?

Answer

An S4: the atrial kick forces blood into a stiff, hypertrophied (non-compliant) LV. It disappears if the patient goes into atrial fibrillation.

Q: A patient with a dilated cardiomyopathy and an ejection fraction of 20% has an extra sound right after S2. What does it signify?

Answer

An S3, which marks volume overload: rapid early filling of an already full ventricle.

LO 3 · Topic 5
⭐ Tachycardia helps a healthy heart and hurts a sick one

SV, EF and CO from the volume curve · why heart rate cuts both ways

Stroke Volume, EF, Cardiac Output & Heart Rate

AbbreviationsSV = stroke volume · EDV / ESV = end-diastolic / end-systolic volume · EF = ejection fraction · CO = cardiac output · HR = heart rate · LV = left ventricle · HFrEF = heart failure with reduced ejection fraction

Stroke volume

SV = EDV − ESV

The volume ejected in one beat, between S1 and S2.

Ejection fraction

EF = SV ÷ EDV

The fraction of the filled ventricle that's ejected. Board add-on Normal is about 50–70%.

Cardiac output

CO = SV × HR

The volume pumped per minute. A low SV means a low CO unless HR makes up the difference. Board add-on About 5 L/min at rest.

EDV (mL)ESV (mL)HR (bpm)

Heart rate cuts both ways

🏃

Normal, compliant heart

With exercise, HR, BP and contractility all rise. A healthy heart on the Starling curve also stretches to hold a larger stroke volume, so a faster rate raises CO.

💔

Sick heart (cardiomyopathy)

SV is already low. A fast rate cuts diastolic filling time, so SV falls further, and tachycardia can actually lower CO.

Q: EDV 150 mL, ESV 90 mL, HR 100 bpm. Calculate SV, EF and CO.

Answer

SV = 60 mL; EF = 60/150 = 40%; CO = 60 × 100 = 6.0 L/min.

Q: Why can a heart rate of 140 lower cardiac output in a patient with a dilated cardiomyopathy, when it raises output in an athlete?

Answer

The cardiomyopathy patient's stroke volume is already impaired. Tachycardia shortens diastolic filling time, so SV drops further and outweighs the gain in rate. The athlete's compliant ventricle keeps filling and stretching, so CO = SV × HR rises.

LO 4 · Topic 6
⭐ Diastolic rumble + opening snap at the apex

the mitral apparatus · rheumatic disease · the LA–LV diastolic gradient

Mitral Stenosis

AbbreviationsMS = mitral stenosis · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LAP / LVP = left atrial / left ventricular pressure · LVEDP = LV end-diastolic pressure · Ao = aorta · AF = atrial fibrillation · OS = opening snap · A2 = aortic part of S2 · PBMC = percutaneous balloon mitral commissurotomy (balloon valvuloplasty) · TEE = transesophageal echocardiography

The mitral apparatus first

  • Two leaflets, anterior and posterior. The anterior one looks bigger and the posterior is crescent-shaped, but their surface areas are about the same.
  • Chordae tendineae tie the leaflets to two papillary muscles (anterolateral and posteromedial). Chordae from each papillary muscle go to both leaflets.
  • The LV itself is part of the apparatus: LV contraction pulls on the papillary muscles and chordae to close the valve. This is why LV disease can cause MR (Topic 8).

What it is and who gets it

  • Restricted opening of the mitral orifice. The lecturer's example: a normal valve opens to about 4 cm², and in MS the orifice is smaller.
  • Most common cause worldwide: rheumatic heart disease. It's everywhere in South Asia (India, Pakistan), where balloon valvuloplasty is the bread-and-butter procedure, but rare in the US, where a few specialists do it.

🔑 Hallmark: the diastolic LA–LV gradient

LA pressure stays above LV diastolic pressure all through diastole (the grey shaded area on the tracing). Normally, once the mitral valve opens, flow is laminar and the two pressures equalize (the lines sit on top of each other, gradient 0–5 mm Hg). In MS, flow is turbulent and the gradient persists.

  • The c wave may fade as the disease progresses and the leaflets stiffen (it's still there while the leaflets are mobile).
  • Today the diagnosis and its severity come from echocardiography (including 3D), which is more accurate than the old cath-lab tracings.

On exam

🩺 Buzzwords she said will be on boards

A diastolic, rumbling murmur heard best at the apex, always with an opening snap. The vignette: a patient who grew up where rheumatic fever is common (e.g. recently emigrated from India), with a childhood streptococcal infection. Think MS immediately.

Board details Board add-on

  • Severity: severe MS = valve area ≤ 1.5 cm². The shorter the A2–to–opening-snap interval, the more severe (high LA pressure opens the valve earlier).
  • Presystolic accentuation of the rumble comes from atrial contraction, so it disappears in AF. UWorld describes S1 as usually soft (a stiff, calcified valve); older teaching gives a loud S1 while the leaflets are still pliable.
  • The big LA leads to AF and LA thrombus, so stroke can be the first sign. Anticoagulate with warfarin (preferred over direct oral anticoagulants in MS).
  • Back-pressure leads to pulmonary edema, hemoptysis, pulmonary hypertension and RV failure. Pregnancy (more blood volume) often unmasks it, and about 80% of rheumatic MS is in women.
  • Treatment: β-blockers (more diastolic filling time), diuretics, and PBMC for severe symptomatic disease.

Q: A 34-year-old woman who emigrated from Pakistan has exertional dyspnea and a low-pitched diastolic murmur at the apex preceded by a sharp early diastolic sound. What will the LA and LV pressure tracings show?

Answer

Rheumatic mitral stenosis with an opening snap. LA pressure stays above LV diastolic pressure throughout diastole (a persistent diastolic gradient). The LA is high and enlarged, and LV diastolic pressure is low.

Q: Why are LA and LV pressures nearly identical during diastole in a normal heart?

Answer

A wide-open mitral valve allows laminar, unobstructed flow, so pressure equalizes across it (gradient ≈ 0–5 mm Hg).

LO 4 · Topic 7
⭐ Systolic crescendo–decrescendo, right 2nd interspace, blunted carotids

bicuspid vs calcific · concentric hypertrophy · the LV–aortic systolic gradient · TAVR

Aortic Stenosis

AbbreviationsAS = aortic stenosis · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LVH = left ventricular hypertrophy · Ao = aorta · CT = computed tomography · TAVR = transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVI = implantation) · SAVR = surgical aortic valve replacement · PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention · RUSB = right upper sternal border (2nd intercostal space) · LVEF = LV ejection fraction · S2 / S4 = heart sounds

Who gets it: a bimodal disease

🧬

40s–60s: bicuspid aortic valve

Born with a congenitally abnormal valve. AS is the most common lesion of a bicuspid valve (AR and mixed disease also occur). A bicuspid valve always comes with an aortopathy (abnormal aortic connective tissue), so screen for an ascending aortic aneurysm and coarctation, e.g. with a contrast CT of the chest.

🦴

70s–90s: calcific (age-related)

A normal three-leaflet valve calcifies with age. Catherine Otto's pathology work found inflammatory cells and actual lamellar bone in the leaflets. Statins did not slow progression. Treatment is mechanical: replace the valve.

🔑 Hallmark: the systolic LV–aortic gradient

LV systolic pressure is far above aortic systolic pressure. The peaks split apart (grey shading), whereas on the normal Wiggers diagram they sit on top of each other.

🎙️ She repeated this: LA pressure is ALWAYS high in AS

"Take my word on this." The cause isn't a problem at the mitral valve. It's the hypertrophied, non-compliant LV, "the epitome of diastolic dysfunction," which can't accept a normal volume. That is a different mechanism from MS, where the obstruction sits right at the LA outlet.

Why the patient gets symptoms

  • Compensation is concentric LVH. Myocardial hypertrophy is never normal (unlike skeletal muscle): the wall is thick and stiff. Afterload rises (with peripheral vasoconstriction), preload rises, and eventually the ventricle falls off the Starling curve into heart failure.
  • Exercise intolerance comes first ("I get really exhausted walking up that hill"), because CO can't rise with exercise.
  • Angina from a supply–demand mismatch: low CO, plus a thick muscle with high O₂ demand.
  • Exertional syncope: CO can't rise to meet exercise.
  • Congestive heart failure at the end.

On exam

🩺 Buzzwords

A systolic crescendo–decrescendo murmur heard best at the right 2nd intercostal space, with blunted (weak, delayed) carotid upstrokes. Pair it with a young patient with a bicuspid valve or an 85-year-old with new exercise intolerance.

Severity clues: as AS worsens, the murmur's peak moves later (more crescendo) and can muffle S2. A crisp S2 makes severe AS less likely. A paradoxically split S2 can occur (delayed aortic closure) but often doesn't.

⚠️ Pulse pressure in AS: recording vs UWorld

A student asked whether AS gives a narrow pulse pressure. Dr. Qazi: not necessarily. It may be wide, normal or narrow, and it is "not the defining characteristic" (unlike AR, where a wide pulse pressure is the hallmark). UWorld lists a narrow pulse pressure with pulsus parvus et tardus as the expected finding in severe AS. For boards: parvus et tardus + late-peaking murmur + soft S2 are the reliable signs, and a narrow pulse pressure supports them.

Treatment

  • No medical therapy slows it, so it must be monitored and then replaced. The lecturer's practice is to intervene once it reaches severe on echo, rather than waiting for symptoms.
  • TAVR is now first-line, mostly transfemoral, and "kind of like having a PCI." Surgical replacement (SAVR) means at least 5 days in hospital and a month or more for the sternotomy to heal.

Board details Board add-on

  • Severe AS on echo: jet ≥ 4 m/s, mean gradient ≥ 40 mm Hg, valve area ≤ 1.0 cm².
  • Guideline triggers for valve replacement: severe AS with any symptom, or severe AS with LVEF < 50%.
  • The murmur radiates to the carotids, and an S4 is common.
  • Severe AS is preload-dependent: avoid nitrates and over-diuresis.
  • Symptomatic severe AS untreated: mean survival about 1–3 years.
  • Heyde syndrome: AS plus GI bleeding from angiodysplasia, because the shear stress across the valve cleaves von Willebrand factor.

Q: An 84-year-old has new exertional fatigue and a harsh systolic crescendo–decrescendo murmur at the right 2nd intercostal space with weak, delayed carotid pulses. What does simultaneous LV and aortic pressure recording show?

Answer

Calcific aortic stenosis: a large systolic gradient, with LV systolic pressure far above aortic systolic pressure. LA pressure is also high, because of the stiff LV.

Q: A 45-year-old is found to have AS from a bicuspid valve. Which two aortic problems should you screen for?

Answer

Ascending aortic aneurysm and coarctation of the aorta, because of the associated aortopathy.

Q: Why does angina occur in AS even with normal coronary arteries?

Answer

Supply–demand mismatch: the hypertrophied LV has a high oxygen demand while the fixed obstruction limits cardiac output (supply), especially with exercise.

LO 4 · Topic 8
⭐ "Tall v wave" = mitral regurgitation

LA filled from two sources · primary (prolapse) vs secondary (functional) MR · click and late systolic murmur

Mitral Regurgitation & Prolapse

AbbreviationsMR = mitral regurgitation · MVP = mitral valve prolapse · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LVEDP = LV end-diastolic pressure · MI = myocardial infarction · PMI = point of maximal impulse · AF = atrial fibrillation · S1 / S3 = heart sounds
  • Blood leaks back into the LA through a mitral valve that should be closed, so it happens in systole. It's very common: after AS, the second most common lesion in her clinic.
  • The LA now fills from two sources: normal pulmonary venous return plus the regurgitant jet. LA volume and pressure rise, and the LA enlarges.
  • That extra LA volume goes into the LV in diastole, making MR a high-preload state: LVEDP rises and the LV dilates (eccentric hypertrophy).
ChamberNormalMitral regurgitation
LA1025 ↑, with a tall v wave
LV120/10110/25: end-diastolic pressure ↑
🔑 Hallmark: a tall v wave

"That's what's gonna be on the exam." The v wave is the LA filling phase, and in MR it's swollen by the regurgitant volume. If a vignette mentions a tall v wave on a hemodynamic tracing, think significant MR, which comes with a high LV end-diastolic pressure.

🧠 Big LA in MS vs MR

Both enlarge the LA. In MS it's mainly a pressure problem from the obstruction. In MR it's mainly a volume problem from two inflows.

Primary vs secondary MR

🎈

Primary: the valve itself (MVP)

Most common cause of MR in the US: mitral valve prolapse. The leaflets are myxomatous: long, floppy, infiltrated with proteoglycans. A long leaflet billows up into the LA and leaves a gap. Tension stretches the chordae, so it worsens over time. If a chord ruptures, the leaflet becomes flail, causing a huge gap, acute heart failure, and surgery before discharge.

🫀

Secondary (functional): the LV

The valve is normal; the ventricle is not. A dilated cardiomyopathy stretches the annulus and pulls on the papillary muscles, causing a central leak. An inferior MI damages the wall under the posteromedial papillary muscle, which tethers the posterior leaflet down (the opposite of prolapse); this is ischemic MR.

  • The distinction matters because treatment differs. Primary MR is a valve problem: surgical mitral valve repair is the standard.
  • Repair is highly specialized. Send the patient to a surgeon with about 95% odds of repair rather than replacement. A good repair lasts a lifetime, while a prosthetic valve has a limited lifespan and commits the patient to another procedure.

On exam

🩺 Buzzwords

A systolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla (and the back). Functional MR is holosystolic, the same pitch throughout. MVP: a mid-systolic click followed by a late systolic murmur. The click is the prolapsing leaflet and chordae snapping taut. As with any valve lesion, the first symptom is usually exercise intolerance, and later heart failure.

Board details Board add-on

  • Chronic severe MR: S3, a soft S1, a laterally displaced PMI, and frequent AF.
  • Acute MR (papillary muscle rupture after an inferior MI, chordal rupture, endocarditis): pulmonary edema and shock, with a murmur that may be soft or absent because LA pressure rises fast and equalizes with the LV.
  • MVP maneuvers: anything that shrinks the LV (standing, Valsalva) makes the click earlier and the murmur longer. Squatting and handgrip do the opposite. MVP is associated with Marfan and Ehlers–Danlos syndromes.
  • Handgrip (↑ afterload) increases the MR murmur.

Q: A pulmonary capillary wedge (LA) tracing shows a very tall v wave. Which lesion, and why?

Answer

Mitral regurgitation. During systole the LA fills from the pulmonary veins and the regurgitant jet, so the filling wave (v) towers.

Q: A thin 30-year-old woman has a mid-systolic click followed by a late systolic murmur at the apex. What is the valve pathology?

Answer

Mitral valve prolapse from myxomatous degeneration (elongated, floppy leaflets), the most common cause of primary MR in the US.

Q: Three days after an inferior MI a patient develops new MR. Which papillary muscle is involved, and is this primary or secondary MR?

Answer

The posteromedial papillary muscle (supplied by the inferior wall). Dysfunction is secondary (ischemic, functional) MR; outright rupture causes acute MR and needs emergency surgery.

⭐ Wide pulse pressure + diastolic murmur + bounding pulses

Aortic Regurgitation

AbbreviationsAR = aortic regurgitation · AS = aortic stenosis · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LVEDP = LV end-diastolic pressure · Ao = aorta · LSB = left sternal border · RUSB = right upper sternal border · PMI = point of maximal impulse · S3 = third heart sound

Causes

  • Less common than AS or MR. In the lecturer's practice the commonest setting is bacterial endocarditis.
  • Bicuspid aortic valve: mostly AS, but many have mixed disease and some have pure AR.
  • Marfan syndrome (a connective-tissue disorder): AR, MVP, and ascending aortic aneurysm.
  • Board add-on UWorld: the commonest cause in resource-rich countries is a bicuspid valve, and rheumatic disease in resource-limited ones. Aortic root dilation (Marfan, Ehlers–Danlos, ankylosing spondylitis, giant cell or Takayasu arteritis) is another route. Acute AR comes from aortic dissection or endocarditis.

🔑 Hallmark: a widened pulse pressure

Systolic pressure high, diastolic pressure low. LV and aortic systolic peaks are superimposed (no obstruction, unlike AS). The difference shows in diastole, where aortic pressure falls away.

Remodeling: stenosis vs regurgitation (her recap)

🧱

AS: pressure overload

A fixed obstruction makes the LV work hard, causing concentric hypertrophy: a thick wall, non-compliant, with very high intraventricular pressure.

🎈

AR (and MR): volume overload

Too much volume sits in the LV, causing LV dilation with eccentric hypertrophy. Wall thickness may look normal, but the myocytes are still abnormal. MR adds LA enlargement on top.

Her rule: if the wall thickens, it's concentric; if the chamber enlarges, it's eccentric.

On exam

🩺 Buzzwords

A diastolic murmur plus bounding ("water-hammer") pulses. The pulse bounds because of the forceful ejection followed by rapid runoff.

Board details Board add-on

  • The murmur is early diastolic, decrescendo, high-pitched and blowing. Valvular AR is heard best at the left sternal border (sitting forward, end-expiration); root dilation at the RUSB.
  • Austin Flint murmur: an apical diastolic rumble in severe AR (the jet hits the anterior mitral leaflet), which mimics MS.
  • Hyperdynamic signs: Corrigan (carotid "water-hammer"), de Musset (head bobbing), Quincke (nail-bed pulsations), Traube (pistol-shot femorals), Duroziez (femoral to-and-fro bruit), Müller (uvula pulsation).
  • Also: S3, a displaced PMI, and angina (low diastolic pressure, so poor coronary perfusion; often nocturnal with bradycardia).
  • Acute AR (dissection, endocarditis): sudden pulmonary edema and shock, with a soft or short murmur and no time for a wide pulse pressure to develop.

Q: A tall 25-year-old with long fingers has BP 160/50, bounding pulses and a diastolic murmur. Explain the low diastolic pressure.

Answer

Aortic regurgitation (Marfan, aortic root). In diastole the aorta loses blood two ways: backward into the LV and forward into the systemic runoff. Diastolic pressure therefore falls, while the large stroke volume raises systolic pressure, giving a wide pulse pressure.

Q: Contrast the LV remodeling in aortic stenosis and aortic regurgitation.

Answer

AS is pressure overload, causing concentric hypertrophy (thick, stiff wall). AR is volume overload, causing dilation with eccentric hypertrophy.

🔑 Two questions solve most vignettes

1. Systolic or diastolic? In systole the aortic valve should be open and the mitral closed, so a systolic murmur is AS or MR. In diastole it's the reverse, so a diastolic murmur is MS or AR.
2. Pressure or volume? A stenosis creates a gradient across the valve. A regurgitant valve dumps volume into the chamber behind it: a tall v wave in the LA (MR), or a dilated LV with a wide pulse pressure (AR).

Q: Which two lesions produce a systolic murmur, and which hemodynamic sign separates them?

Answer

AS and MR. AS has an LV–aortic systolic gradient (with blunted carotids). MR has a tall LA v wave with no gradient between the LV and aorta.

Q: Which two lesions produce a diastolic murmur, and which hemodynamic sign separates them?

Answer

MS and AR. MS has an LA–LV diastolic gradient (opening snap, rumble). AR has a wide pulse pressure with bounding pulses.

Q: In which lesion is the LV small and underfilled rather than hypertrophied or dilated?

Answer

Mitral stenosis. The obstruction is upstream of the LV, so the LV is protected and underfilled while the LA takes the strain.

THE NORMAL CYCLE

Valve rule

  • Flow goes high → low pressure. A valve opens when the proximal chamber is higher, closes when the distal one overtakes
  • LVP > LAP: mitral closes (S1) · LVP > AoP: aortic opens · LVP < AoP: aortic closes (S2, dicrotic notch) · LVP < LAP: mitral opens
  • P before atrial contraction · QRS before ventricular contraction · T = repolarization in late ejection

Seven phases

  • 1 atrial systole (a wave, kick, S4) · 2 isovolumetric contraction (all closed, P ↑, V flat, c wave)
  • 3 rapid / 4 reduced ejection (aortic open, T wave, v builds)
  • 5 isovolumetric relaxation (all closed, P ↓, V flat, v peaks)
  • 6 rapid filling (most filling, S3) · 7 diastasis (shortens first with ↑ HR)

Waves & notch

  • a = atrial contraction · c = AV valve bulges back at onset of systole · v = atrial filling
  • AF: no a wave, no kick, no S4
  • Dicrotic notch = aortic closure + elastic recoil
  • Normal: LV and aortic peaks superimposed; LA and LV diastolic pressures superimposed

Numbers

  • Ao 120/80 · LV 120/10 · LA ~10 · EDV ~130, ESV ~50 mL
  • SV = EDV − ESV · EF = SV/EDV (50–70%) · CO = SV × HR (~5 L/min)
  • Atrial kick ≈ 20–25% of output (lecture)
SOUNDS & OUTPUT

Heart sounds

  • S1 AV closure · S2 semilunar closure
  • S3 right after S2: rapid filling of a volume-overloaded LV (HFrEF, MR, AR); can be normal in the young · "Ken-tuck-y"
  • S4 just before S1: kick into a stiff LV (LVH, AS, hypertension) · "Ten-nes-see" · recording said "before S2" (slip)
  • Paradoxical split S2: severe AS, LBBB · fixed: ASD

Heart rate

  • Healthy: ↑ HR + ↑ contractility + stretch → ↑ CO
  • Sick heart: tachycardia cuts filling time → ↓ SV → ↓ CO

Two-question method

  • Systolic murmur → AS or MR · diastolic → MS or AR
  • Stenosis → gradient · regurgitation → extra volume
  • Pressure overload → concentric LVH · volume overload → dilation (eccentric)

Mitral apparatus

  • 2 leaflets of ~equal area · chordae from both papillary muscles go to both leaflets · the LV is part of the apparatus

AS

  • Bimodal: bicuspid (40s–60s; screen for ascending aneurysm + coarctation) vs calcific (70s+; lamellar bone; statins don't help)
  • Exercise intolerance → angina → exertional syncope → HF · LA always high (stiff LV)
  • Severe: late-peaking murmur, soft S2 · ≥ 4 m/s, ≥ 40 mm Hg, ≤ 1.0 cm² · TAVR first-line · avoid nitrates

MS · MR · AR extras

  • MS: severe ≤ 1.5 cm²; short A2–OS = severe; AF + LA clot (warfarin); PBMC
  • MR: MVP = myxomatous, chordal rupture → flail → acute HF · secondary: dilated LV, inferior MI (posteromedial papillary) · repair over replace
  • AR: endocarditis, bicuspid, Marfan / root, dissection (acute) · Corrigan, de Musset, Quincke, Austin Flint
