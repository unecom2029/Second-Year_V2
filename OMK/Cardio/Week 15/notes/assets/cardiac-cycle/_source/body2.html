One Diagram Explains It All

Dr. Qazi's goal for this session: understand the cardiac cycle well enough to read the Wiggers diagram. That one figure stacks the pressure tracings, the left ventricular volume curve, the ECG and the heart sounds on a shared time axis. Once you can read the normal diagram, each valve lesion is just a variation on it. Stenosis shows up as a pressure gradient, and regurgitation shows up as extra volume.

🎙️ From the recording: how she wants you to think

Blood flows from high pressure to low pressure. A valve opens when the chamber before it has the higher pressure, and it closes when the chamber after it overtakes. If you hold onto that one rule, every event on the diagram follows. She also said bluntly: on boards, "if you overthink the question you're gonna get it wrong." Learn the buzzwords for each lesion and trust them.

⭐ Valves open and close only because of pressure differences

Systole, Diastole & the Valve Rule

AbbreviationsLA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LAP / LVP / AoP = left atrial / left ventricular / aortic pressure · AV valves = atrioventricular valves (mitral, tricuspid) · semilunar valves = aortic, pulmonic · ECG = electrocardiogram · S1 / S2 = first / second heart sound
💥

Systole

Ventricular contraction and ejection. Starts at S1 (AV valves close) and ends at S2 (semilunar valves close). Made up of isovolumetric contraction, rapid ejection and reduced ejection.

🫗

Diastole

Ventricular relaxation and filling. Starts at S2. Made up of isovolumetric relaxation, rapid filling, diastasis (slow filling) and finally atrial systole (the "atrial kick").

The valve rule (slide 8 + recording)

  • For a valve to open, the pressure in the chamber before it (proximal) must be higher than the chamber after it (distal).
  • For a valve to close, pressure in the distal chamber must rise above the proximal chamber.
  • That gives four crossover points on the left side of the heart:
Pressure crossoverValve eventWhat you hear / see
LVP rises above LAPMitral valve closesS1; start of isovolumetric contraction
LVP rises above AoP (~80 mm Hg)Aortic valve opensEjection begins (silent in a normal valve)
LVP falls below AoPAortic valve closesS2 and the dicrotic notch; start of isovolumetric relaxation
LVP falls below LAPMitral valve opensFilling begins (silent normally; an opening snap in mitral stenosis)

Electrical before mechanical (slides 4, 8)

  • P wave (atrial depolarization) comes just before atrial contraction.
  • QRS complex (ventricular depolarization) comes just before ventricular contraction.
  • T wave = ventricular repolarization, which happens during late ejection, just before relaxation.
  • Volume: LV volume falls during ejection and rises during filling. It is flat during both isovolumetric phases.
🧠 Why "isovolumetric"

In both isovolumetric phases all four valves are closed, so blood has nowhere to go and the volume can't change. Only the pressure moves: it rises in isovolumetric contraction and falls in isovolumetric relaxation. Board add-on There is never a moment when all the valves are open (AMBOSS).

Q: What has to happen to the pressures for the aortic valve to open, and what closes it again?

Answer

It opens when LV pressure exceeds aortic pressure (~80 mm Hg). It closes when LV pressure falls below aortic pressure at the end of ejection, producing S2 and the dicrotic notch.

Q: During which two phases are all four valves closed, and what changes in each?

Answer

Isovolumetric contraction, where pressure rises at constant volume, and isovolumetric relaxation, where pressure falls at constant volume.

Reading the Wiggers Diagram

AbbreviationsAo = aorta · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · EDV / ESV = end-diastolic / end-systolic volume · SV = stroke volume · ECG = electrocardiogram · S1–S3 = heart sounds · JVP = jugular venous pulse

The Wiggers diagram is one graph that combines the ECG, the aortic, LV and LA pressures, the LV volume and the heart sounds on one time axis. It shows how the electrical signal drives the mechanical events, and it is the reference "normal" that every lesion in Topics 6–9 is compared against.

{{F:wiggers}}

Line by line

Aortic pressure

Grey top curve · ~80 to ~120 mm Hg

Rises during ejection because the stroke volume is pushed into it, then drifts down as blood runs off into the systemic circulation. The dicrotic notch marks aortic valve closure.

LV pressure

Blue · ~0–10 → ~120 mm Hg

Near zero through diastole. It shoots up in isovolumetric contraction, tracks right on top of aortic pressure during ejection (no gradient across a normal valve), and plunges in isovolumetric relaxation.

LA pressure

Grey bottom curve · ~5–10 mm Hg

Three small waves: a, c and v (below). In diastole, while the mitral valve is open, LA and LV pressures sit almost on top of each other.

LV volume

Red · EDV ~130 → ESV ~50 mL

Flat at EDV during isovolumetric contraction, falls steeply then slowly during ejection, flat at ESV during isovolumetric relaxation, then rapid filling, diastasis and the atrial kick. EDV − ESV = stroke volume.

ECG

Navy

P just before atrial contraction · QRS just before ventricular contraction · T = repolarization late in ejection.

Phonocardiogram

Grey bottom trace

S1 = mitral and tricuspid closure · S2 = aortic and pulmonic closure · S3 in early diastole during rapid filling.

The atrial pressure waves (recording)

WaveWhat causes itTiming
aAtrial contraction (atrial systole). Pressure rises as the atrium squeezes, then falls as blood empties into the LVRight after the P wave, end of diastole
cThe ventricle contracts against the closed mitral valve and bows the valve back into the LA, which briefly raises LA pressureStart of ventricular systole (lecturer: during early, rapid ejection)

⚠️ Recording vs textbook: timing of the c wave

Dr. Qazi placed the c wave during rapid ejection, with the aortic valve already open. UWorld and AMBOSS place it at the start of ventricular systole, during isovolumetric contraction, when the AV valve closes and bulges back into the atrium. On the slide 11 diagram the c wave sits right at the boundary between the two. For boards, say "AV valve bulging at the onset of systole." Both versions agree on the mechanism.

🧠 Same waves, right side Board add-on

The jugular venous pulse shows the same a, c and v waves from the right atrium. Two board favourites: atrial fibrillation removes the a wave (no organized atrial contraction), and tricuspid regurgitation gives a giant v wave, just as mitral regurgitation does on the left (Topic 8). The dips are named too: the x descent (atrial relaxation) follows a and c, and the y descent (atrium empties when the AV valve opens) follows v.

The dicrotic notch (recording)

  • It lines up with aortic valve closure (S2).
  • It has two causes. The valve snaps shut, and the stretched aorta recoils elastically, giving a small rebound in pressure as blood runs off into the systemic circulation.

Q: On a left atrial pressure tracing, what produces the a wave, the c wave and the v wave?

Answer

a = atrial contraction (after the P wave). c = the closed mitral valve bulging back into the atrium as the ventricle starts to contract. v = atrial filling from the pulmonary veins against the closed mitral valve.

Q: What event does the dicrotic notch mark, and why is there a small pressure rebound after it?

Answer

Aortic valve closure (S2). The rebound comes from the valve snapping shut plus the elastic recoil of the stretched aorta.

Q: On the normal diagram, the LV volume is 130 mL at the end of diastole and 50 mL at the end of systole. What is the stroke volume?

Answer

80 mL (EDV − ESV), which gives an ejection fraction of about 62%.

🔑 The atrial kick (recording)

Atrial systole adds the last part of LV filling, which the lecturer put at about 20–25% of cardiac output (roughly a quarter to a third of LV volume). Atrial fibrillation loses the atrial kick, and that matters most in stiff or obstructed hearts such as aortic stenosis, mitral stenosis and hypertrophy. Board add-on Most textbooks give ~20% of filling at rest, rising in older or stiff ventricles.

🧠 Diastole shortens first Board add-on

When heart rate rises, diastole shortens much more than systole, and diastasis is the first part to go. Coronary arteries fill mainly in diastole, which is why tachycardia provokes angina in coronary disease, and why slowing the heart helps mitral stenosis (more time to empty the atrium).

Q: Put the three parts of ventricular filling in order and say which one is lost in atrial fibrillation.

Answer

Rapid filling → diastasis → atrial systole. Atrial fibrillation removes the atrial kick (and the a wave).

Q: In which phase does most ventricular filling occur, and which heart sound can appear then?

Answer

Rapid filling (early diastole, right after the mitral valve opens). An S3 occurs here when the ventricle is volume-overloaded.

Q: During reduced ejection, which ECG wave is inscribed and which atrial wave is building?

Answer

The T wave (ventricular repolarization), while the v wave builds as the LA fills against the closed mitral valve.

🌊

S3: a VOLUME problem

"You're trying to fill the ventricle in the context of a lot of volume." It's a marker of volume overload on exam, typically in HFrEF / dilated cardiomyopathy, and in mitral and aortic regurgitation. Board add-on Cadence "Ken-tuck-y" (S1–S2–S3).

🧱

S4: a PRESSURE / stiffness problem

"You're trying to fill the ventricle in the context of high pressure." The hypertrophied ventricle can't stretch, so it fills poorly. Think LVH, aortic stenosis, hypertensive heart disease. Board add-on Cadence "Ten-nes-see" (S4–S1–S2).

⚠️ Recording slip: where the S4 falls

In the recording the lecturer said S4 is "just before S2." It is just before S1, in late diastole, because it comes from atrial contraction (look at the S4 tick on the lecture figure, slide 12, sitting right before S1). Her mechanism (the atrial kick into a non-compliant ventricle) is correct.

Q: A 68-year-old with long-standing hypertension has an extra sound just before S1. What is it and why does it happen?

Answer

An S4: the atrial kick forces blood into a stiff, hypertrophied (non-compliant) LV. It disappears if the patient goes into atrial fibrillation.

Q: A patient with a dilated cardiomyopathy and an ejection fraction of 20% has an extra sound right after S2. What does it signify?

Answer

An S3, which marks volume overload: rapid early filling of an already full ventricle.

Stroke Volume, EF, Cardiac Output & Heart Rate

AbbreviationsSV = stroke volume · EDV / ESV = end-diastolic / end-systolic volume · EF = ejection fraction · CO = cardiac output · HR = heart rate · LV = left ventricle · HFrEF = heart failure with reduced ejection fraction

Stroke volume

SV = EDV − ESV

The volume ejected in one beat, between S1 and S2.

Ejection fraction

EF = SV ÷ EDV

The fraction of the filled ventricle that's ejected. Board add-on Normal is about 50–70%.

Cardiac output

CO = SV × HR

The volume pumped per minute. A low SV means a low CO unless HR makes up the difference. Board add-on About 5 L/min at rest.

Heart rate cuts both ways (recording)

🏃

Normal, compliant heart

With exercise, HR, BP and contractility all rise. A healthy heart on the Starling curve also stretches to hold a larger stroke volume, so a faster rate raises CO.

💔

Sick heart (cardiomyopathy)

SV is already low. A fast rate cuts diastolic filling time, so SV falls further, and tachycardia can actually lower CO.

Q: EDV 150 mL, ESV 90 mL, HR 100 bpm. Calculate SV, EF and CO.

Answer

SV = 60 mL; EF = 60/150 = 40%; CO = 60 × 100 = 6.0 L/min.

Q: Why can a heart rate of 140 lower cardiac output in a patient with a dilated cardiomyopathy, when it raises output in an athlete?

Answer

The cardiomyopathy patient's stroke volume is already impaired. Tachycardia shortens diastolic filling time, so SV drops further and outweighs the gain in rate. The athlete's compliant ventricle keeps filling and stretching, so CO = SV × HR rises.
