⭐ Diastolic rumble + opening snap at the apex

Slides 20–21 + recording · the mitral apparatus · rheumatic disease · the LA–LV diastolic gradient

Mitral Stenosis

AbbreviationsMS = mitral stenosis · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LAP / LVP = left atrial / left ventricular pressure · LVEDP = LV end-diastolic pressure · Ao = aorta · AF = atrial fibrillation · OS = opening snap · A2 = aortic part of S2 · PBMC = percutaneous balloon mitral commissurotomy (balloon valvuloplasty) · TEE = transesophageal echocardiography

The mitral apparatus first (recording)

  • Two leaflets, anterior and posterior. The anterior one looks bigger and the posterior is crescent-shaped, but their surface areas are about the same.
  • Chordae tendineae tie the leaflets to two papillary muscles (anterolateral and posteromedial). Chordae from each papillary muscle go to both leaflets.
  • The LV itself is part of the apparatus: LV contraction pulls on the papillary muscles and chordae to close the valve. This is why LV disease can cause MR (Topic 8).

What it is and who gets it

  • Restricted opening of the mitral orifice. The lecturer's example: a normal valve opens to about 4 cm², and in MS the orifice is smaller.
  • Most common cause worldwide: rheumatic heart disease. It's everywhere in South Asia (India, Pakistan), where balloon valvuloplasty is the bread-and-butter procedure, but rare in the US, where a few specialists do it.

🔑 Hallmark: the diastolic LA–LV gradient

LA pressure stays above LV diastolic pressure all through diastole (the grey shaded area on the tracing). Normally, once the mitral valve opens, flow is laminar and the two pressures equalize (the lines sit on top of each other, gradient 0–5 mm Hg). In MS, flow is turbulent and the gradient persists.

  • The c wave may fade as the disease progresses and the leaflets stiffen (it's still there while the leaflets are mobile).
  • Today the diagnosis and its severity come from echocardiography (including 3D), which is more accurate than the old cath-lab tracings.

On exam

🩺 Buzzwords she said will be on boards

A diastolic, rumbling murmur heard best at the apex, always with an opening snap. The vignette: a patient who grew up where rheumatic fever is common (e.g. recently emigrated from India), with a childhood streptococcal infection. Think MS immediately.

Board details Board add-on

  • Severity: severe MS = valve area ≤ 1.5 cm². The shorter the A2–to–opening-snap interval, the more severe (high LA pressure opens the valve earlier).
  • Presystolic accentuation of the rumble comes from atrial contraction, so it disappears in AF. UWorld describes S1 as usually soft (a stiff, calcified valve); older teaching gives a loud S1 while the leaflets are still pliable.
  • The big LA leads to AF and LA thrombus, so stroke can be the first sign. Anticoagulate with warfarin (preferred over direct oral anticoagulants in MS).
  • Back-pressure leads to pulmonary edema, hemoptysis, pulmonary hypertension and RV failure. Pregnancy (more blood volume) often unmasks it, and about 80% of rheumatic MS is in women.
  • Treatment: β-blockers (more diastolic filling time), diuretics, and PBMC for severe symptomatic disease.

Q: A 34-year-old woman who emigrated from Pakistan has exertional dyspnea and a low-pitched diastolic murmur at the apex preceded by a sharp early diastolic sound. What will the LA and LV pressure tracings show?

Answer

Rheumatic mitral stenosis with an opening snap. LA pressure stays above LV diastolic pressure throughout diastole (a persistent diastolic gradient). The LA is high and enlarged, and LV diastolic pressure is low.

Q: Why are LA and LV pressures nearly identical during diastole in a normal heart?

Answer

A wide-open mitral valve allows laminar, unobstructed flow, so pressure equalizes across it (gradient ≈ 0–5 mm Hg).

Aortic Stenosis

AbbreviationsAS = aortic stenosis · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LVH = left ventricular hypertrophy · Ao = aorta · CT = computed tomography · TAVR = transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVI = implantation) · SAVR = surgical aortic valve replacement · PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention · RUSB = right upper sternal border (2nd intercostal space) · LVEF = LV ejection fraction · S2 / S4 = heart sounds

Who gets it: a bimodal disease

🧬

40s–60s: bicuspid aortic valve

Born with a congenitally abnormal valve. AS is the most common lesion of a bicuspid valve (AR and mixed disease also occur). A bicuspid valve always comes with an aortopathy (abnormal aortic connective tissue), so screen for an ascending aortic aneurysm and coarctation, e.g. with a contrast CT of the chest.

🦴

70s–90s: calcific (age-related)

A normal three-leaflet valve calcifies with age. Catherine Otto's pathology work found inflammatory cells and actual lamellar bone in the leaflets. Statins did not slow progression. Treatment is mechanical: replace the valve.

🔑 Hallmark: the systolic LV–aortic gradient

LV systolic pressure is far above aortic systolic pressure. The peaks split apart (grey shading), whereas on the normal Wiggers diagram they sit on top of each other.

🎙️ She repeated this: LA pressure is ALWAYS high in AS

"Take my word on this." The cause isn't a problem at the mitral valve. It's the hypertrophied, non-compliant LV, "the epitome of diastolic dysfunction," which can't accept a normal volume. That is a different mechanism from MS, where the obstruction sits right at the LA outlet.

Why the patient gets symptoms

  • Compensation is concentric LVH. Myocardial hypertrophy is never normal (unlike skeletal muscle): the wall is thick and stiff. Afterload rises (with peripheral vasoconstriction), preload rises, and eventually the ventricle falls off the Starling curve into heart failure.
  • Exercise intolerance comes first ("I get really exhausted walking up that hill"), because CO can't rise with exercise.
  • Angina from a supply–demand mismatch: low CO, plus a thick muscle with high O₂ demand.
  • Exertional syncope: CO can't rise to meet exercise.
  • Congestive heart failure at the end.

On exam

🩺 Buzzwords

A systolic crescendo–decrescendo murmur heard best at the right 2nd intercostal space, with blunted (weak, delayed) carotid upstrokes. Pair it with a young patient with a bicuspid valve or an 85-year-old with new exercise intolerance.

Severity clues: as AS worsens, the murmur's peak moves later (more crescendo) and can muffle S2. A crisp S2 makes severe AS less likely. A paradoxically split S2 can occur (delayed aortic closure) but often doesn't.

⚠️ Pulse pressure in AS: recording vs UWorld

A student asked whether AS gives a narrow pulse pressure. Dr. Qazi: not necessarily. It may be wide, normal or narrow, and it is "not the defining characteristic" (unlike AR, where a wide pulse pressure is the hallmark). UWorld lists a narrow pulse pressure with pulsus parvus et tardus as the expected finding in severe AS. For boards: parvus et tardus + late-peaking murmur + soft S2 are the reliable signs, and a narrow pulse pressure supports them.

Treatment (recording)

  • No medical therapy slows it, so it must be monitored and then replaced. The lecturer's practice is to intervene once it reaches severe on echo, rather than waiting for symptoms.
  • TAVR is now first-line, mostly transfemoral, and "kind of like having a PCI." Surgical replacement (SAVR) means at least 5 days in hospital and a month or more for the sternotomy to heal.

Board details Board add-on

  • Severe AS on echo: jet ≥ 4 m/s, mean gradient ≥ 40 mm Hg, valve area ≤ 1.0 cm².
  • Guideline triggers for valve replacement: severe AS with any symptom, or severe AS with LVEF < 50%.
  • The murmur radiates to the carotids, and an S4 is common.
  • Severe AS is preload-dependent: avoid nitrates and over-diuresis.
  • Symptomatic severe AS untreated: mean survival about 1–3 years.
  • Heyde syndrome: AS plus GI bleeding from angiodysplasia, because the shear stress across the valve cleaves von Willebrand factor.

Q: An 84-year-old has new exertional fatigue and a harsh systolic crescendo–decrescendo murmur at the right 2nd intercostal space with weak, delayed carotid pulses. What does simultaneous LV and aortic pressure recording show?

Answer

Calcific aortic stenosis: a large systolic gradient, with LV systolic pressure far above aortic systolic pressure. LA pressure is also high, because of the stiff LV.

Q: A 45-year-old is found to have AS from a bicuspid valve. Which two aortic problems should you screen for?

Answer

Ascending aortic aneurysm and coarctation of the aorta, because of the associated aortopathy.

Q: Why does angina occur in AS even with normal coronary arteries?

Answer

Supply–demand mismatch: the hypertrophied LV has a high oxygen demand while the fixed obstruction limits cardiac output (supply), especially with exercise.

⭐ "Tall v wave" = mitral regurgitation

Slides 24–25 + recording · LA filled from two sources · primary (prolapse) vs secondary (functional) MR · click and late systolic murmur

Mitral Regurgitation & Prolapse

AbbreviationsMR = mitral regurgitation · MVP = mitral valve prolapse · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LVEDP = LV end-diastolic pressure · MI = myocardial infarction · PMI = point of maximal impulse · AF = atrial fibrillation · S1 / S3 = heart sounds
  • Blood leaks back into the LA through a mitral valve that should be closed, so it happens in systole. It's very common: after AS, the second most common lesion in her clinic.
  • The LA now fills from two sources: normal pulmonary venous return plus the regurgitant jet. LA volume and pressure rise, and the LA enlarges.
  • That extra LA volume goes into the LV in diastole, making MR a high-preload state: LVEDP rises and the LV dilates (eccentric hypertrophy).

🔑 Hallmark: a tall v wave

"That's what's gonna be on the exam." The v wave is the LA filling phase, and in MR it's swollen by the regurgitant volume. If a vignette mentions a tall v wave on a hemodynamic tracing, think significant MR, which comes with a high LV end-diastolic pressure.

🧠 Big LA in MS vs MR

Both enlarge the LA. In MS it's mainly a pressure problem from the obstruction. In MR it's mainly a volume problem from two inflows.

Primary vs secondary MR

🎈

Primary: the valve itself (MVP)

Most common cause of MR in the US: mitral valve prolapse. The leaflets are myxomatous: long, floppy, infiltrated with proteoglycans. A long leaflet billows up into the LA and leaves a gap. Tension stretches the chordae, so it worsens over time. If a chord ruptures, the leaflet becomes flail, causing a huge gap, acute heart failure, and surgery before discharge.

🫀

Secondary (functional): the LV

The valve is normal; the ventricle is not. A dilated cardiomyopathy stretches the annulus and pulls on the papillary muscles, causing a central leak. An inferior MI damages the wall under the posteromedial papillary muscle, which tethers the posterior leaflet down (the opposite of prolapse); this is ischemic MR.

  • The distinction matters because treatment differs. Primary MR is a valve problem: surgical mitral valve repair is the standard.
  • Repair is highly specialized. Send the patient to a surgeon with about 95% odds of repair rather than replacement. A good repair lasts a lifetime, while a prosthetic valve has a limited lifespan and commits the patient to another procedure.

On exam

🩺 Buzzwords

A systolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla (and the back). Functional MR is holosystolic, the same pitch throughout. MVP: a mid-systolic click followed by a late systolic murmur. The click is the prolapsing leaflet and chordae snapping taut. As with any valve lesion, the first symptom is usually exercise intolerance, and later heart failure.

Board details Board add-on

  • Chronic severe MR: S3, a soft S1, a laterally displaced PMI, and frequent AF.
  • Acute MR (papillary muscle rupture after an inferior MI, chordal rupture, endocarditis): pulmonary edema and shock, with a murmur that may be soft or absent because LA pressure rises fast and equalizes with the LV.
  • MVP maneuvers: anything that shrinks the LV (standing, Valsalva) makes the click earlier and the murmur longer. Squatting and handgrip do the opposite. MVP is associated with Marfan and Ehlers–Danlos syndromes.
  • Handgrip (↑ afterload) increases the MR murmur.

Q: A pulmonary capillary wedge (LA) tracing shows a very tall v wave. Which lesion, and why?

Answer

Mitral regurgitation. During systole the LA fills from the pulmonary veins and the regurgitant jet, so the filling wave (v) towers.

Q: A thin 30-year-old woman has a mid-systolic click followed by a late systolic murmur at the apex. What is the valve pathology?

Answer

Mitral valve prolapse from myxomatous degeneration (elongated, floppy leaflets), the most common cause of primary MR in the US.

Q: Three days after an inferior MI a patient develops new MR. Which papillary muscle is involved, and is this primary or secondary MR?

Answer

The posteromedial papillary muscle (supplied by the inferior wall). Dysfunction is secondary (ischemic, functional) MR; outright rupture causes acute MR and needs emergency surgery.

Aortic Regurgitation

AbbreviationsAR = aortic regurgitation · AS = aortic stenosis · LA / LV = left atrium / ventricle · LVEDP = LV end-diastolic pressure · Ao = aorta · LSB = left sternal border · RUSB = right upper sternal border · PMI = point of maximal impulse · S3 = third heart sound

Causes

  • Less common than AS or MR. In the lecturer's practice the commonest setting is bacterial endocarditis.
  • Bicuspid aortic valve: mostly AS, but many have mixed disease and some have pure AR.
  • Marfan syndrome (a connective-tissue disorder): AR, MVP, and ascending aortic aneurysm.
  • Board add-on UWorld: the commonest cause in resource-rich countries is a bicuspid valve, and rheumatic disease in resource-limited ones. Aortic root dilation (Marfan, Ehlers–Danlos, ankylosing spondylitis, giant cell or Takayasu arteritis) is another route. Acute AR comes from aortic dissection or endocarditis.

🔑 Hallmark: a widened pulse pressure

Systolic pressure high, diastolic pressure low. LV and aortic systolic peaks are superimposed (no obstruction, unlike AS). The difference shows in diastole, where aortic pressure falls away.

Remodeling: stenosis vs regurgitation (her recap)

🧱

AS: pressure overload

A fixed obstruction makes the LV work hard, causing concentric hypertrophy: a thick wall, non-compliant, with very high intraventricular pressure.

🎈

AR (and MR): volume overload

Too much volume sits in the LV, causing LV dilation with eccentric hypertrophy. Wall thickness may look normal, but the myocytes are still abnormal. MR adds LA enlargement on top.

Her rule: if the wall thickens, it's concentric; if the chamber enlarges, it's eccentric.

On exam

🩺 Buzzwords

A diastolic murmur plus bounding ("water-hammer") pulses. The pulse bounds because of the forceful ejection followed by rapid runoff.

Board details Board add-on

  • The murmur is early diastolic, decrescendo, high-pitched and blowing. Valvular AR is heard best at the left sternal border (sitting forward, end-expiration); root dilation at the RUSB.
  • Austin Flint murmur: an apical diastolic rumble in severe AR (the jet hits the anterior mitral leaflet), which mimics MS.
  • Hyperdynamic signs: Corrigan (carotid "water-hammer"), de Musset (head bobbing), Quincke (nail-bed pulsations), Traube (pistol-shot femorals), Duroziez (femoral to-and-fro bruit), Müller (uvula pulsation).
  • Also: S3, a displaced PMI, and angina (low diastolic pressure, so poor coronary perfusion; often nocturnal with bradycardia).
  • Acute AR (dissection, endocarditis): sudden pulmonary edema and shock, with a soft or short murmur and no time for a wide pulse pressure to develop.

Q: A tall 25-year-old with long fingers has BP 160/50, bounding pulses and a diastolic murmur. Explain the low diastolic pressure.

Answer

Aortic regurgitation (Marfan, aortic root). In diastole the aorta loses blood two ways: backward into the LV and forward into the systemic runoff. Diastolic pressure therefore falls, while the large stroke volume raises systolic pressure, giving a wide pulse pressure.

Q: Contrast the LV remodeling in aortic stenosis and aortic regurgitation.

Answer

AS is pressure overload, causing concentric hypertrophy (thick, stiff wall). AR is volume overload, causing dilation with eccentric hypertrophy.
