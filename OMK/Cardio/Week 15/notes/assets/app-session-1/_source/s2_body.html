⭐ Rule out the serious things before you call it anxiety

Dyspnea & Edema: Building the Differential

AbbreviationsDM = diabetes mellitus · A1C = hemoglobin A1c · HTN = hypertension · DOE = dyspnea on exertion · PND = paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea · NKDA = no known drug allergies · ACEi = ACE inhibitor · DHP CCB = dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker · ALS = amyotrophic lateral sclerosis · CHF = congestive heart failure
Case 1: 60-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes (A1C 8%, no retinopathy), HTN, hypercholesterolemia and obesity. 3 weeks of progressive DOE, orthopnea and bilateral ankle swelling. No chest pain or radiation, palpitations, PND, syncope or productive cough. No angina, MI or valve disease. Poor diet, sedentary. Meds: lisinopril 40 (ACEi), amlodipine 2.5 (DHP CCB → vasodilation), metformin 850 BID, atorvastatin 20 ("often forgets").

Dyspnea: put it in buckets

Cardiac

ACS/CAD, CHF, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, valve disease, myocardial or pericardial disease

Pulmonary

COPD, asthma, pneumonia, PE, ILD…

Anemia

"What was our last system before cardiology? Heme-Onc."

Neuromuscular

A weak diaphragm (muscular dystrophies, ALS) makes breathing hard

Anxiety / panic

A real cause, but rule out the serious things first (arrhythmia, thyroid) before you label it anxiety

🎙️ From the recording: the third-year warning

"This is something that you're going to have to do on the 3rd year. If you ask Dr. Qazi to do a rotation with her, she's going to pimp you on things like this." Also from the CSA: students listed anxiety first for worsening palpitations. Always exclude arrhythmia and thyroid disease first.

Edema: think physiology, not organs

  • ↑ Capillary hydrostatic pressure: most commonly heart failure (also venous insufficiency, medications such as DHP CCBs).
  • ↓ Plasma oncotic pressure: cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing enteropathy or malnutrition.
  • Also lymphedema. "Pitting edema is nothing more than salt and water trapped in the interstitium."

Q: A patient has bilateral pitting ankle edema, a serum albumin of 1.9 g/dL and 4+ proteinuria, with normal JVP and no orthopnea. Which Starling force is deranged?

Answer

↓ Plasma oncotic pressure (nephrotic syndrome), not ↑ capillary hydrostatic pressure as in heart failure.

Exam, Labs & Natriuretic Peptides

AbbreviationsJVP = jugular venous pressure · S3 / S4 = third / fourth heart sounds · CBC = complete blood count · CMP = comprehensive metabolic panel · TSH = thyroid-stimulating hormone · hs-cTnT = high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T · BNP = B-type natriuretic peptide · NT-proBNP = N-terminal pro-BNP · NPV / PPV = negative / positive predictive value · CKD = chronic kidney disease · ARNI = angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor · OSA = obstructive sleep apnea
Exam: BP 150/86, HR 90 regular, RR 18, SpO₂ 95%, BMI 33. Slow to get out of the chair, mildly breathless. Mallampati II. JVP 7 cm (2 cm above the sternal angle + 5) at 30°. Bibasilar crackles ¼ up. Regular, no murmur, +S4, no S3. No hepatomegaly or ascites. 1+ bilateral pitting edema to mid-tibia.

  • Mallampati grades intubation difficulty and doubles as an OSA screen.
  • JVP: height above the sternal angle + 5 cm (sternal angle to right atrium).
  • S4 = non-compliant ventricle (atrium pushing into a stiff LV). S3: Dr. Qazi says it is physiologic in the young (very compliant LV), but in her patients it is a marker of volume overload.

Q: A patient with HFrEF was switched from enalapril to sacubitril/valsartan, and his BNP rose although he feels better. Which test should you follow instead, and why?

Answer

NT-proBNP. Sacubitril inhibits neprilysin, which normally degrades BNP, so BNP rises as a drug effect. NT-proBNP is an inert fragment that neprilysin doesn't clear.

Q: Which natriuretic peptide has the longer half-life?

Answer

NT-proBNP (1–2 hours vs about 20 minutes for BNP).

ECG, Echo & the H₂FPEF Score

AbbreviationsLVH = left ventricular hypertrophy · LAD = left axis deviation · AVB = atrioventricular block · LBBB = left bundle branch block · PSAX = parasternal short-axis view · LVM / RWT = LV mass / relative wall thickness · EF = ejection fraction · PASP = pulmonary artery systolic pressure · LA = left atrium · E/e′ = mitral inflow to annular velocity ratio (filling pressure) · AF = atrial fibrillation · CXR = chest X-ray · TTE = transthoracic echo

Additional studies: CXR (PA and lateral), 12-lead ECG (ischemia, LVH) and TTE (chamber size and function).

Her ECG

  • Rate 60, sinus. Axis upright in I, down in aVF → left axis deviation (about −60°). PR borderline → first-degree AV block.
  • LVH voltage: Dr. Segal's "11 and 35": R in aVL > 11 mm (hers ~13–14), or S in V1/V2 + R in V5/V6 ≥ 35 mm (19 + 23 = 42). Only valid in adults; Dr. Qazi says the criteria were validated at age ≥ 40, and young people often have high voltage.
  • LV strain pattern in I, aVL, V5–V6; no ischemic changes. No R-wave progression: the precordial leads stay negative with no transition.
  • Dr. Qazi's read: the QRS is > 120 ms and the tracing meets most criteria for LBBB; what's missing is the notched R wave in I, aVL and V6.
  • Rhythm tip: look for P waves in lead II (upright) and V1 (often biphasic: right then left atrial). A negative P in aVR confirms correct limb-lead placement.

The diagnosis

🔑 Congestive HF, subtype HFpEF ("diastolic HF")

Pathophysiology: impaired relaxation (a filling problem) in a concentrically hypertrophied ventricle. Etiologies: ischemic (always exclude), valvular, hypertensive (HFpEF early, HFrEF late; the most common route, especially with diabetes, metabolic dysfunction and aging), primary cardiomyopathy, storage diseases (amyloid), acquired (tachycardia, medications), congenital heart disease.

Q: A 67-year-old woman with obesity (BMI 34), HTN on three drugs, paroxysmal AF, PASP 40 and E/e′ 12 has dyspnea and an EF of 60%. What is her H₂FPEF score?

Answer

2 + 1 + 3 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 9: HFpEF is near-certain.

Q: An echo report says "grade 1 diastolic dysfunction" in an 82-year-old. Is the LA pressure likely elevated?

Answer

No. In grade 1, LA pressure is usually normal, and the pattern is normal for that age. Grade 2 (pseudonormal) implies elevated LA pressure.

HFrEF vs HFpEF phenotypes

HFrEF

Typically a younger man after MI. AF, CAD, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, HTN, lipids, prior MI. Eccentric hypertrophy, ↑ LV mass, rounded heart. Outcomes: CV mortality, HF hospitalization.

HFpEF

Typically an older woman. AF, CAD, CKD, diabetes, HTN, obesity. Concentric remodeling, ↑ LA size, ↑ filling pressure. Outcomes: more often non-CV death. Our patient's HTN, T2DM and obesity all raise HFpEF risk.

Q: A hemodialysis patient with a large upper-arm AV fistula has dyspnea, edema, a wide pulse pressure and an elevated cardiac output with low SVR. Which HF category?

Answer

High-output heart failure (↑ metabolic/shunt demand: ↑ CO, ↓ SVR).

Q: An EF that was 30% is now 46% on therapy. How is this HF classified?

Answer

HFimpEF (HF with improved EF): previous ≤ 40%, now > 40%. Continue GDMT.

HF Case 1 · Topic 5
⭐ SGLT2 inhibitors: the first drug with real HFpEF benefit

HF slides 19–23 + recording · diet and exercise · loop and thiazide diuretics (and the braking phenomenon) · SGLT2 inhibitors · GLP-1 · MRAs

Treating HFpEF: Lifestyle, Diuretics, SGLT2i, MRA

AbbreviationsDASH = Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension · HRmax = maximal heart rate · VO₂peak = peak oxygen uptake · OSA = obstructive sleep apnea · NKCC2 = Na⁺–K⁺–2Cl⁻ cotransporter · TAL = thick ascending limb · DCT = distal convoluted tubule · NCC = Na⁺–Cl⁻ cotransporter · ENaC = epithelial Na⁺ channel · SGLT2i = sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitor · TGF = tubuloglomerular feedback · GLP-1 = glucagon-like peptide-1 (GIP = glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide) · MRA = mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist · T1DM = type 1 diabetes · UTI = urinary tract infection · eGFR = estimated glomerular filtration rate · ARNI = angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor

Lifestyle

  • Diet: DASH (Na⁺ 2,300 mg, K⁺ 4,700 mg, fruit, vegetables, lean protein, low-fat dairy) and Mediterranean.
  • Fluid restriction: none unless hyponatremic. Dr. Qazi: patient-specific; she restricts fluids only in patients "in and out of the hospital a lot."
  • Exercise: start low, go slow → cardiac rehab. 30 min of moderate walking (60–70% HRmax) → 16% ↓ all-cause and CV mortality; exercise ↑ VO₂peak and improves diastolic dysfunction.
  • No substance use, limited alcohol, weight management (BMI < 30), treat OSA.

Drugs for HFpEF

Everyone

Diuretic: loop ± thiazide (thiazide-like) as add-on, for congestion. SGLT2 inhibitor (empagliflozin or dapagliflozin) unless contraindicated: pregnancy, T1DM, volume depletion or symptomatic hypotension, amputation risk, complicated UTI or genitourinary infection.

Some

GLP-1 (± GIP) agonist if BMI ≥ 30 (semaglutide, tirzepatide): weight loss, A1C to goal. MRA if K⁺ ≤ 4.7 and eGFR ≥ 30: ↓ myocardial fibrosis.

Not first-line in HFpEF

ACEi, ARB and sacubitril/valsartan are not primary HFpEF treatment (the algorithm reserves ARNI and MRA for selected patients with EF 50–60%).

{{F:hfpef-algorithm}}

Diuretics: Dr. Scully's pharmacology

  1. Why loop first? The thick ascending limb reabsorbs ~20–30% of the filtered Na⁺ load. Blocking NKCC2 early in the nephron gets "a good bang for your buck" when you need fluid off quickly.
  2. Why not loop alone, long-term? Tolerance (the braking phenomenon): the kidney is smart, and the distal convoluted tubule hypertrophies and grabs more Na⁺ (e.g. 10–15% → 20–25%). Furosemide (Lasix: "lasts six" hours) causes natriuresis for ~6 h, then the kidney reabsorbs avidly for the next 18 h.
  3. Add a thiazide (NCC in the DCT) for sequential nephron blockade: the Na⁺ escaping the loop gets blocked downstream.
  4. Why both waste K⁺: more Na⁺ reaches the principal cell → ENaC takes up Na⁺, leaving the lumen negative → K⁺ (via ROMK) and H⁺ are secreted → hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis. Loops also lose Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ (lost lumen-positive potential).
{{F:diuretic-sites}}{{F:loop-diuretic}}

SGLT2 inhibitors

  • Their HF benefit has little to do with glucose: they lower A1C by only ~0.5% and lose glycemic effect as GFR falls. They are natriuretic and diuretic (free water and Na⁺).
  • ↑ Na⁺/Cl⁻ delivery to the macula densa → tubuloglomerular feedback → afferent arteriolar constriction → ↓ intraglomerular pressure (renal and CV protection).
  • Dr. Qazi: for HFpEF the only class I drugs for decades were loop diuretics; ACEi and ARB trials "didn't pan out" until the SGLT2 inhibitor trials. They are now a mainstay in HFrEF too.
  • Genital and urinary infections: glucose in the urine is "like sending bacteria to Six Flags." Dr. Segal treats through an uncomplicated UTI and continues the drug.
{{F:sglt2i-mechanism}}

GLP-1 and MRAs

  • GLP-1 agonists: weight loss "can be really, really powerful" and lets patients exercise. Board add-on Semaglutide (STEP-HFpEF) improved symptoms and function in obese HFpEF.
  • Steroidal MRAs: proven in HFrEF. Spironolactone is long-acting and causes gynecomastia (poorly tolerated in men above ~50 mg). Eplerenone doesn't, but needs twice-daily dosing and costs more.
  • Non-steroidal MRA: finerenone: proven in albuminuric CKD with T2D, rare sexual side effects, less BP lowering, and now entering HFpEF. With every MRA, potassium is the danger.
{{F:mra-mechanisms}}

Q: A patient on furosemide 40 mg daily has stopped losing weight despite good adherence. What renal adaptation explains this, and what do you add?

Answer

The braking phenomenon: distal convoluted tubule hypertrophy increases distal Na⁺ reabsorption (plus post-diuretic Na⁺ retention). Add a thiazide (sequential nephron blockade).

Q: A patient on furosemide plus metolazone has K⁺ 2.9 and HCO₃⁻ 33. Explain.

Answer

↑ Distal Na⁺ delivery → ENaC reabsorption in the principal cell → lumen-negative → K⁺ and H⁺ secretion → hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis (plus contraction and aldosterone activation).

Q: Through which renal mechanism do SGLT2 inhibitors lower intraglomerular pressure?

Answer

↑ Na⁺/Cl⁻ at the macula densa → tubuloglomerular feedback → afferent arteriolar constriction.

The Patient, Alcohol & Cocaine

AbbreviationsED = emergency department · CAD = coronary artery disease · PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention · LAD = left anterior descending · DES = drug-eluting stent · EF = ejection fraction · AFib = atrial fibrillation · DOE = dyspnea on exertion · DOAC = direct oral anticoagulant · JVP = jugular venous pressure · HJR = hepatojugular reflux · S3 = third heart sound · ETOH = alcohol · IDU = injection drug use · NET = norepinephrine transporter · SVR = systemic vascular resistance · SNS / RAAS = sympathetic / renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system
Case 2: 70-year-old man in the ED. CAD s/p PCI to mid-LAD (DES, 2 years ago), HFrEF (EF 35–40%), HTN, persistent AFib. 4 weeks of progressive DOE, now < 1 block. No chest pain. Poor diet, 3–4 beers daily for years, crack cocaine recently. Meds: furosemide 40, metoprolol succinate, apixaban 5 BID, atorvastatin 80. Exam: BP 110/70, HR 84, RR 20, SpO₂ 90%, BMI 30. Accessory muscles, diaphoretic, speaks 1–2 words, tired. JVP 12 cm with +HJR, crackles ½ up, irregular rhythm, loud S3, 3+ edema to the distal thigh and sacrum (anasarca).
  • Metoprolol succinate is the long-acting, once-daily form; tartrate is short-acting, usually twice daily.
  • Diagnosis: decompensated congestive HF, HFrEF ("pump problem"): impaired contraction.
  • Etiologies to consider: ischemic (always exclude) · valvular · primary cardiomyopathy · secondary: alcohol (also thiamine deficiency → "wet" beriberi), cocaine, iron overload · infective endocarditis (IDU?) · myocarditis.
  • "Tired appearing (why?)": think hypoperfusion and hypercapnia from respiratory fatigue.

How alcohol and cocaine hurt the heart

🍺

Alcohol → dilated cardiomyopathy

Indirect: ↑ SNS and RAAS activity, endothelial dysfunction → HTN. Direct: oxidative stress, cardiac fibrosis, impaired excitation–contraction coupling → ↓ EF.

❄️

Cocaine

Blocks catecholamine reuptake (NET) → norepinephrine lingers on α₁ (vasoconstriction) and β₁. Also a Na⁺ channel blocker (local anesthetic, which is why ENT uses it for nasal surgery: vasoconstriction + anesthesia). Cardiotoxicity: Na⁺ channel blockade, Ca²⁺ handling disruption, inflammation and fibrosis.

🧠 Dr. Scully: look at the drug list

He takes metoprolol, a β₁-selective blocker. With cocaine on board, β₁ effects (heart rate, inotropy) are blunted, but α-mediated vasoconstriction is unopposed → ↑ peripheral vascular resistance = ↑ afterload, "the one thing you don't want to increase in heart failure." Carvedilol and labetalol block both α and β. And cocaine + alcohol together form cocaethylene. Board add-on It's more cardiotoxic and longer-acting than cocaine alone.

Q: A patient on metoprolol uses cocaine and becomes markedly hypertensive with worsening pulmonary edema. Which receptor effect is responsible?

Answer

Unopposed α₁-adrenergic vasoconstriction (↑ SVR/afterload): the β₁-selective blocker leaves α-receptors free while cocaine floods the synapse with norepinephrine.

Q: A malnourished man with chronic heavy drinking has high-output HF with peripheral edema and neuropathy. Which vitamin deficiency?

Answer

Thiamine (B₁): wet beriberi.

Pathophysiology informs management

↓ CO → sympathetic and RAAS activation → vasoconstriction (↑ afterload) and Na⁺/water retention (↑ preload) → more cardiac strain and remodeling, a vicious cycle. Each GDMT pillar breaks a link in it.

{{F:neurohormonal}}{{F:adhf-cycle}}

Initial therapy

  • Volume management: IV loop ± thiazide (some use a continuous infusion; DOSE trial), strict intake/output, daily morning weights, Na⁺ restriction, close monitoring of electrolytes (Mg²⁺) and kidney function.
  • Dr. Qazi: "Get the patient back on the Starling curve." Reduce preload first (diurese); once breathing and O₂ needs improve, work on afterload.
  • Most GDMT can't be started while decompensated. Never start a β-blocker in decompensated HFrEF (negative inotropy). Short-acting captopril (TID) is sometimes used for early afterload reduction.

Inotropes and the Starling curve

An inotrope ↑ contractility → ↑ CO and perfusion. Cold–dry: fluids if tolerated, often inotropes. Cold–wet (low CO, cardiogenic shock): inotropes plus diuretics, vasodilators, ultrafiltration. Venous return sets where on a curve the heart sits; afterload and inotropy set which curve. ↓ afterload + ↑ inotropy → curve up and to the left; ↑ afterload + ↓ inotropy → down and to the right.

{{F:starling-curves}}{{F:shock-types}}

Q: A patient with HFrEF has cool, mottled extremities, confusion, rising creatinine, JVP 16 cm and crackles. Profile and setting?

Answer

Wet and cold (cardiogenic shock) → ICU for inotropes plus decongestion (diuretics, vasodilators, ultrafiltration).

Q: On admission for wet–warm ADHF, should metoprolol be newly started?

Answer

No. Diurese first. β-blockers are started once the patient is compensated (near euvolemic) because of their negative inotropy. A patient already on a β-blocker who is warm is usually continued.

Q: His troponin is mildly elevated and flat on repeat. What kind of myocardial injury is this most likely?

Answer

Secondary (type 2, supply–demand) injury/MI from decompensated HF, hypoxemia and cocaine, not plaque rupture.

🎙️ From the recording: how much does it matter?

Dr. Segal: on all four pillars, morbidity and mortality fall by ~64%, and patients live on average ~5 years longer. The summary slide (Greene, 2024) is even stronger: RRR 73%, ARR 26%, NNT 3.9, median survival extended 7–11 years.

Pearls for each pillar

  • GDMT is individualized.
  • β-blockers: start only in compensated HF (approaching euvolemia). Use the evidence-based three: metoprolol succinate, carvedilol (β₁, β₂ + α₁), bisoprolol.
  • ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan) preferred over ACEi, and ACEi over ARB. Neprilysin breaks down natriuretic peptides, so sacubitril prolongs BNP's natriuretic effect (and raises measured BNP). Hypotension (SBP ↓ 5–7) and cost can limit it; Dr. Qazi notes Entresto is now generic.
  • SGLT2i: re-evaluate diuretic doses to avoid hypovolemia.
  • MRA: watch SCr and K⁺ closely, especially alongside ARNI and SGLT2i.
  • Others: ivabradine (blocks the HCN "funny" current, If, in the SA node; ↓ HR without changing contractility or BP) when HR stays high on a maximally tolerated BB · digoxin (cardiac glycoside) · vericiguat.
⚠️ Slide error: vericiguat

Slide 38 calls vericiguat a "soluble guanylate cyclase inhibitor." It is a soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator: it boosts cGMP and restores the NO–sGC–cGMP pathway, as the slide's own "restores NO pathway" implies.

Getting patients on all four

  1. Diurese first.
  2. Add afterload reduction, typically by starting an ARNI.
  3. Add a β-blocker once compensated (catecholamine excess drives chronic HFrEF).
  4. Add an SGLT2 inhibitor (and the MRA), at low doses, before up-titrating any single drug.
  5. Start in hospital: Dr. Qazi says follow-up is too unreliable to wait. Teach with a nurse educator and the family member who is the main support. Follow up within 7–10 days of discharge, then titrate within 4–6 weeks (labs: kidney function, K⁺, NT-proBNP).

🔑 The four HF questions

Is this HF, and what type? Is the patient congested? Is perfusion adequate? Which treatments improve symptoms now and outcomes later?

Q: Which β-blockers have mortality evidence in HFrEF?

Answer

Metoprolol succinate, carvedilol and bisoprolol (not metoprolol tartrate or atenolol).

Q: A patient with HFrEF on maximally tolerated metoprolol succinate, sacubitril/valsartan, spironolactone and dapagliflozin is in sinus rhythm at 82/min. Which drug lowers HR without lowering contractility or BP, and how?

Answer

Ivabradine: blocks the HCN (funny, If) channel in the SA node, slowing diastolic depolarization.

Epidemiology, Risk & the "Statin" Question

AbbreviationsPAD = peripheral artery disease · CAD = coronary artery disease · LDL-C = low-density lipoprotein cholesterol · Lp(a) = lipoprotein(a) · DM = diabetes mellitus · OR = odds ratio · FHx = family history
  • ~236 million people worldwide; ~7% of US adults (8.5 million). Non-traumatic amputation rates are rising.
  • Age: one of the strongest risk factors. Sex: men = women.
  • Smoking: ↑ risk 2–3× (2–4× on the quiz slide), and it persists for ~30 years after quitting.

🎙️ From the recording: "the Energizer Bunny of vascular disease"

After quitting, coronary risk falls substantially by ~10 years, but PAD risk keeps going for ~30 years. "Smoking and diabetes, smoking and diabetes." There is no real genetic risk for PAD itself (family history is overshadowed by other risk factors), unlike varicose veins, which do run in families.

Could it be the statin?

Never dismiss the concern, but this is far more likely vascular claudication. Dr. Segal described an N-of-1 statin trial in patients who blamed statins for side effects (Board add-on SAMSON, NEJM 2020): months on the statin, months on an identical placebo, and months with no tablets. Symptoms were nearly as bad on placebo as on the statin, and lowest with no pills. That's the nocebo effect: expect a side effect and you're likely to feel one.

⚠️ Recording vs the trial

In the recording the statin arm "had the lowest incidence." In SAMSON, symptom scores were similar on statin and placebo (placebo produced ~90% of the statin's symptom burden), and lowest in the no-tablet months. The take-home is the same: most statin "side effects" are nocebo.

Q (Knowledge check 2): A 59-year-old woman with HTN, T2DM and hyperlipidemia. Which additional history item most increases her PAD risk: family history of PAD, female sex, smoking, or age < 60?

Answer

Smoking: 2–4× risk and modifiable. Family history is overshadowed by other factors; PAD is M = F; risk climbs from 60–70.

PAD Case · Topic 11
⭐ ALI: time = tissue

PAD slides 15–18 + recording · CLTI vs ALI · clinical subsets · dependent rubor · where symptoms localize · Leriche syndrome

CLTI, Acute Limb Ischemia & Leriche

AbbreviationsCLTI = chronic limb-threatening ischemia · ALI = acute limb ischemia · UFH = unfractionated heparin · SFA = superficial femoral artery · ED = erectile dysfunction · PAD = peripheral artery disease · DP / PT = dorsalis pedis / posterior tibial pulses
🦶

CLTI (chronic limb-threatening ischemia)

The severe form of PAD: ischemic rest pain, tissue loss, gangrene for ≥ 2 weeks, with objective hypoperfusion. Pain is worse with elevation (lying in bed) and better hanging the foot down (dependency → rubor). Dr. Segal: think of it as "the unstable angina of PAD".

🚨

ALI (acute limb ischemia)

Sudden severe hypoperfusion, < 2 weeks. 6 Ps: pain, pallor, pulselessness, poikilothermia (cold), paresthesias, paralysis (most severe: "the ballgame is over"). Often thromboembolic. Emergency: "time = tissue" → IV unfractionated heparin, emergent vascular surgery, careful sensory/motor exam, look for an embolic source.

{{F:pad-subsets}}
🎙️ From the recording: the island patient

On Swan's Island a woman treated the week before for "cellulitis" came back with "recurrent cellulitis" in the leg. She had no cellulitis: a cool foot, gangrene of the fourth toe and rest pain (CLTI), and she refused to leave the island despite an hour of persuasion. The clue was dependent rubor: pale white when raised, bright red when hanging down. Dependent rubor fades with elevation, whereas cellulitis doesn't change. Also: purple toes after catheter procedures (cholesterol emboli from the aorta), common in his dialysis patients.

{{F:dependent-rubor}}{{F:buerger-test}}

Where it hurts tells you where it's blocked

  • Buttock, hip, thigh → aortoiliac disease.
  • Upper two-thirds of the calf → superficial femoral artery (the most common site); lower third of the calf → popliteal.
  • Ankle and foot → tibial disease.
🔑 Leriche syndrome: "a great board question"

Aortoiliac occlusive disease (often bilateral) with a triad: buttock and hip claudication + ↓ or absent femoral pulses + erectile dysfunction. Dr. Segal: ED, especially in a young man, is a vascular sign ("not just low T, give them T, out the door"). The occlusion sits at the level of the kidneys, so these patients often have kidney disease too.

Answer

Aortoiliac occlusion: Leriche syndrome.

Q: A woman with AF stopped apixaban a week ago; she now has a cold, pale, pulseless leg with numbness for 3 hours. First step?

Answer

Acute limb ischemia (likely embolic): IV unfractionated heparin at diagnosis and emergent vascular surgery evaluation.

Exam, ABI & Waveforms

AbbreviationsABI = ankle–brachial index · TBI = toe–brachial index · DP / PT = dorsalis pedis / posterior tibial · PVR = pulse volume recording · CKD = chronic kidney disease · DM = diabetes · SV = stroke volume · CLTI = chronic limb-threatening ischemia · CTA / MRA = CT / MR angiography · TcPO₂ = transcutaneous oxygen pressure · SPP = skin perfusion pressure

The PAD exam

  • BP in both arms: the difference should be < 10 mm Hg; a larger gap suggests proximal (subclavian) disease.
  • Pulse pressure: wide (> 40) hints at stiff arteries. Dr. Segal: pulse pressure ≈ stroke volume ÷ arterial compliance, so a big SV (aortic regurgitation) or a stiff aorta widens it.
  • Skin (rash, ulcers, color, hair loss, nails) and temperature; elevation pallor and dependent rubor (rubor fades with elevation; cellulitis doesn't).
  • Heart, lungs and fundi; symmetric peripheral pulses; auscultate for bruits; sensory testing (10-g monofilament) and motor testing.

Ankle–brachial index

ABI = highest ankle pressure (DP or PT) on that side ÷ highest arm (brachial) pressure. Non-invasive and doable in the office.

Smoking, Natural History & Polyvascular Disease

Smoking cessation

  • Quitting improves survival and amputation-free survival in PAD.
  • Counseling (assess dependence, advise, follow up; phone calls, groups, programs) + pharmacotherapy: NRT (patch, lozenge, gum, inhaler, nasal spray), bupropion, varenicline (α4β2 nicotinic partial agonist). E-cigarettes: uncertain.

🔑 Key takeaways

PAD is common, under-recognized and deadly, often asymptomatic or misdiagnosed. Smoking is the strongest modifiable factor; diabetes drives severe, limb-threatening disease. Separate vascular (exertional, relieved by rest, ↓ pulses) from neurogenic (better with flexion, "grocery cart," uphill). First line: lifestyle and medical management: aggressive smoking cessation, supervised exercise (cilostazol may help claudication), statins, ACEi/ARB, antiplatelet therapy. Recognize ALI (6 Ps) and CLTI (ulcers, gangrene): time = tissue.

Q: Over 5 years, what is the more likely bad outcome in a patient with claudication: limb amputation, or MI/stroke/death?

MI, stroke or death (~20% MI/stroke, 15–30% death) far exceeds limb loss (CLI 1–2%). Treat PAD as high-risk ASCVD.

Q: What is varenicline's mechanism, and which cheaper European drug shares it?

Answer

Partial agonist at the α4β2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. Cytisine works the same way.
