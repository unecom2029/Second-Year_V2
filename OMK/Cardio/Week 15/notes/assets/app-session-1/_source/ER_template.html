Exercise Is Medicine

Most coronary disease comes from three habits: poor diet, physical inactivity and smoking. This lecture covers the physiology of exercise, the outcome data that make fitness as protective as cardiac drugs, cardiac rehabilitation (phases, indications, evidence, why it's underused), how to prescribe exercise, and the diets with proven benefit.

LO 1 · Topic 1
⭐ 75–90% of CAD comes from modifiable risk factors

Slides 4–5, 11 · global burden · inactivity as an independent risk factor · how exercise protects

Modifiable Risk & Why Exercise Protects

AbbreviationsCVD = cardiovascular disease · CAD = coronary artery disease · ASCVD = atherosclerotic CVD · CRP = C-reactive protein · LDL-C / HDL-C = low- / high-density lipoprotein cholesterol · NO = nitric oxide
  • CVD is the number one cause of death globally (~17.6 million deaths in 2016).
  • An estimated 75–90% of CAD incidence is explained by modifiable risk factors: poor diet, physical inactivity, cigarette smoking.
  • Physical inactivity is an independent risk factor for CVD and is associated with 12% of all US mortality.
  • Regular moderate-intensity activity: 25% lower all-cause mortality in CAD patients and 20–30% lower in the general population, yet most people still don't exercise enough.

How exercise protects (slide 11)

EffectMechanisms
Anti-inflammatory↓ CRP
Antithrombotic↓ platelet aggregation, ↑ fibrinolysis
AntiarrhythmicBetter autonomic function: ↑ vagal tone, ↓ sympathetic activity
AntiatherogenicBetter risk factors; better endothelial function from ↑ flow and shear stress; ↑ NO synthesis
Risk factors↓ total cholesterol, LDL-C and triglycerides · ↑ HDL-C · ↓ BP · ↑ insulin sensitivity · weight loss
Anti-ischemicBetter myocardial perfusion, raised ischemic threshold, ischemic preconditioning
Psychological↓ depression and stress, ↑ social support

Q: Which three modifiable factors explain most coronary disease, and roughly what share?

Answer

Poor diet, physical inactivity and smoking: about 75–90% of CAD incidence.

Q: Give the autonomic mechanism by which exercise is antiarrhythmic.

Answer

↑ Vagal tone and ↓ sympathetic activity (better cardiac autonomic function).

LO 2 · Topic 2
⭐ SBP rises 8–12 mm Hg per MET; DBP stays flat

Slides 6–10 · definitions · heart rate, stroke volume, oxygen extraction, blood pressure

Cardiorespiratory Response to Exercise

AbbreviationsHR = heart rate · SV = stroke volume · CO = cardiac output · VO₂max / VO₂peak = maximal / symptom-limited peak oxygen uptake · MET = metabolic equivalent · SaO₂ / SvO₂ = arterial / venous O₂ saturation · SBP / DBP = systolic / diastolic BP

Definitions (slide 6)

TermMeaning
Age-predicted maximal HR220 − age (SD about ±10–15 bpm)
Peak HRHR at symptom-limited peak performance
HR reservePeak HR − resting HR
VO₂max vs VO₂peakO₂ use at maximal exertion vs at symptom-limited peak; VO₂peak < VO₂max in most sedentary adults
METEnergy for basal homeostasis: resting VO₂ ÷ body weight; 1 MET ≈ 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min
MET-hoursIntensity × time × frequency, e.g. walking at 3 METs × 1 h × 3 days = 9 MET-h/week

What each variable does as work rises (slides 7–10)

Heart rate

Linear → plateau

Rises linearly with work, then plateaus within 2–3 min at a given submaximal work rate. Max ≈ 220 − age.

Stroke volume

Curvilinear → plateau early

Rises curvilinearly and reaches near-maximum at about 50% of VO₂max, then plateaus or rises only slightly. Beyond that, CO rises mainly through HR.

O₂ extraction

↑ 3-fold

Muscle O₂ uptake (SaO₂ − SvO₂) rises three-fold from rest to max. Blood flow redistributes: rest 15–20% to muscle (80–85% to viscera) → exercise 85–90% of CO to active muscle.

Blood pressure

SBP ↑, DBP flat

SBP rises linearly, ~8–12 mm Hg per MET, to max ~190 ± 25 (men) and ~170 ± 25 (women). DBP falls slightly or stays the same.

🧠 Putting it together Board add-on

VO₂ = CO × (arterial − venous O₂ content) (the Fick principle). Exercise raises both terms: CO (HR × SV) and extraction. DBP stays flat because arteriolar dilation in working muscle lowers total peripheral resistance. On an exercise test, a fall in SBP with increasing work (exercise hypotension) suggests severe ischemia or LV dysfunction; an exaggerated rise suggests future hypertension.

Q: During a treadmill test a healthy 50-year-old climbs from 3 to 9 METs. Predict the change in systolic and diastolic BP.

Answer

SBP rises about 8–12 mm Hg per MET (~50–70 mm Hg over 6 METs). DBP falls slightly or stays the same.

Q: At about what fraction of VO₂max does stroke volume plateau, and what then drives further increases in cardiac output?

Answer

About 50% of VO₂max; after that, cardiac output rises mainly through heart rate.

Q: A patient walks 3 METs for 1 hour, 3 days a week. MET-hours per week?

Answer

3 × 1 × 3 = 9 MET-h/week.

LO 4 · Topic 3
⭐ Each 1-MET gain ≈ the benefit of a cardiac drug

Slides 12–17 · fitness as a predictor · per-MET mortality · getting fit later still counts · the 2019 activity recommendations

Fitness, METs & Mortality

AbbreviationsMET = metabolic equivalent · CAD = coronary artery disease · MI = myocardial infarction · RR = relative risk · ACE = angiotensin-converting enzyme · CR = cardiac rehabilitation · COR / LOE = class of recommendation / level of evidence · ASCVD = atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease
  • Cardiorespiratory fitness is a powerful independent predictor of mortality in patients with CVD.
  • In CAD, each 1-MET increase in exercise capacity → 8–35% relative reduction in mortality, comparable to statins, β-blockers, low-dose aspirin and ACE inhibitors after MI.
  • Meta-analysis (Kodama, 33 studies, ~103,000 people): summary RR 0.87 per 1-MET higher capacity.
  • Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study (Blair, 9,777 men, two exams ~5 years apart): unfit → fit roughly halved mortality (~120 → ~68 per 10,000 man-years); fit–fit was lowest (~40). Getting fit later still counts.
  • "Exercise is medicine." The least-fit, least-active high-risk patients with CVD (bottom 20%, < 5 METs) should be referred to structured exercise-based CR.

2019 ACC/AHA primary prevention: physical activity (slides 16–17)

CORRecommendation
ICounsel adults at healthcare visits to optimize a physically active lifestyle
I≥ 150 min/week moderate or ≥ 75 min/week vigorous aerobic activity (or an equivalent combination)
IIaIf they can't reach that, some moderate or vigorous activity is still beneficial
IIbDecreasing sedentary behaviour may be reasonable

Q: A post-MI patient improves their exercise capacity by 2 METs. Roughly what does the lecture say this does to mortality?

Answer

Each 1 MET lowers mortality by 8–35% (meta-analysis RR 0.87 per MET), so 2 METs is a benefit comparable to adding a cardioprotective drug, or more.

Q: What is the class I physical-activity target for adults in the 2019 guideline?

Answer

≥ 150 min/week of moderate or ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous aerobic activity (or a combination).

LO 3 · Topic 4
⭐ Phase II = 36 monitored sessions over 8–12 weeks

Slides 18–24 · the prevention pyramid · four phases · indications · when to start · the intake · AHA risk classes

Cardiac Rehabilitation: Phases, Indications, Intake

AbbreviationsCR = cardiac rehabilitation · ACS = acute coronary syndrome · CABG = coronary artery bypass graft · PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention · PAD = peripheral artery disease · LVAD = left ventricular assist device · ECG = electrocardiogram · HR = heart rate · ASCVD = atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease
  • CR is the secondary-prevention tier of the ASCVD prevention pyramid (primordial → primary → secondary). Core components: patient assessment, exercise training, physical activity counseling, nutrition counseling, weight, BP, lipid and diabetes management, tobacco cessation, psychosocial management.

The four phases (slide 19)

  1. Phase I — inpatientPhysical therapy and education immediately after the cardiac event or intervention.
  2. Phase II — outpatient, supervisedIndividualized exercise prescription and risk-factor reduction under a medical team: 36 sessions, typically three times a week, ECG-monitored, over 8–12 weeks.
  3. Phases III and IV — maintenanceLong-term lifestyle change: independent continuation of the exercise programme and risk reduction learned in phase II.

Indications (slides 20–21)

IndicationClass
ACS within the preceding 12 monthsI
Post-CABG or post-PCII
Chronic (stable) anginaI
Peripheral artery diseaseI
Chronic heart failureIIa
Valve replacement/repair, LVAD, heart or heart–lung transplantListed

Evidence for other conditions is incomplete, so insurance coverage is often limited to these common diagnoses.

Starting and intake (slide 23)

  • Enter CR 1–2 weeks after discharge after a non-surgical event; 2–6 weeks after CABG or other major surgery.
  • Intake: careful history and examination plus a symptom-limited exercise test to set baseline fitness and maximal HR and to watch for ischemia or arrhythmia.
  • Keep the patient on their usual medications during the exercise test (the prescription must match how they'll exercise, e.g. on a β-blocker).

AHA risk classification for exercise training (slide 24)

ClassWho
AApparently healthy, no evidence of increased exercise risk
BKnown stable CVD, low risk with vigorous exercise (slightly above healthy)
CModerate to high risk of complications during exercise, or unable to self-regulate activity
DUnstable disease → activity restriction

Most patients referred for CR are class B or C.

Q: Describe phase II cardiac rehab.

Answer

Outpatient, supervised: individualized exercise and risk-factor reduction, 36 sessions, about three per week, ECG-monitored, over 8–12 weeks.

Q: When can a patient start CR after an uncomplicated PCI for unstable angina, and after CABG?

Answer

1–2 weeks after discharge for a non-surgical event; 2–6 weeks after CABG.

Q: Which CR indication is class IIa rather than class I?

Answer

Chronic heart failure.

LO 4 · Topic 5
⭐ HF-ACTION: the seminal heart-failure rehab trial

Slides 22, 25–26 · CAD meta-analysis · HF-ACTION · REHAB-HF

The Evidence in CAD & Heart Failure

AbbreviationsCR = cardiac rehabilitation · CAD = coronary artery disease · MI = myocardial infarction · CABG / PCI = bypass / percutaneous coronary intervention · HF = heart failure · HF-ACTION = Heart Failure: A Controlled Trial Investigating Outcomes of Exercise Training · REHAB-HF = Rehabilitation Therapy in Older Acute Heart Failure Patients · KCCQ = Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire · QoL = quality of life
🫀

CAD (47 trials, 10,794 patients)

After MI, CABG, PCI or angina, exercise-based CR vs usual care: total mortality ↓ 13%, CV mortality ↓ 26%. Also better ASCVD risk factors (BP, lipids, smoking), fewer hospitalizations, less angina and depression, better exercise performance and QoL.

💪

HF-ACTION (systolic HF)

The seminal trial establishing CR in HFrEF: 11% ↓ all-cause mortality and hospitalization, 15% ↓ CV death and HF hospitalization, better QoL. Exercise is safe across a broad spectrum of HF.

🧓

REHAB-HF (frail, older, after HF admission)

Multicomponent, low-intensity programme (strength, balance, mobility, endurance) over 36 sessions vs usual care: better physical function, better KCCQ scores, fewer depressive symptoms.

Q: In the CAD meta-analysis, how much did exercise-based CR lower total and cardiovascular mortality?

Answer

Total 13%, cardiovascular 26%.

Q: Which trial established cardiac rehab in systolic heart failure, and what did it show?

Answer

HF-ACTION: 11% lower all-cause mortality/hospitalization, 15% lower CV death/HF hospitalization, better QoL.

LO 5 · Topic 6
⭐ Symptomatic? Train ~10 beats below the ischemic threshold

Slides 27–29 · FITT · intensity thresholds · beyond aerobic exercise

Writing the Exercise Prescription (FITT)

AbbreviationsFITT = frequency, intensity, time, type · HR = heart rate · VO₂ = oxygen uptake · RPE = rating of perceived exertion (Borg 6–20 scale) · HIIT = high-intensity interval training · CVD = cardiovascular disease
FITTPrescription (slide 27)
FrequencyAt least 3 times a week, preferably most days
Intensity40–80% of peak HR reserve · 40–80% of peak VO₂ reserve · 65–80% of peak exercise HR · RPE 11–16
Time5–10 min warm-up · 30–60 min aerobic · 5–10 min cool-down
TypeWalking, jogging, rowing, cycling, elliptical

Intensity in CVD (slide 28)

  • The minimum (threshold) intensity that improves VO₂max in CVD is about 45% of oxygen-uptake reserve ≈ 70% of peak HR.
  • Progress to 50–80% of oxygen-uptake reserve.
  • Symptomatic patients: set the target ~10 beats below the ischemic threshold (the HR at which typical angina appears).
  • Board add-on Heart-rate-reserve (Karvonen) method: target HR = resting HR + % × (peak HR − resting HR).

❤️ Target heart rate

Uses peak HR from the exercise test if you have it (otherwise 220 − age). Karvonen method.

AgeResting HRPeak HR (test)
Ischemic threshold HR

Beyond aerobic activity (slide 29)

  • High-intensity interval training · resistance exercise · flexibility training · balance and mobility (especially older patients).

Q: A 60-year-old on CR has a resting HR of 70 and a peak HR of 140 on the exercise test. What is 60% of the heart-rate reserve as a target?

Answer

Reserve = 140 − 70 = 70; target = 70 + 0.6 × 70 = 112 bpm.

Q: Angina appears at a heart rate of 120 on the exercise test. Where should the training heart rate be set?

Answer

About 10 beats below the ischemic threshold: ~110 bpm.

Q: State the FITT minimums for frequency and aerobic time, and the RPE range.

Answer

≥ 3 days/week; 30–60 min aerobic (plus 5–10 min warm-up and cool-down); RPE 11–16.

LO 3 · Topic 7
⭐ The strongest lever: the physician's endorsement

Slides 30–32 · referral, enrollment, adherence, completion · who misses out · take-home points

Why CR Is Underused

AbbreviationsCR = cardiac rehabilitation · SPP = secondary prevention programme · SES = socioeconomic status
  • Referral: better with automatic inpatient referrals, but varies by region and hospital.
  • Enrollment: only 30–40% of eligible patients take part.
  • Adherence: many don't attend regularly. Completion: dropout is substantial.
👤

Patient & medical factors

Older age, women, racial/ethnic minorities · no or limited insurance · low SES, education or health literacy · low self-efficacy · no perceived need · language or cultural barriers · work, limited social support, home responsibilities · multiple comorbidities, depression.

🏥

System factors

Lack of referral · poor facilitation after referral · strength of the patient's physician's endorsement · patient–provider relationship · programme availability (rural, low-income areas) · distance, hours, parking and transport · lack of diversity in the CR workforce.

🔑 Take-home points (slide 32)

CR is a multi-modality, multidisciplinary intervention that emphasizes exercise and provides comprehensive secondary prevention. It is individualized, benefits behaviour, function and health outcomes across participants, and remains underused.

Q: Roughly what share of eligible patients enroll in CR, and what is the most powerful system-level factor you control?

Answer

30–40%; the strength of the physician's endorsement (and making the referral).

LO 1 & 5 · Topic 8
⭐ Mediterranean = the only diet with RCT evidence of fewer events

Slides 33–43 · primary prevention wheel · heart-healthy diets · guideline recommendations · PREDIMED · sodium · DASH

Diet: Mediterranean, DASH & Sodium

AbbreviationsASCVD = atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease · DASH = Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension · TLC = Therapeutic Lifestyle Changes · PREDIMED = Prevención con Dieta Mediterránea · EVOO = extra-virgin olive oil · SBP = systolic BP · CHD = coronary heart disease · COR = class of recommendation · SGLT-2 = sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 · GLP-1 = glucagon-like peptide-1

2019 ACC/AHA primary prevention (slide 33)

A comprehensive, team-based approach that accounts for social determinants of health: assess ASCVD risk (cholesterol, refine with risk enhancers and coronary calcium) · BP < 130/80 · ≥ 150 min/week moderate or ≥ 75 min/week vigorous activity · aspirin only for select high-risk patients · type 2 diabetes: diet and exercise, metformin first, then SGLT-2 inhibitor or GLP-1 agonist · diet rich in vegetables, fruit, nuts, legumes, fish, whole grains · tobacco: drugs plus behavioural support.

Diets for heart health (slide 34)

DASH, low-fat, Mediterranean, TLC, Ornish, Esselstyn all share: more fruit and vegetables, whole grains, more fibre, less saturated fat.

Guideline nutrition recommendations (slides 35–36)

CORRecommendation
IEmphasize vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts, whole grains and fish
IIaReplace saturated fat with mono- and polyunsaturated fat
IIaLess dietary cholesterol and sodium
IIaMinimize processed meat, refined carbohydrates, sweetened beverages
III: HarmAvoid trans fats

Mediterranean diet and PREDIMED (slides 37–39)

  • Daily: grains, fruit, vegetables, legumes, nuts, olive oil, cheese/yogurt · a few times a week: fish, poultry, eggs, sweets · a few times a month: red meat.
  • PREDIMED (7,447 high-risk adults): Mediterranean diet with extra-virgin olive oil (1 L/week) or nuts (walnuts, hazelnuts, almonds; the slide says 30 g/week, the trial used 30 g/day) lowered MI, stroke and CV death: HR 0.70 for each arm vs a control (low-fat) diet.
  • Other possible benefits: less metabolic syndrome, diabetes, cognitive decline and breast cancer.

Sodium and DASH (slides 40–42)

  • Higher sodium intake (14 cohorts, 37 RCTs): ↑ stroke 24%, ↑ fatal CHD 32%; sodium reduction lowers SBP by ~3.4 mm Hg.
  • DASH-Sodium: the DASH diet lowers SBP at every sodium level (most at high sodium, −5.9 mm Hg), and cutting sodium lowers it further on both diets. The effects add.
  • 2013 lifestyle guideline: anyone who benefits from BP lowering → < 2,400 mg sodium/day; further benefit at < 1,500 mg/day.
🔑 Conclusions (slide 43)

Diet and nutrition are the cornerstone of primary prevention. Endorse patterns proven to lower events. The Mediterranean diet is the only pattern with RCT evidence of fewer ASCVD events. Lowering sodium lowers BP and potentially events, especially in higher-risk patients. Dietary cholesterol tracks blood cholesterol, and heart-healthy patterns are naturally low in it.

Q: Which diet has randomized-trial evidence of fewer cardiovascular events, and what was the effect size?

Answer

Mediterranean (PREDIMED): ~30% fewer MIs, strokes and CV deaths (HR 0.70) with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts.

Q: A hypertensive patient asks how much salt to eat. Give the guideline targets.

Answer

< 2,400 mg sodium/day, with further benefit at < 1,500 mg/day; combine with the DASH diet for additive BP lowering.

Q: Which dietary component carries a class III (harm) recommendation?

Answer

Trans fats: avoid them.

RISK & PHYSIOLOGY

Modifiable risk

  • 75–90% of CAD ← poor diet, inactivity, smoking
  • Inactivity = independent risk factor, 12% of US deaths
  • Moderate activity: ↓ mortality 25% in CAD, 20–30% overall
  • Benefits: ↓ CRP, ↓ platelets/↑ fibrinolysis, ↑ vagal/↓ sympathetic, ↑ NO, ↑ HDL, ↓ BP, ↑ insulin sensitivity, ↑ ischemic threshold, ↓ depression

Definitions

  • HRmax = 220 − age (±10–15) · HR reserve = peak − rest
  • 1 MET ≈ 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min · MET-h = METs × h × days
  • VO₂peak (symptom-limited) < VO₂max in sedentary adults

Response to exercise

  • HR: linear, plateaus in 2–3 min at a steady load
  • SV: curvilinear, plateaus at ~50% VO₂max
  • a–v O₂ difference ↑ 3×; muscle flow 15–20% → 85–90% of CO
  • SBP ↑ 8–12 mm Hg/MET (max ~190 men, ~170 women) · DBP flat or ↓

Fitness & mortality

  • Each 1 MET → 8–35% ↓ mortality in CAD (≈ statins, BB, aspirin, ACEi) · RR 0.87/MET
  • Unfit → fit halves mortality (Blair)
  • < 5 METs (bottom 20%) → refer to CR
  • ≥ 150 min/wk moderate or ≥ 75 vigorous (I); some is better than none (IIa); less sitting (IIb)
CARDIAC REHAB

Phases

  • I inpatient (PT, education)
  • II outpatient: 36 sessions, ~3/wk, ECG-monitored, 8–12 wk
  • III–IV maintenance, independent

Indications

  • Class I: ACS within 12 mo, post-CABG/PCI, chronic angina, PAD
  • Class IIa: chronic HF · also valves, LVAD, transplant

Start & intake

  • 1–2 wk after non-surgical events · 2–6 wk after CABG
  • H&P + symptom-limited exercise test on usual meds
  • AHA class A healthy · B stable low risk · C moderate–high · D unstable → restrict; most CR = B/C

Evidence

  • CAD (47 RCTs): total mortality ↓ 13%, CV ↓ 26%; fewer admissions, less angina and depression
  • HF-ACTION: ↓ 11% death/hospitalization, ↓ 15% CV death/HF admission, better QoL
  • REHAB-HF (frail elderly): better function, KCCQ, mood

Underuse

  • Only 30–40% enroll; poor adherence and completion
  • Missed: older, women, minorities, uninsured, low SES, depression
  • Key lever: physician's endorsement
PRESCRIPTION & DIET

FITT

  • F ≥ 3×/wk · I 40–80% HR reserve, 65–80% peak HR, RPE 11–16 · T 5–10 warm-up, 30–60 aerobic, 5–10 cool-down · T walk, cycle, row
  • Threshold ≈ 45% VO₂ reserve ≈ 70% peak HR → progress to 50–80%
  • Angina: ~10 beats below ischemic threshold
  • Board: Karvonen target = rest + % × (peak − rest)
  • Add HIIT, resistance, flexibility, balance

Primary prevention

  • BP < 130/80 · aspirin only select high-risk · DM: metformin → SGLT-2i/GLP-1 RA
  • Diet I: vegetables, fruit, legumes, nuts, whole grains, fish · IIa: unsaturated for saturated fat, ↓ cholesterol/sodium, ↓ processed meat, refined carbs, sugary drinks · III-Harm: trans fats

Mediterranean · sodium · DASH

  • PREDIMED: EVOO or nuts → HR 0.70 for MI/stroke/CV death — only diet with RCT event reduction
  • High sodium: ↑ stroke 24%, ↑ fatal CHD 32%; ↓ sodium → SBP −3.4
  • DASH + low sodium are additive
  • < 2,400 mg/day; further benefit < 1,500
