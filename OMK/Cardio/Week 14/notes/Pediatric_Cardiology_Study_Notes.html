Listen Like a Pediatrician

Dr. David Baker, DO, is a general pediatrician from Central Maine Medical Center (CMMC). He taught from slides written by Dr. Jim Foster. His approach is practical: what you will actually hear and decide with a stethoscope on a child. You do not have to name the exact lesion. Your job is to decide innocent or refer. The final diagnosis is made by the cardiologist with a 2D echocardiogram with colour-flow Doppler.

🔑 His closing summary — the whole lecture in six lines
  • Where is it loudest? Right upper sternal border → aortic · left upper → pulmonic · left lower → tricuspid / VSD · apex → mitral.
  • Systolic or diastolic? A diastolic murmur is always pathologic.
  • Continuous murmur under the clavicle that changes with position → most likely a venous hum (normal).
  • Murmurs can be normal in high-output states: fever, illness, anemia.
  • Fainting with a prodrome (nausea, lightheadedness, pallor) → vasovagal. Sudden fainting, especially with sports, is abnormal.
  • The most common pathologic lesions today: VSD and bicuspid aortic valve. "The more you listen, the better you'll get."

Listening to the Child's Heart

AbbreviationsRUSB / LUSB = right / left upper sternal border · LLSB = left lower sternal border · S1 = first heart sound (mitral + tricuspid closing) · S2 = second heart sound (aortic + pulmonic closing) · A2 / P2 = aortic / pulmonic parts of S2 · ICS = intercostal space · LV / RV = left / right ventricle · LA / RA = left / right atrium

Why children are harder than adults

  • The crying baby. You have to "lock in" on the heart sounds through the noise.
  • The rate. An adult rests at 60–70/min. A young child may rest at 90–100+, so a murmur is squeezed into a much shorter cycle.
  • A small chest leaves little space between listening posts, but the location of maximal loudness still points to the valve.

Three physics principles behind every murmur

1

Flow

Noise is made by blood moving through valves, vessels or holes. More flow (fever, anemia) makes more noise.

2

Turbulence

The more turbulent the flow, the louder the murmur.

3

Pressure gradients

Blood flows from high pressure to low. After birth the left heart is at higher pressure, so a hole in either septum shunts left → right.

⚠️ Recording slip: the left lower sternal border is tricuspid, not mitral

In his closing summary he said "left sternal border → mitral problems." His own slide (and every board source) puts the mitral valve at the apex and the tricuspid at the LLSB.

The path of blood — and which valves shut when

  • Vena cavae → RA → tricuspid → RV → pulmonic valve → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → LA → mitral → LV → aortic valve → aorta.
  • Systole: aortic and pulmonic open; mitral and tricuspid must be closed. Diastole: the reverse.
  • A leaky (regurgitant) or narrow (stenotic) valve makes noise at the matching point in the cycle.

Describe every murmur four ways

Timing

Where in the cycle?

Systolic = between S1 and S2 · diastolic = after S2 · continuous = through both.

Type (shape)

How does it change?

Holosystolic (flat, S1 to S2 — VSD) · ejection / crescendo-decrescendo (rises and falls — stenosis) · early · late.

Quality

What does it sound like?

Harsh · blowing · vibratory / musical (Still's).

Intensity

How loud?

Grades I–VI (below).

Grading heart murmurs

Slide footnote: diastolic murmurs are only graded up to 4.

Palpate before you listen

  • Thrill: a palpable vibration from a strong murmur, "like a purring cat". A thrill makes it at least grade 4.
  • Lift / heave: a diffuse, forceful impulse from an overworked ventricle.
  • Pericardial friction rub: scratchy, high-pitched and grating. It sounds like a pleural rub, but does not change with breathing.
  • Other pulsations, and accentuated S1 and S2.
🚩 The one rule to never forget

Find S1 and S2 first ("boom-boom"). A sound between them is systolic. An isolated diastolic murmur (after S2) is never normal. It implies regurgitation, e.g. mitral or tricuspid regurgitation.

Q: You hear a loud systolic murmur and feel a vibration when your palm is on the child's chest. You can't hear it with the stethoscope lifted off. What grade is it?

Answer

At least grade 4/6. A thrill makes it grade 4. Grade 5 is heard with only the stethoscope's edge touching; grade 6 is heard off the chest.

Q: A grade 2/6 systolic murmur is loudest at the 2nd right intercostal space and is also heard over the carotids. Which valve?

Answer

Aortic valve, e.g. aortic stenosis from a bicuspid aortic valve.

Q: A healthy 8-year-old has a soft murmur that starts right after S2. Innocent?

Answer

No — a diastolic murmur is always pathologic. Refer for an echo.

⭐ 95 % are innocent

Innocent Murmurs

AbbreviationsRUSB / LUSB / LLSB = right upper / left upper / left lower sternal border · PPS = peripheral pulmonary stenosis · SEM = systolic ejection murmur · S1 / S2 = first / second heart sounds
🔑 The headline number

95 % of heart murmurs in children are innocent. Hearing a murmur is not a reason to "hit the panic button." Other names for the same thing: functional, flow, physiologic, ejection, Still's murmur. Calling any of them "innocent / benign" is never wrong.

Why they come and go: high-output states

  • More flow → more turbulence → more noise. Innocent murmurs get louder in fever, anemia, pregnancy, thyrotoxicosis.
  • A thin chest wall also makes them easier to hear.
  • Heard in a febrile child → recheck when the fever is gone; usually the murmur is gone too.
  • A new murmur in a young child with sickle cell disease → think chronic anemia first.
  • Use discretion with parents. A very soft murmur in a well child doesn't need a list of scary possibilities.

Look at the whole child

😊

Reassuring

  • Thriving, gaining weight, feeding well
  • Good colour, oxygen saturation, pulses and capillary refill
  • Soft systolic murmur that changes with position or fever
🚩

Worrying

  • Turns blue with feeding
  • Excessively irritable (chronic hypoxia?)
  • Chronic cough, wheeze, fast breathing (pulmonary edema)
  • Enlarged liver (backed-up blood / heart failure)
  • Poor feeding or weight gain
🧠 Memory hook Board add-on

The 7 S's of an innocent murmur: Sensitive (changes with position), Short, Single (no clicks or gallops), Small (not radiating), Soft (grade ≤2), Sweet (not harsh), Systolic.

Q: A 4-year-old has a continuous, soft hum below the right clavicle. It disappears when he lies down or turns his head. Next step?

Answer

Reassure: venous hum, an innocent murmur. A continuous murmur that changes with position or jugular compression is benign.

Q: A 3-week-old who is feeding and growing well has a soft systolic murmur at the left upper sternal border radiating to both axillae and the back. Most likely?

Answer

Peripheral pulmonary stenosis, an innocent newborn murmur that fades as the branch pulmonary arteries grow.

Q: A 2-year-old with a temperature of 39.5 °C has a new soft systolic murmur. His exam is otherwise normal. What do you do?

Answer

Fever is a high-output state. Recheck after the fever resolves. The murmur usually disappears.

When to Refer — and Congenital Heart Disease

AbbreviationsCHD = congenital heart disease · SOB = shortness of breath · VSD / ASD = ventricular / atrial septal defect · PDA = patent ductus arteriosus · PFO = patent foramen ovale · TOF = tetralogy of Fallot · TGA = transposition of the great arteries · COA = coarctation of the aorta · WCC = well-child check · ECG / EKG = electrocardiogram

Reasons for referral to a pediatric cardiologist (slide 22)

  1. A possibly structural murmurA valve problem or septal defect — "out of your comfort zone."
  2. Exertional chest painAny chest pain with exercise.
  3. Possible arrhythmiaEspecially with chest pain, dizziness or shortness of breath.
  4. Recurrent syncopeAlways worrisome. The sports-screening questions are in Topic 7.
🔍 Features that make a murmur sound structural Board add-on

Any diastolic murmur (lecture) · grade ≥3 or a thrill · harsh or holosystolic · an abnormal S2 (e.g. fixed split) or a click · loudness that does not change with position · symptoms (poor growth, cyanosis, sweating with feeds).

Congenital heart disease — the big picture

  • CHD happens in 1 in 125 live births — "you are going to see it."
  • It is the most common birth defect.
  • It forms in the first 8 weeks of fetal development.
  • Most cases have no known cause. Some go with syndromes (Turner → coarctation, bicuspid valve).
📈 His update to the slide

The chart says VSD is the most common lesion (>30 %), followed by PDA, pulmonary stenosis, ASD, coarctation, bicuspid aortic valve, tetralogy and TGA. Now that 2D echo is routine, many bicuspid aortic valves are found by chance. For practical purposes, VSD and bicuspid aortic valve are about equally common today.

Case: a newborn murmur that won't go away

Newborn exam → 4-month well-child check
  • Nursery: systolic murmur; the rest of the exam is normal (pink, good saturation, good femoral and brachial pulses, clear lungs). Reasonable to wait: it might be a closing PDA or PFO.
  • 1- and 2-week weight checks: still there, but gaining well.
  • 2-month WCC: crying the whole time, so you can't tell.
  • 4-month WCC: the systolic murmur is louder (grade 2–3). It shouldn't be there by now → refer.
  • The cardiologist takes a history, examines, and gets an ECG and an echo → a large VSD (Topic 4).

Slide 30 asks "What direction would the blood flow in this VSD?" Left ventricle → right ventricle, from high pressure to low. It is already oxygenated blood, so the baby isn't blue. A large shunt overloads the lungs.

Q: Which of these needs a pediatric cardiology referral: (a) a soft vibratory LLSB murmur in a well 5-year-old, (b) chest pain every time a 13-year-old runs, (c) a murmur that appears only with fever?

Answer

(b) Exertional chest pain. (a) is a Still's murmur and (c) is a high-output flow murmur. Both are innocent.

Q: How common is congenital heart disease, and when does it form?

Answer

About 1 in 125 live births (the most common birth defect), in the first 8 weeks of gestation, usually with no known cause.

Fetal Circulation & Left-to-Right Shunts

AbbreviationsVSD = ventricular septal defect · PDA = patent ductus arteriosus · ASD = atrial septal defect · FO / PFO = (patent) foramen ovale · DA = ductus arteriosus · IVC / SVC = inferior / superior vena cava · PVR = pulmonary vascular resistance · RDS = respiratory distress syndrome (of the premature newborn) · CHF = congestive heart failure · RV / LV = right / left ventricle · PGE₁ = prostaglandin E1 · NSAID = non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug

How the septa form (the embryology video)

  • The heart starts as a simple tube that grows fast, loops to the right, then swings back to the midline.
  • Three septa divide it: one between the atria, one between the ventricles, and one splitting the truncus arteriosus into aorta and pulmonary artery.
  • Endocardial cushions fuse to divide the atrioventricular canal. A muscular septum grows up from the floor of the common ventricle.
  • The atrial septum closes the foramen primum. Before it closes, a second hole (the foramen secundum) opens so blood can keep crossing right → left.
  • If a step goes wrong, you get a residual hole (VSD, ASD) or swapped great arteries (TGA).

Fetal circulation — two shunts that must close

  1. Placenta → umbilical veinGas exchange happens in the placenta, not the lungs. Fetal oxygen saturation is only 50–65 % (slide).
  2. Ductus venosus → IVC → right atriumA channel through the liver. (The recording says "ductal stenosis" — it means ductus venosus.)
  3. ~⅓ crosses the foramen ovaleFrom the right atrium to the left atrium → LV → aorta.
  4. ~⅔ goes RV → pulmonary arteryOnly a small fraction (he said ~7 %) enters the fluid-filled lungs. The rest crosses the ductus arteriosus into the aorta.
  5. Umbilical arteries → placentaThey branch off the iliac arteries.
🧠 At the first breath

The lungs open, PVR falls and oxygen rises. The ductus senses the pressure and oxygen change and functionally closes within ~24 h. Left-atrial pressure now exceeds right, pushing the foramen ovale flap shut. It seals anatomically by 6–12 months. Any hole left open now shunts left → right.

VSD — the most common lesion

  • The most common CHD. It forms at 4–8 weeks of gestation, when the single ventricle divides.
  • 80 % membranous, 20 % muscular.
  • Murmur: harsh holosystolic murmur at the LLSB — "one of the most common, if not the most common, pathologic murmurs you'll hear."
  • Small VSD = high-pitched and often louder. Large VSD = lower-pitched and often softer. You can't judge the size by the loudness.
  • A closing VSD can get louder as the hole shrinks and the jet speeds up. That is a good sign.
  • Small VSDs often close on their own within the first 2 years.
  • A large VSD sends extra blood to the lungs → pulmonary edema, heart failure.
⚠️ Check the slide's incidence figure

Slide 32 says "Incidence = 2 to 5 % of births." Board sources give about 2–5 per 1,000 births, i.e. ~30 % of all CHD. Board add-on

PDA — the ductus that stays open

  • The vessel between pulmonary artery and aorta that bypassed the lungs in utero. It usually closes within 6–24 hours (slide).
  • Persists more often in preterm infants and with neonatal RDS.
  • After birth, aortic pressure is higher, so blood flows aorta → pulmonary artery → extra lung volume → pulmonary edema, heart failure, big liver, fast breathing, feeding problems. These develop gradually.
  • Murmur: can sound like a simple systolic ejection murmur, but the classic is a continuous "machinery" murmur.
  • Diagnosis: echo with Doppler.
  • Treatment: IV indomethacin, catheter closure, or surgical ligation.

Board add-on Indomethacin or ibuprofen (NSAIDs) close the ductus by blocking prostaglandin. PGE₁ keeps it open when the baby depends on it (Topics 5–6). PDA goes with congenital rubella. Look for bounding pulses and a wide pulse pressure.

ASD — the atrial hole

  • The foramen ovale is a normal, necessary ASD in utero. It usually closes by 6–12 months.
  • True ASDs come in 4 types and are less common than VSDs. Small ones are often innocent.
  • Larger ASDs → right-ventricular volume overload with a fixed split S2, arrhythmias (SVT, atrial fibrillation), and thrombotic stroke.
  • Closure: an Amplatzer septal occluder placed by catheter.

Board add-on The ASD murmur is a soft systolic ejection murmur at the LUSB. It comes from extra flow across the pulmonic valve, not from the hole itself. Ostium secundum is the most common type (~80 %). Paradoxical embolism (a venous clot crossing to the brain) is why it causes stroke.

Q: A VSD murmur that was grade 2 last year is now grade 3 and higher-pitched. The child is thriving. Is this bad news?

Answer

Usually good news. A shrinking hole speeds up the jet, making it louder and higher-pitched. It may be about to close. Confirm with echo.

Q: A 28-week preterm infant has a continuous murmur, bounding pulses and increasing breathing difficulty. What is the lesion and the drug?

Answer

PDA, treated with IV indomethacin (an NSAID that blocks prostaglandin), or catheter/surgical closure.

Q: Which heart sound finding points to a large ASD, and what complications does it bring?

Answer

A fixed split S2 (right-ventricular overload). Complications: SVT / atrial fibrillation and thrombotic stroke.

Coarctation, Bicuspid Valve & Vascular Ring

AbbreviationsCOA = coarctation of the aorta · BAV = bicuspid aortic valve · AS = aortic stenosis · BP = blood pressure · SVD = spontaneous vaginal delivery · RUSB = right upper sternal border · FHx = family history · CXR = chest x-ray · CT = computed tomography · GERD = gastro-esophageal reflux disease · PGE₁ = prostaglandin E1

Case: 6-hour-old with cool, mottled legs

Term male, no risk factors — the nurse is worried
  • Mildly fast breathing (~40–50/min) and fast heart rate (~150), a murmur, and mottled, cool legs.
  • O₂ saturation 94 % in arms and legs; glucose 60 mg/dL (low-normal for a newborn).
  • Grade 2–3/6 systolic murmur radiating to the back; weak femoral pulses.
  • Four-limb blood pressures: arms 25–30 mmHg higher than legs.
  • → Coarctation of the aorta. The lower body gets oxygenated blood, just not enough of it.

Coarctation of the aorta

  • About 5 % of CHD · boys > girls, 2:1.
  • The narrowing is usually near where the ductus arteriosus inserts.
  • BP is normal or high before the narrowing (arms) and low beyond it (legs).
  • Check femoral pulses on every newborn. It takes diligence, but do it.
  • How fast to intervene depends on the pressure gradient across the narrowing.
  • Options: balloon angioplasty (catheter up from the femoral artery) or surgical resection and re-anastomosis.

Board add-on A critical coarctation is ductal-dependent: the baby crashes into shock as the ductus closes (days 1–2). Give PGE₁ to reopen it. In older children and adults, look for arm hypertension, delayed femoral pulses ("brachiofemoral delay") and rib notching from collateral arteries.

Turner syndrome (45,X)

  • Webbed neck, broad chest with widely spaced nipples, short stature, low hairline, high-arched palate.
  • About 25–30 % have coarctation, and nearly as many have a bicuspid aortic valve.
  • → Suspect Turner? Get an echo to rule out coarctation and BAV.

Case: 3-year-old with a family history of bicuspid valves

New patient, screening echo cancelled on moving day

Grade 2/6 systolic murmur at the RUSB. Could it be BAV? Yes. RUSB points to the aortic valve, and the recording is aortic stenosis introduced by an ejection click. The systolic murmur ends at S2, then silence.

Bicuspid aortic valve

  • Inherited. It has 2 leaflets instead of 3.
  • Found in 1–2 % of people · males > females, 2:1.
  • Often silent in young children, so it's missed or found by chance on echo.
  • Over time: aortic stenosis and regurgitation, aortic dilation, aneurysm and dissection, and endocarditis.
  • Nearly all will need valve surgery in their lifetime.
  • Aortic-area murmur → listen over the carotids. The murmur (even a thrill) may radiate there.
💬 Student question: operate early?

No. The cardiologist follows with an echo about every 2 years. Children are usually symptom-free. They intervene when the pressure gradient across the valve reaches set criteria: first balloon valvuloplasty, eventually a prosthetic valve. "If it ain't broke, don't fix it."

Case: "noisy breathing" that wasn't tracheomalacia

Term girl, noisy breathing from birth; choking at 6 months
  • First thought to be benign tracheomalacia (a floppy trachea; usually just observed).
  • At 6 months: frequent choking episodes, with no reflux (GERD) and no wheeze.
  • CXR: a suspected right-sided mass beside the trachea → barium swallow: the esophagus is indented instead of straight → CT angiogram confirms it.
  • Diagnosis: vascular ring (here a double aortic arch). The vessels wrap around and squeeze both the trachea and the esophagus with every heartbeat.

Vascular rings or slings

7 per 10,000 live births

Abnormal embryologic development of the great vessels.

Symptoms

Airway + swallowing

Stridor (inspiratory noise), cough, choking, wheeze, trouble swallowing, poor feeding, vomiting, frequent respiratory infections, shortness of breath.

Work-up → treatment

CXR → barium → CT

Barium swallow suggests it; CT angiogram confirms it. Treatment is cardiothoracic surgery.

Q: A newborn has weak femoral pulses and arm blood pressure 30 mmHg higher than leg blood pressure. What's the diagnosis and which syndrome should you think of in a girl?

Answer

Coarctation of the aorta. In a girl, think Turner syndrome (45,X) (25–30 % have coarctation).

Q: A child with a bicuspid aortic valve has no symptoms and a mild gradient. Should the valve be replaced now to prevent dissection?

Answer

No. Follow with serial echo (~every 2 years). Intervene when the gradient meets criteria. Nearly all will eventually need surgery.

Q: An infant has stridor and choking, and the barium swallow shows the esophagus pushed in from the side. What confirms the diagnosis?

Answer

CT angiogram, which shows a vascular ring. Treatment is surgery.

⭐ Boot-shaped heart = classic board question

Slides 67–83 · tetralogy of Fallot · the Blalock-Taussig story · hypoplastic left heart · transposition

Cyanotic Heart Disease

AbbreviationsTOF = tetralogy of Fallot · PS = pulmonary (pulmonic) stenosis · RVH = right ventricular hypertrophy · RVOT = right ventricular outflow tract · SVR / PVR = systemic / pulmonary vascular resistance · BT shunt = Blalock-Taussig shunt · HLHS = hypoplastic left heart syndrome · TGA / TGV = transposition of the great arteries / vessels · PDA = patent ductus arteriosus · PFO = patent foramen ovale · PGE₁ = prostaglandin E1 · NICU = neonatal intensive care unit · APGAR = newborn score out of 10 · LMA = laryngeal mask airway · U/S = ultrasound

Case: sat 80 % on 100 % oxygen

37-week infant, APGAR 4 and 7
  • O₂ saturation stays at 80 % despite 100 % oxygen. Circumoral cyanosis (blue around the mouth). Grade 3/6 systolic murmur. Clear, equal breath sounds.
  • Blue with healthy lungs that doesn't respond to oxygen → cyanotic congenital heart disease. Stat CXR → boot-shaped heart → tetralogy of Fallot.

Board add-on Failure to improve on 100 % O₂ (the hyperoxia test) separates a cardiac shunt from lung disease.

Tetralogy of Fallot

  • Rare and complex: about 5 in 10,000 babies.
  • Four defects: large VSD · pulmonary stenosis · right ventricular hypertrophy · overriding aorta.
  • The narrow pulmonic outflow forces deoxygenated RV blood across the VSD into the aorta (right → left) → cyanosis.
  • Tet spell: during crying or feeding, right-to-left shunting surges → blue lips and limbs.
  • "Boot-shaped heart" on CXR — the hypertrophied RV lifts the apex. "A typical board question."

Board add-on The AMBOSS mechanism: agitation ↓ SVR (or ↑ PVR) → worse outflow obstruction → more right-to-left shunt. Knee-to-chest position or squatting ↑ SVR, pushing blood back toward the lungs. The degree of pulmonary stenosis decides how blue the baby is.

Repair — and the story behind the first operation

  • Repair is done soon after birth or later in infancy depending on the degree of pulmonary stenosis. The worse the stenosis, the less blood reaches the lungs and the sooner surgery is needed.
  • Goal of surgery: reduce the shunting and get more blood to the lungs.
  • The first procedure was the Blalock-Taussig shunt: an end-to-side subclavian artery → pulmonary artery connection. It produces a continuous murmur under the clavicle on the side of the shunt.
  • Developed by surgeon Alfred Blalock, cardiologist Helen Taussig and lab technician Vivien Thomas. Their story is told in the 2004 HBO film Something the Lord Made.

Case: the 10-day-old autopsy

Died en route to the ER: fast breathing, fast heart rate, diffuse cyanosis, shock
  • Autopsy: large liver, heavy congested lungs, a small, light heart.
  • The PDA is nearly closed (1 mm); there is an ASD; the left ventricle is tiny.
  • → Hypoplastic left heart syndrome. The baby lived only while the ductus stayed open.
  • Incidence about 1 in 4,350 births (~960 infants/year).
  • For the first few days, the PDA and PFO keep the body supplied.
  • As they close → worsening cyanosis → sudden fast breathing, fast heart rate, low BP, unconsciousness, cardiogenic shock.
  • High mortality, but it may be picked up on prenatal ultrasound.
  • Treatment: prostaglandin E1 (PGE₁) to keep the ductus open → 3-stage heart surgery or heart transplant.
⚠️ Getting the flow direction right in HLHS

The recording's description of the shunt directions was garbled. The correct version: the tiny LV can't pump to the body, so the RV pumps into the pulmonary artery and across the PDA into the aorta (right → left) to reach the body. Oxygenated blood returning from the lungs to the LA must cross the ASD / PFO into the RA (left → right). Close either one and the baby goes into shock. The three stages are Norwood → Glenn → Fontan Board add-on.

Transposition of the great arteries

  • The great arteries are swapped: aorta from the right ventricle, pulmonary artery from the left. Deoxygenated blood goes back out to the body; oxygenated blood goes back to the lungs.
  • That makes two parallel, separate circuits. Survival depends on mixing through a PDA, PFO/ASD or VSD.
  • The lecture only touched on it, and the recording mixed up which artery came from which ventricle. The version above is correct.

Board add-on Cyanosis in the first hours of life. Give PGE₁ ± a balloon atrial septostomy to allow mixing, then the arterial switch operation. The CXR shows an "egg on a string." It is associated with maternal diabetes.

Pediatric Syncope

AbbreviationsNMS = neurally mediated syncope (vasovagal) · α-adrenergic = alpha-adrenergic (sympathetic tone that tightens blood vessels) · SOB = shortness of breath · AAP = American Academy of Pediatrics · HCM = hypertrophic cardiomyopathy · LQTS = long QT syndrome · VT = ventricular tachycardia · ECG = electrocardiogram

Case: the frog dissection

14-year-old in a hot science classroom in late spring

Little breakfast, a hot room, a frog dissection ("probably not her thing"). She collapses; a classmate catches her. She lies pale, sweaty and nauseated. → Vasovagal (neurally mediated) syncope — "very common."

Definitions and numbers

  • Syncope = a temporary loss of consciousness from a reversible disturbance of brain function (too little blood flow to the brain).
  • In children it is usually benign, most often a brief change in vasomotor tone.
  • 15–20 % of children faint at least once before the end of adolescence (slide; he also quoted ~25 %). About 60 % of those have another episode.
CategoryHow commonMechanism / examples
1. Neurally mediated (vasovagal)By far the most commonPeripheral α-adrenergic withdrawal + increased parasympathetic tone. Triggered by emotional distress or prolonged standing.
2. CardiacRare (~4 %)Arrhythmia (long QT, WPW, VT), obstruction (HCM, aortic stenosis)
3. Non-cardiac~4 % neurologicSeizure, etc.
🌡️

Set-ups for vasovagal syncope

Anemia · dehydration · hunger · exhaustion · prolonged standing · heat · crowded or poorly ventilated rooms.

😵‍💫

The prodrome — "something's not right"

Lightheaded · dizzy · nausea · pallor · short of breath · sweating · visual changes.

🛌

Initial treatment

Lie down and elevate both legs · rest and more fluids · slow position changes (sit back down if lightheaded on standing — that's orthostatic) · mildly liberalize salt. "Kids usually get too little salt, not too much."

When a faint is worrying

🚩 Red flags
  • Sudden loss of consciousness with no prodrome, while sitting or lying.
  • Syncope during exercise or activity.
  • Chest pain, palpitations or shortness of breath before or with the episode.
  • Recurrent unexplained episodes.
  • Family history of sudden death, HCM, long QT or VT.

AAP: four questions to ask at every sports pre-participation exam

  1. Have you ever fainted, passed out or had a seizure suddenly and without warning?Especially during exercise, or in response to sudden loud sounds (doorbell, alarm clock, ringing phone).
  2. Have you ever had exercise-induced chest pain or shortness of breath?
  3. Is anyone in your family known to have died suddenly and unexpectedly before age 50?
  4. Is anyone in your family diagnosed with a sudden-death condition?E.g. hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, long QT syndrome, ventricular tachycardia.

Board add-on Fainting triggered by a sudden loud noise is classic for long QT type 2. Fainting while swimming or exercising is classic for long QT type 1.

👶 Breath-holding spells

Toddlers and infants in a temper tantrum can hold their breath until they pass out. It is a syncopal episode, but self-induced.

A newborn at 120–140 is normal, not tachycardic. The rate falls with age. A very fit 16–17-year-old athlete can rest in the 50s.

Impulses start at the SA node, travel the internodal tracts (plus Bachmann's bundle to the left atrium) to the AV node, then down the right and left bundle branches.

Innocent irregular beats — felt as "palpitations"

Sinus arrhythmia

Most common · normal

The rate speeds up on inspiration and slows on expiration. Every beat still has an identical P before the QRS.

Premature atrial contraction (PAC)

Common in everyone

An early beat from the atrium (not the SA node) → different P-wave shape and timing, normal narrow QRS.

Premature ventricular contraction (PVC)

Occasional is common

Early, wide, bizarre QRS with no P wave before it. Ask about caffeine and energy drinks.

🧠 Why sinus arrhythmia happens

He explained it through oxygen delivery. The standard mechanism is vagal tone: breathing in briefly lowers vagal tone, so the rate rises; breathing out restores it, so the rate falls. Either way, it is a normal finding, especially in children. Board add-on

PVCs — when do they get worrying?

  • Couplet = 2 PVCs in a row.
  • 3 PVCs in a row = ventricular tachycardia — "a little worrisome."
  • Uniform (unifocal) = every PVC looks the same (one focus). Multiform (multifocal) = different shapes from more than one focus. That means a hyperexcitable heart and is more worrying.

Catching the beat: Holter and event monitors

  • An office ECG usually misses an intermittent arrhythmia.
  • A Holter monitor records continuously. An event recorder lets the child press a button when they feel symptoms.
  • "Once you capture it, it makes life a lot easier."

Q: A healthy 7-year-old's heart rate rises when she breathes in and falls when she breathes out. Every beat has a normal P wave. What is it?

Answer

Sinus arrhythmia, the most common and completely normal pediatric "arrhythmia." Reassure.

Q: A teen has occasional wide early beats with no preceding P wave, all the same shape. He drinks two energy drinks a day. What are they, and when would they be worrying?

Answer

Uniform PVCs, likely caffeine-related (cut back). Worrying if multiform, or in runs of 3 or more (VT), or with symptoms.

Q: Is a heart rate of 130 normal in a 2-week-old?

Answer

Yes. The normal newborn range is 100–170 (average ~120).

SVT, WPW & Long QT

AbbreviationsPSVT / SVT = (paroxysmal) supraventricular tachycardia · WPW = Wolff-Parkinson-White · QT / QTc = QT interval / rate-corrected QT · VT / VF = ventricular tachycardia / fibrillation · AED = automated external defibrillator · ICD = implantable cardioverter-defibrillator · RF ablation = radiofrequency ablation · DDAVP = desmopressin · ms = milliseconds · PALS = Pediatric Advanced Life Support

The concerning arrhythmias in children

Paroxysmal SVTWolff-Parkinson-WhiteProlonged QTVentricular tachycardia / fibrillation
👶

Infants

Remarkably resilient: they can tolerate SVT at 200–250 for hours. They show lethargy, poor feeding, irritability, and eventually heart failure.

🧒

Children

Less resilient: palpitations, syncope, dizziness, chronic fatigue, shortness of breath, chest discomfort. Anything from no symptoms to sudden cardiac death.

Paroxysmal SVT

  • Abrupt onset of a fast, regular rate: 180–280.
  • Often short and may stop on its own. It may cause no symptoms, or palpitations, dizziness, shortness of breath or chest tightness.
  • Usually re-entry (a "circus rhythm"), sometimes through an accessory pathway.
  • A rate of 120–150 on an anxious 10-year-old's smartwatch is not SVT. SVT needs rates of ~180+ with no sinus variation.
  • Reading the rate: count big boxes between QRS complexes — 1 → 300, 2 → 150, 3 → 100. The slide's tracing is a little under 300, so about 250.

Treating SVT

Treat if it is symptomatic, prolonged or recurrent. First decide: hemodynamically stable or unstable? A child who is talking, with good oxygen and blood pressure, gives you time. One who is unresponsive, poorly perfused or hypoxic does not.

  1. Vagal manoeuvresOlder child: hold the breath, cough, bear down / tense the abdomen. Infant: an ice bag over the forehead and nasal bridge for 20–30 s. This triggers the diving reflex — "literally put an ice bag on a baby in SVT and it can break it."
  2. AdenosineVery short half-life (seconds). "All of a sudden the SVT becomes asystole, and then the rhythm kicks back in."
  3. Synchronized cardioversionIf unstable (hypotensive, poorly responsive).
  4. Recurrent → ablationCatheter ablation of the extra pathway is the cure.
⚠️ Don't do these — and one correction
  • No pressure on the eyeballs: it can cause retinal detachment. Jugular pressure doesn't usually work.
  • He said adenosine "needs to be given close to the heart, like a central line." Board add-on In PALS it is given as a rapid push into a large IV as close to the heart as possible, followed immediately by a saline flush (0.1 mg/kg, then 0.2 mg/kg). A peripheral IV works if the push is fast. A central line is not required.

Wolff-Parkinson-White

  • Slide 109 asks "What is this wave form?" → the delta wave: a slurred upstroke running from the P wave into the QRS.
  • An accessory pathway bypasses the AV node → early activation (delta wave) and re-entry tachycardia.
  • 1.5 per 1,000 · males slightly > females · two peaks: infants and adolescents.
  • Infants: irritability, poor feeding. Older children: palpitations, shortness of breath, chest pain, syncope.
  • Treatment: possibly antiarrhythmic drugs, but primarily radiofrequency ablation.

Board add-on WPW on the ECG: short PR + delta wave + wide QRS. If atrial fibrillation conducts down the pathway, avoid AV-nodal blockers (adenosine, calcium channel blockers, β-blockers, digoxin); use procainamide or cardiovert. Associated with Ebstein anomaly. More in the ECG II notes.

Case: syncope on imipramine

12-year-old with bedwetting, three unexplained faints in 4 months
  • Night-time bedwetting (nocturnal enuresis) resistant to hypnosis and DDAVP → started on low-dose imipramine (a tricyclic antidepressant). It helped.
  • Then three brief, unexplained syncopal episodes. The heart exam is normal, but the ECG explains it.
  • QT from the start of Q to the end of T: 0.63 s (normal example: 0.44 s) → prolonged QT syndrome.

What it is

Genetic ion-channel disease

Inherited abnormal ion-channel proteins. 1 in 5,000 · about 1,000 US deaths a year.

Symptoms

Can be the first and last

Unexplained bradycardia, palpitations, syncope, seizure, or sudden death.

ECG cut-offs (children)

Slide 114

Normal QT < 440 ms · prolonged > 460 ms.

Treatment

Long term

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD).

Board add-on Tricyclics like imipramine prolong the QT, which is why this case unmasked it. Stop QT-prolonging drugs (tricyclics, macrolides, ondansetron, antipsychotics). β-blockers are first-line, with an ICD for high-risk patients. Torsades → IV magnesium. Long QT + congenital deafness = Jervell and Lange-Nielsen syndrome.

Q: A 3-month-old is feeding poorly and irritable, with a regular narrow rhythm at 260. BP and perfusion are good. What is the first move?

Answer

Vagal manoeuvre: an ice bag on the face for 20–30 s (diving reflex). If that fails → adenosine. If unstable → synchronized cardioversion.

Q: A slurred upstroke into the QRS on an adolescent's ECG — what is it, and what is the definitive treatment?

Answer

A delta wave → Wolff-Parkinson-White (accessory pathway). Definitive treatment is radiofrequency ablation.

Q: What QT value is prolonged in a child, and what is the long-term treatment in the lecture?

Answer

> 460 ms (normal < 440 ms). Long-term treatment: ICD (plus β-blockers and avoiding QT-prolonging drugs Board add-on).

Pericarditis & Pericardial Effusion

AbbreviationsJRA = juvenile rheumatoid arthritis (now called juvenile idiopathic arthritis) · VSD = ventricular septal defect · BP = blood pressure · CT = computed tomography · MRI = magnetic resonance imaging · CXR = chest x-ray · LLSB = left lower sternal border · JVD = jugular venous distension · ECG = electrocardiogram · PR = PR segment · STE = ST elevation

Case: 3 weeks after VSD surgery

6-year-old girl with JRA and a repaired VSD
  • Open-heart VSD repair → 3 weeks later a non-specific viral infection.
  • Now: pallor and increasing shortness of breath. In the office she is hypotensive with muffled heart sounds.
  • Echo: a large pericardial effusion — all the black space between the myocardium and the pericardium.
  • Fluid in the sac stops the heart from expanding → it can't fill → cardiac output and blood pressure fall.
  • Pericarditis may give a friction rub. It usually has three parts (mid-systolic, mid-diastolic, late diastolic) and is best heard at the LLSB.
  • Treatment when symptomatic: pericardiocentesis. A needle goes in under the subcostal angle (subxiphoid) to drain the fluid. The before-and-after CXR is "life-saving."

Board add-on Tamponade = Beck triad: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds. Also pulsus paradoxus (systolic BP drops >10 mmHg on inspiration) and electrical alternans. An effusion weeks after heart surgery = post-pericardiotomy syndrome. Pericarditis ECG: diffuse ST elevation with PR depression (see the ECG II notes).

Q: Three weeks after cardiac surgery, a child is short of breath and hypotensive, with muffled heart sounds. Diagnosis and treatment?

Answer

Pericardial effusion with tamponade (likely post-pericardiotomy). Confirm on echo. Treat with pericardiocentesis (subxiphoid).

LO 1–3 · MURMURS & REFERRAL

Where and how to listen

  • RUSB aortic · LUSB pulmonic · LLSB tricuspid/VSD · apex mitral
  • Describe: timing · type (holosystolic vs crescendo-decrescendo) · quality · grade I–VI
  • Grade 3 loud · 4 = thrill · 5 = stethoscope edge · 6 = off the chest · diastolic graded only to 4
  • Diastolic murmur = always pathologic
  • Physics: flow · turbulence · high → low pressure (after birth, shunts go L→R)

Innocent murmurs (95 %)

  • Louder in fever, anemia, pregnancy, thyrotoxicosis
  • Flow: LUSB, through a normal pulmonic valve
  • Still's: LLSB/apex, vibratory/musical, chordae
  • Venous hum: RUSB/infraclavicular, continuous, age > 2, gone with head turn / supine / jugular pressure
  • PPS: newborn, LUSB → axillae and back
  • Red flags: blue with feeds, irritable, cough/wheeze, big liver, poor growth

Refer when

  • Possibly structural murmur · exertional chest pain · arrhythmia with chest pain, dizziness or SOB · recurrent syncope
  • CHD 1/125 births, first 8 weeks, mostly no cause; VSD ≈ BAV most common today
  • Final diagnosis = echo with colour Doppler

  • Birth: PVR ↓ → ductus closes (~24 h); FO seals by 6–12 mo

Left-to-right shunts

  • VSD: 80 % membranous; holosystolic LLSB; small = louder; small ones close by ~2 yr
  • PDA: closes 6–24 h; preemies/RDS; machinery; IV indomethacin, catheter, ligation
  • ASD: fixed split S2, RV overload, SVT/AF, stroke; Amplatzer device

Obstruction

  • Coarctation: 5 % CHD, M:F 2:1, near the ductus; weak femorals, arm > leg BP; balloon or resection
  • Turner (45,X): 25–30 % coarctation, similar % BAV → echo
  • BAV: 1–2 %, M:F 2:1; RUSB + click → carotids; aneurysm/dissection; nearly all need surgery; follow echo q2y
  • Vascular ring: 7/10,000; stridor + dysphagia; barium → CT angiogram → surgery

Cyanotic

  • TOF: VSD + PS + RVH + overriding aorta; tet spells; boot-shaped heart; BT shunt = subclavian → PA
  • HLHS: 1/4,350; shock as the PDA closes; PGE₁ → 3 stages or transplant
  • TGA: aorta from RV; needs mixing (PDA/PFO/VSD)
LO 4–6 · SYNCOPE · RHYTHM · ACQUIRED

Syncope

  • 15–20 % of children; vasovagal most common (α-adrenergic withdrawal + ↑ vagal tone); cardiac ~4 %
  • Prodrome: lightheaded, nausea, pallor, sweating → legs up, fluids, salt
  • 🚩 No warning, exertional, loud-noise trigger, family history of sudden death < 50

Rhythms

  • Newborn 100–170 (avg 120) → teen 60–100
  • Sinus arrhythmia = normal · PACs · PVCs (caffeine) · couplet = 2 · ≥ 3 = VT · multiform is worse · Holter
  • PSVT 180–280, abrupt: vagal (ice to the face, no eye pressure) → adenosine → sync cardioversion → ablation
  • WPW: delta wave, 1.5/1000, infants and teens, RF ablation
  • Long QT: 1/5,000; > 460 ms (normal < 440); syncope/seizure/death; ICD; imipramine case

Acquired

  • HCM: #1 SCD in athletes (36 %); AD; murmur ↑ Valsalva; septum; shows by adolescence; VT/VF; ICD; AEDs cut mortality to ~1 %
  • Kawasaki: fever + eyes, lips, node, rash, hands/feet; platelets ↑; urgent echo; IVIG + aspirin; aneurysms ~5 %
  • Effusion: muffled sounds + hypotension → pericardiocentesis (subxiphoid)
  • IE: fever + murmur + splinter, Roth, Janeway → cultures + echo
