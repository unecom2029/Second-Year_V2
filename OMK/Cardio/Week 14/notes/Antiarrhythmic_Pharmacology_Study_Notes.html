Rate vs Rhythm

Unlike the other cardio pharmacology lectures, this is new material, not an OMK 1 refresher. Dr. Scully builds everything on one skill: knowing which ion carries each phase of the action potential in nodal tissue and in myocytes. Once you know that, each drug class tells you which tissue it targets, what it does to the action potential, its indication and its adverse effects.

The learning objectives

  1. Sort the four antiarrhythmic classes by mechanism…and by their effect on the nodal (pacemaker) and myocyte (non-pacemaker) action potential, through the ion currents they block.
  2. For every drug: mechanism, AP effect, indication, adverse effects, contraindications and drug interactionsClasses I–IV, plus adenosine, digoxin and atropine.
  3. Apply them to a caseThe hockey player at the start and end of the lecture (Topic 11).

The organising idea

⏱️

Rate control → nodal tissue

Target the tissue with automaticity (SA and AV node). Nodal upstroke is carried by Ca²⁺, and its rate is set by sympathetic tone.

Class II (β-blockers) and class IV (verapamil, diltiazem). Digoxin and adenosine also slow the node.

🎼

Rhythm control → myocytes

Target atrial and ventricular muscle: either the Na⁺ upstroke (phase 0) or the K⁺ repolarisation that ends the plateau.

Class I (Na⁺-channel blockers) and class III (K⁺-channel blockers).

🔑 "When you hear someone say…"

"We're going to try a rate approach for this patient's AF" → a β-blocker or a calcium channel blocker. "Rhythm control" → a class I or class III drug.

Action Potentials & How Arrhythmias Start

AbbreviationsAP = action potential · If = funny current · ICa / INa / IK = calcium / sodium / potassium current · Ito = transient outward K⁺ current · β1 = beta-1 adrenergic receptor · M2 = muscarinic type-2 receptor · Gs / Gi = stimulatory / inhibitory G-protein · cAMP = cyclic adenosine monophosphate · PKA = protein kinase A · SERCA = sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺-ATPase · EAD / DAD = early / delayed afterdepolarisation

Pacemaker vs non-pacemaker cells

🔁

Pacemaker cells — automaticity

They depolarise above threshold rhythmically on their own.

  • SA node · AV node
  • Ventricular conduction system: bundle of His, bundle branches, Purkinje fibres
💪

Non-pacemaker cells

Atrial and ventricular myocytes.

  • In disease, these can acquire automaticity (ectopic beats).
🧠 The classroom Q&A, distilled
  • Which AP has a plateau? The myocyte's. It is a Ca²⁺ plateau that prolongs contraction to "get that good squeeze" for ejection.
  • Why does the pacemaker cell have automaticity? The funny current (If), carried by Na⁺. It is "funny" because hyperpolarisation, not depolarisation, opens it.
  • Phase 0: carried by Ca²⁺ in nodal cells and Na⁺ in myocytes. K⁺ repolarises both.

How autonomic tone sets the rate

Sympathetic → β1→Gs→↑ adenylyl cyclase → ↑ cAMP → PKA→phosphorylates Ca²⁺ channels, phospholamban (↑ SERCA), phospholemman→steeper phase 4, faster conduction, stronger contraction
Parasympathetic → M2→Gi→↓ adenylyl cyclase → ↓ cAMP→more hyperpolarised, flatter phase 4→slower rate

"S and I work on the same effector": both converge on adenylyl cyclase and therefore on PKA. Phosphorylating the proteins that traffic Ca²⁺ is how the heart changes contractility and conduction velocity.

What sets the firing rate (hidden slide 10)

  • Steeper phase 4 → threshold reached sooner → faster firing.
  • More negative threshold → reached sooner → faster.
  • Less negative (more positive) resting potential → less time to repolarise fully → faster.
  • The fastest pacemaker sets the heart rate: SA node 60–100/min · AV node and His 40–60/min · Purkinje 20–40/min. Board add-on (the slide leaves these blank)

Defects in impulse formation

Altered automaticity

Slide 11

SA rate = sympathetic vs parasympathetic balance. If disease slows the SA node, latent pacemakers take over. Damaged tissue that can't hold its ion gradients or loses gap junctions fires ectopic beats.

Early afterdepolarisations

Triggered activity

Oscillations during repolarisation (or the plateau) of a prolonged AP. Repeated ones trigger an arrhythmia → torsades.

Delayed afterdepolarisations

Triggered activity

Just after repolarisation. Intracellular Ca²⁺ overload drives the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (3 Na⁺ in per Ca²⁺ out), which depolarises the cell.

Defects in impulse conduction

  • Re-entry: a self-sustaining circuit re-excites the same myocardium again and again, fast. The AV node has a slow and a fast branch. If one recovers while the other is still refractory, the impulse runs back up and loops. Each lap sends a new impulse out, overdriving the SA node. Circuits can be tiny (AV nodal re-entry) or long (accessory pathway).
  • Conduction block: the impulse fails to propagate (still-refractory tissue, or tissue damaged by trauma, ischemia or scarring). It can show up clinically as bradycardia.
  • Accessory pathway — the bundle of Kent: conducts straight from atria to ventricles, bypassing the AV node, faster than the node → the set-up for re-entrant tachycardias (WPW).

State-dependent block (hidden slide 14)

Na⁺ channels cycle through resting → open → inactivated. Class I drugs have high affinity for the open and inactivated states. That makes the block use-dependent: the faster the tissue fires, the more block. Binding prolongs the refractory period, especially in ischemic tissue, where the channels are more active.

Q: Which ion carries phase 0 in an SA-node cell, and which carries it in a ventricular myocyte?

Answer

Ca²⁺ (L-type channels) in the SA node; Na⁺ (fast channels) in the myocyte. K⁺ repolarises both. That is why CCBs act on the node and Na⁺-channel blockers on muscle.

Q: β1 and M2 receptors both act on one enzyme. Which one, and through which G-proteins?

Answer

Adenylyl cyclase: β1 via Gs (↑ cAMP → PKA) and M2 via Gi (↓ cAMP).

Q: A delayed afterdepolarisation appears in a Ca²⁺-overloaded myocyte. What current depolarises the cell?

Answer

The Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger working forward: it extrudes 1 Ca²⁺ for 3 Na⁺ in, a net inward (depolarising) current.

How the Drugs Work: Vaughan Williams

AbbreviationsAP = action potential · ERP = effective refractory period · PR / QRS / QT = ECG intervals (AV conduction / ventricular depolarisation / depolarisation + repolarisation) · CCB = calcium channel blocker

Four levers (slide 15)

  1. Maximum diastolic potentialIn pacemaker cells (and the resting potential in ventricular myocytes).
  2. Rate of phase 4 depolarisationIn either tissue.
  3. Threshold potentialFor the phase 0 upstroke in nodal cells.
  4. Action potential durationIn both tissue types.
🧠 Worked example from the lecture

Raise the threshold without changing the slope of the funny-current drift → phase 4 takes longer to reach threshold → the rate goes down. Draw it: same slope, higher line to cross.

⚠️ The goal — and the catch (slide notes)

Restore and maintain normal sinus rhythm without causing pro-arrhythmia. "They're all pro-arrhythmic at the right dose." These drugs are also not completely selective; several act in more than one class.

Q: A cardiologist chooses "rate control" for a patient's AF. Which two Vaughan Williams classes are on the table, and what tissue do they target?

Answer

Class II (β-blockers) and class IV (verapamil, diltiazem), targeting nodal tissue (the AV node), where Ca²⁺ and sympathetic tone set conduction.

Q: A drug makes the threshold potential of a nodal cell more positive but leaves the phase 4 slope unchanged. What happens to heart rate, and why?

Answer

It falls. The drift takes longer to reach the higher threshold, so each cycle is longer.

LO 2 · Topic 3
⭐ Quinidine ↑ digoxin — a board favourite

Slides 17–20 · Na⁺-channel block · the two mnemonics · quinidine, procainamide, disopyramide

Class I Overview & Class IA

AbbreviationsNa⁺ / K⁺ = sodium / potassium · ERP = effective refractory period · AP = action potential · EAD = early afterdepolarisation · TdP = torsades de pointes · MI = myocardial infarction · NAPA = N-acetylprocainamide · SVT = supraventricular tachycardia · SLE = systemic lupus erythematosus

What all class I drugs share

  • They block the fast Na⁺ channel that carries the phase 0 upstroke in myocytes → slower upstroke, slower conduction.
  • In nodal tissue (slide 17) they also make the threshold more positive and flatten phase 4. This is a minor effect, so class I counts as a rhythm drug.
  • Some also block K⁺ channels and change repolarisation. That is where the three subclasses differ.
🚕

Upstroke slowing: "salty CAB driver"

From the biggest deflection of phase 0 to the smallest: C > A > B.

Or the "salty cheeseburger with Bacon And Cheese", in increasing order: B < A < C. Pick your favourite.

🐻

Repolarisation: Goldilocks

IA is too big (prolongs repolarisation) · IB is too short (shortens it) · IC is just right (no change).

Prolonged repolarisation → prolonged refractory period → torsades risk (IA, like class III).

Class IA — quinidine, procainamide, disopyramide

  • Block Na⁺ channels in the open state → moderate slowing of phase 0.
  • Block K⁺ channels → prolonged repolarisation (phases 2–3) and a longer ERP.
  • Together: ↓ conduction velocity + ↑ ERP → less re-entry.
🧠 Why a long refractory period causes torsades

If repolarisation is too slow, the voltage-gated Na⁺ channels recover from inactivation before the membrane has dropped below threshold. They fire again and the membrane oscillates. These are early afterdepolarisations: triggered activity → torsades de pointes.

Quinidine — the IA prototype

Oral · Na⁺ + K⁺ channel blocker

Adverse effects: torsades de pointes · cinchonism — ↓ hearing, tinnitus, blurred vision, delirium (named after the cinchona bark of traditional medicine) · anticholinergic effects · nausea, diarrhea, headache.

Contraindications: QT prolongation · myasthenia gravis (anticholinergic effects).

Interaction: quinidine ↑ digoxin levels. It knocks digoxin off plasma proteins and lowers its renal clearance. "Drug–drug interactions with digoxin are a board favourite."

Monitor K⁺: hypokalemia ↓ efficacy and worsens QT prolongation.

Procainamide

IV / oral · supraventricular and ventricular

Indications: supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias. Used to reduce re-entrant arrhythmia in acute MI with ↓ cardiac output.

PK: bioavailability 75–95% · protein bound only 15–20% · acetylated in the liver → NAPA (active) · renally cleared; dialyzable.

AEs: same as quinidine, plus drug-induced lupus with chronic use (butterfly rash). Does not alter digoxin levels.

Disopyramide

Slide notes

Similar to quinidine, with fewer GI effects but more profound anticholinergic effects.

Board add-on It is negatively inotropic and can worsen heart failure.

💬 Student question: "So quinidine kicks digoxin off?"

Yes. Drug bound to plasma protein is generally inactive, because effect tracks free drug. Quinidine displaces digoxin from its plasma-protein sites, so free digoxin rises. Its K⁺ effects also change digoxin's pharmacodynamics (Topic 9).

Board add-on Memory hook: "the QUEEN PROofreads DISsertations" (Quinidine, Procainamide, Disopyramide). Procainamide is used for WPW with atrial fibrillation (see ECG II notes). Its lupus is more likely in slow acetylators.

Q: A patient on quinidine reports ringing in the ears, blurred vision and confusion. Name the syndrome.

Answer

Cinchonism: decreased hearing, tinnitus, blurred vision and delirium.

Q: Why can quinidine produce digoxin toxicity, and which IA drug avoids that interaction?

Answer

Quinidine displaces digoxin from plasma proteins and reduces its renal clearance → ↑ free digoxin. Procainamide does not alter digoxin levels.

Q: After months of procainamide, a patient develops a malar rash and arthralgias. What happened?

Answer

Drug-induced lupus, an adverse effect of chronic procainamide.

LO 2 · Topic 4
⭐ IC only in a structurally normal heart

Slides 21–24 · lidocaine and mexiletine · flecainide and propafenone

Class IB & IC

AbbreviationsPVC = premature ventricular contraction · VT = ventricular tachycardia · AF = atrial fibrillation · CNS = central nervous system · CYP = cytochrome P450 liver enzymes · QRS = ventricular depolarisation on the ECG · MI = myocardial infarction

Class IB — lidocaine, mexiletine

  • Block Na⁺ channels in the open and inactivated states. They dissociate rapidly, so the block is mild.
  • Most effective in depolarised or rapidly driven tissue. Ischemic or diseased myocardium can't hold its membrane potential and fires more often, so its channels sit in the bindable states more of the time.
  • Result: an almost preferential effect on damaged tissue, with little effect on normal myocardium.

Lidocaine — the IB prototype

IV · also a local anesthetic ("-caine")

Indication: ventricular arrhythmias, as second-line emergency use. In stable patients it is reserved for bothersome PVCs or ventricular tachyarrhythmias.

Adverse effects: CNS effects (confusion, dizziness, seizures; it blocks CNS Na⁺ channels too) · methemoglobinemia · tremor.

Interactions: pharmacokinetic, via CYP competition or inhibition. Adjust the dose for hepatic function.

Mexiletine

Oral IB

The oral relative of lidocaine (named on the slide). Board add-on Used for chronic ventricular arrhythmias. Phenytoin also has class IB activity.

Board add-on "Class IB is Best after MI; class IC is Contraindicated." IB drugs also treat digitalis-induced arrhythmias (summary table, slide 42).

Class IC — flecainide, propafenone

  • Marked slowing of the phase 0 upstroke: the biggest deflection of the three. Repolarisation is unchanged.
  • They suppress PVCs.
  • Widen the QRS, more evident with exertion. The QRS is ventricular depolarisation. A flatter upstroke means depolarisation takes longer, so the QRS broadens, and more so at faster rates (use-dependence).

Flecainide — the IC prototype

Oral · "pill in the pocket" for AF has been tried

Indications: sustained VT · prevention of AF in a structurally normal heart.

Pro-arrhythmic in ischemic or structural heart disease. "Paying attention to context clues in your vignettes … can help you from causing an arrhythmia."

AEs: new or worsening arrhythmia · sinus-node dysfunction · marked ↓ conduction velocity · CNS: blurred vision, headache, ataxia.

Combinations in the literature: flecainide + metoprolol improved rhythm control and tolerability in persistent AF (Europace 2016). Expect board answers and clinic practice to differ as indications evolve.

Propafenone

IC

Named on the slide with flecainide. Board add-on It also has weak β-blocking activity.

⚠️ The vignette clue

Prior MI, scar, heart failure or structural disease in the history → avoid class IC. Board add-on The CAST trial showed higher mortality with IC drugs after MI.

Q: Why does lidocaine suppress ventricular ectopy from ischemic tissue while barely affecting healthy myocardium?

Answer

It binds Na⁺ channels in the open/inactivated states and dissociates rapidly. Ischemic, depolarised tissue fires often and holds channels in those states, so the block builds up there. In normal tissue it washes off between beats.

Q: A patient with a prior MI and reduced ejection fraction develops recurrent AF. Is flecainide a good choice?

Answer

No. Class IC drugs are pro-arrhythmic in ischemic or structural heart disease. They are for AF prevention in a structurally normal heart.

Q: On flecainide, why does the QRS widen more during exercise?

Answer

Use-dependent Na⁺-channel block: more beats per minute give more block, so phase 0 is slower and ventricular depolarisation takes longer.

⭐ Lipophilic β-blockers → vivid nightmares

Slides 25–27 · β1 blockade of nodal tissue · three generations · esmolol · labetalol in pregnancy · propranolol and long QT

Class II: β-Blockers

Abbreviationsβ1 / β2 = beta-1 / beta-2 adrenergic receptor · α1 = alpha-1 adrenergic receptor · If = funny current · AV = atrioventricular · PSVT = paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia · VT = ventricular tachycardia · MI = myocardial infarction · NO = nitric oxide · CNS = central nervous system · QT = ECG interval of depolarisation + repolarisation

What they do to the nodal AP

  • Block sympathetic input to the pacing regions → flatter phase 4 (slower funny-current drift).
  • Prolong repolarisation, especially at the AV node, and deepen it slightly → longer to reach threshold.
  • Prolonged repolarisation ↓ re-entry: an aberrant impulse arriving at an AV node still in its refractory period simply stops.

Slide notes: normally β1 activation ↑ If in the SA node (↑ rate). In the AV node it ↑ Ca²⁺ and K⁺ currents, ↑ conduction velocity and ↓ the AV node's refractory period. β-blockers reverse all of that.

Three generations

1st — non-selective

β1 + β2

Propranolol (also nadolol, pindolol, timolol). Not for asthmatics: albuterol needs β2, and blocking β2 causes bronchoconstriction.

2nd — β1-selective

Less β2 risk

Atenolol, metoprolol, esmolol. Esmolol has a very short half-life: it "washes in and washes out", so it suits hemodynamically unstable patients. In practice it is limited by the volume of fluid that must be pushed with it.

3rd — extra CV effects

Vasodilating

Carvedilol and labetalol also block α1. Nebivolol stimulates endothelial NO production.

Indications (slide 26)

  • Supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias, especially those triggered by sympathetic activation: exercise, emotional stress, test-taking, public speaking ("palpitations that get in the way of daily life").
  • Stable exertional angina (ischemic heart disease notes) · reduce mortality after MI · hypertension (not first-line).
🤰 When is a β-blocker first-line for hypertension? Pregnancy → labetalol

Lowering cardiac output triggers the baroreceptor reflex: sympathetic outflow clamps down peripheral vessels, including those that perfuse the placenta. Labetalol's α1 blockade prevents that compensatory vasoconstriction, protecting placental perfusion.

🔑 "Fun fact" for the nitty-gritty question

Propranolol does not prolong repolarisation, so it can be used in patients with a prolonged QT. If a question asks which β-blocker for a long-QT patient, this is the answer.

Adverse effects (slide 27)

😴 How to counsel

The nightmares "feel like you're awake". A colleague described paralysed dread with silhouetted figures standing over her bed. You don't have to plant the idea: say the drug "may cause some issues around sleep — let me know if anything occurs."

Board add-on β-blockers mask hypoglycemia (tachycardia, tremor) in diabetics. Taper them; abrupt withdrawal → rebound tachycardia and ACS. Avoid in pheochromocytoma or cocaine use before α-blockade (unopposed α). Beware combining with verapamil or diltiazem (AV block).

Q: A student gets palpitations and a racing heart before every exam and presentation. Which drug class targets this, and why?

Answer

A β-blocker (e.g. propranolol): the arrhythmia is driven by sympathetic activation, which β-blockade removes at the node.

Q: Why is labetalol, rather than metoprolol, the β-blocker for hypertension in pregnancy?

Answer

Its α1 blockade stops the baroreflex vasoconstriction that would otherwise follow the ↓ cardiac output, preserving placental perfusion.

Q: Which β-blocker suits a patient with a prolonged QT, and why?

Answer

Propranolol, because it does not prolong repolarisation.

Class III: K⁺ Blockers & Amiodarone

AbbreviationsK⁺ = potassium · IKr = rapid component of the delayed-rectifier K⁺ current · AF / AFL = atrial fibrillation / flutter · QT = depolarisation + repolarisation interval · TdP = torsades de pointes · VT / VF = ventricular tachycardia / fibrillation · EF = ejection fraction · CrCl = creatinine clearance · PFT / TFT / LFT = pulmonary / thyroid / liver function tests

Class III drugs block repolarising K⁺ currents → a longer phase 2 plateau and phase 3 → a longer effective refractory period. K⁺ flows out against the Ca²⁺ coming in during the plateau. When the Ca²⁺ channels close, K⁺ repolarises the cell. You won't be examined on the delayed vs rapid delayed-rectifier subtypes. The effect on the AP is the same.

⚠️ The class III risk: QT → torsades

The plateau is the distance between depolarisation and repolarisation, so prolonging it prolongs the QT → torsades. Ibutilide 2%, dofetilide 1–3%. "Seems like a low number — that's 2 in 100 people." So these are not started as outpatient drugs.

Contraindications: concurrent QT-prolonging drugs, congenital long QT, and any class I or class III drug at the same time. Which class I subclass also prolongs repolarisation? IA (Goldilocks: "too big").

Ibutilide

IV · pharmacological conversion

MOA: inhibits the delayed-rectifier K⁺ current and enhances the slow inward Na⁺ current.

Indication: termination of AF and AFL (chemical conversion; the alternative is electrical cardioversion).

AEs: AV block, prolonged QT, TdP (~2%).

Dofetilide

Oral · conversion and maintenance

MOA: inhibits the rapid component of the delayed-rectifier K⁺ current (IKr).

Indications: conversion of AF/AFL and maintenance of sinus rhythm after conversion (the difference from ibutilide).

No negative inotropy → OK with a depressed EF. Renally excreted → dose by CrCl.

Sotalol

Mixed class II + III ("-olol")

Indications: severe ventricular arrhythmias, especially in patients who can't tolerate amiodarone · preventing recurrent AF/AFL. Not usually first-line.

AEs: like class II (fatigue, bradycardia) and like class III (TdP).

Can you draw it? Flatter phase 4 in the node (β) + longer plateau in the myocyte (K⁺).

Amiodarone — "it does all the things"

Amiodarone

Mixed class I, II, III and IV activity · IV and oral

Indications: unstable VT · recurrent VF · supraventricular arrhythmias · prevention of recurrent paroxysmal AF/AFL (off-label).

Contraindications: 2nd- and 3rd-degree heart block · symptomatic bradycardia · cardiogenic shock · polymorphic VT with a prolonged QT (may worsen it).

History: once used for everything, then dropped as adverse effects emerged. It is back on "every algorithm and every protocol" now that monitoring is understood.

💬 Student question: from one dose?

No. The cardiovascular effects can appear with a single acute dose. The other organ toxicities come with long-term use.

Board add-on Amiodarone is about 37% iodine by weight → thyroid effects (Wolff-Chaikoff hypothyroidism or Jod-Basedow thyrotoxicosis). It is a CYP450 inhibitor → ↑ warfarin, digoxin and simvastatin. It is lipophilic, with a half-life of weeks. Despite the QT prolongation, its torsades risk is low. It has almost no negative inotropy, so it is safe in HFrEF.

Q: What distinguishes dofetilide from ibutilide in indication, and why can dofetilide be used with a reduced ejection fraction?

Answer

Dofetilide also maintains sinus rhythm after conversion (ibutilide only terminates AF/AFL), and it has no negative inotropic effect.

Q: A patient on long-term amiodarone develops a dry cough, dyspnea and bilateral reticular opacities. What toxicity, and what monitoring should have been in place?

Answer

Amiodarone pulmonary fibrosis. Baseline and annual PFTs, plus thyroid, liver and eye monitoring.

Q: Why are ibutilide and dofetilide started in hospital?

Answer

They prolong the QT with a 1–3% risk of torsades de pointes, so patients need monitoring.

Class IV: Verapamil & Diltiazem

AbbreviationsCCB = calcium channel blocker · DHP = dihydropyridine ("-dipine") · L-type = long-lasting voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel · AV / SA = atrioventricular / sinoatrial · PSVT = paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia · AF with RVR = atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response · HF = heart failure
🩸

Dihydropyridines ("-dipines")

Arterial vasodilators → ↓ systemic vascular resistance. Better at the peripheral vasculature.

🫀

Non-dihydropyridines

Negative inotropes and chronotropes → ↓ contractility, heart rate and conduction. Verapamil is best at nodal tissue; diltiazem "just does both", less effectively than either.

  • They act preferentially on pacemaker tissue, which depends on Ca²⁺ for its upstroke. They block L-type channels → slower upstroke in the AV node → ↓ conduction velocity.
  • The AV node is kept refractory, so an ectopic signal can't propagate through it.
  • Caution in conduction-system disease: both drugs can potentiate SA- and AV-node abnormalities.

Indications (slide 33)

  • Re-entrant PSVT, where the AV node is part of the circuit.
  • AF with RVR: rate control. Atrial ectopy hits refractory AV node and doesn't reach the ventricles.
  • Hypertension · ischemic heart disease, including vasospastic angina (see ischemic heart disease notes).

Verapamil and diltiazem — adverse effects and interactions (slide 34)

Exaggerations of the effect

Myocardial and nodal: bradycardia, AV block, heart failure ("they're all pro-arrhythmic at the right dose").

Hypotension (↓ rate; diltiazem also dilates vessels) · edema (diltiazem) · constipation (verapamil).

IV verapamil + a β-blocker is contraindicated: it can cause irreversible electromechanical dissociation (drug-induced heart failure).

Non-DHPs ↑ plasma digoxin by competing for its renal excretion (pharmacokinetic interaction).

Q: Why does verapamil terminate AV-nodal re-entrant tachycardia but do little for a ventricular arrhythmia?

Answer

Nodal phase 0 is Ca²⁺-dependent. Verapamil slows AV-node conduction and prolongs its refractory period, breaking circuits that run through the AV node. Ventricular myocytes depolarise with Na⁺.

Q: A patient on metoprolol is given IV verapamil for SVT and becomes profoundly hypotensive with no effective contraction. Explain.

Answer

The IV verapamil + β-blocker combination causes electromechanical dissociation. It is contraindicated.

LO 2 · Topic 8
⭐ "Have you tried turning it off and on again?"

Slide 35 + the ED video · P1 receptors · converting narrow-complex PSVT

Adenosine

AbbreviationsP1 = purinergic (adenosine) receptor class · Gi/o = inhibitory G-protein · IKACh = acetylcholine-activated K⁺ channel · PSVT = paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia · ED = emergency department · ACLS = Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support · IV = intravenous · BVM = bag-valve mask
Adenosine → P1 purinergic receptor (GPCR)→Gi/o; the βγ subunit opens K⁺ channels (IKACh)→hyperpolarises SA-node, atrial and AV-node cells→a big pause: brief asystole or severe bradycardia→the underlying rhythm takes back over

The "IT approach" to tachyarrhythmias: switch the conduction system off and on again. It washes out very fast (very short half-life), so the pause is brief.

Use (slide 35)

  • Conversion of narrow-complex PSVT. Common pre-hospital and in the ED; highly effective.
  • First try no drug — vagal manoeuvres: Valsalva (bear down; or have the patient try to blow the plunger out of a syringe, which avoids the "bear down like you're pooping" instruction) · cold water · carotid sinus massage · tilting.
  • Slide notes (ACLS): if vagal manoeuvres fail in stable narrow-complex SVT, adenosine is the drug of choice. In unstable regular narrow-complex SVT it may be tried before synchronised cardioversion.
🔑 The ED video — what to notice
  • 6 mg, given "all at once, like a hand grenade" with an immediate saline flush. It must reach the heart at a high concentration because it is fast on and fast off.
  • The monitor went from ~200 to a severe bradycardia with signs of AV block (never quite asystole), then sinus rhythm.
  • The patient groaned: they feel it.
  • "If she's tachycardic because she needs 15 L of fluid, it may not work." Adenosine fixes re-entry, not sinus tachycardia.
  • If it fails: repeat it, or move to cardioversion.

Adverse effects — generally transient

How it feels

Warn the patient

A sense of impending doom ("I'm going to die") · feels like being kicked in the chest · headache · flushing · chest pain.

ECG

Expected

Brief asystole, AV block or severe bradycardia.

Airway

Bronchoconstriction

Can last up to 30 min in asthma — the clue in the hockey case (↑ resistance on the BVM).

Board add-on Theophylline and caffeine block adenosine receptors, so larger doses are needed. Avoid in pre-excitation (WPW) and in asthma. Adenosine is also used diagnostically: it unmasks flutter waves in atrial flutter.

Q: What is adenosine's mechanism in PSVT, and why is it pushed rapidly with a flush?

Answer

P1 (A1) receptor → Gi/o → opens K⁺ channels → hyperpolarises SA and AV nodal tissue → transient AV block that breaks re-entry. Its half-life is seconds, so it must reach the heart as a concentrated bolus.

Q: Seconds after adenosine, an asthmatic patient becomes hard to ventilate. Why?

Answer

Adenosine-induced bronchoconstriction, which can last up to 30 minutes in asthma.

LO 2 · Topic 9
⭐ Toxic? Check the potassium first

Slides 36–38 · Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase · nodal and autonomic effects · K⁺ · interactions · toxicity

Digoxin

AbbreviationsNa⁺/K⁺-ATPase = the sodium–potassium pump · NCX = Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger · SR = sarcoplasmic reticulum · CICR = Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release · AF / AFL = atrial fibrillation / flutter · HF = heart failure · CKD = chronic kidney disease · BB / CCB = β-blocker / calcium channel blocker · Fab = antibody fragment
Digoxin blocks the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase→↑ intracellular Na⁺→the NCX can't extrude Ca²⁺ (it can even reverse)→↑ SR Ca²⁺ stores → bigger CICR→↑ contractility (inotropy)
💪

Inotropic effect

The pump-block → Ca²⁺ chain above. This is why digoxin appears in heart failure (HF notes).

⚡

Antiarrhythmic effect

Direct action on the conduction system: ↓ AV-node automaticity, longer nodal refractory period, slower conduction. Autonomic: ↓ sympathetic outflow (looks like β1 blockade), sensitised baroreceptors, ↑ vagal tone (M2).

Indications: heart failure, atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter (rate control). Digoxin and digitoxin are gaining use again after falling out of favour.

Potassium — the key to its pharmacodynamics

🔑 K⁺ competes with digoxin at the pump
  • Hypokalemia → ↑ digoxin effect and toxicity. Low extracellular K⁺ phosphorylates the pump, which raises digoxin's binding affinity.
  • Hyperkalemia → ↓ clinical effect.
  • Signs of toxicity → check serum K⁺ first. "We don't have to give an antidote if all we have to do is fix their potassium."
  • Narrow therapeutic index: consider kidney function (renally cleared) and K⁺ when dosing.

Toxicity (slide 38)

ECG

"Swoosh" / "reverse check"

Down-sloping (scooped) ST depression and arrhythmias. "Here's the EKG — what drug are they on?" is a common question.

Eyes

Xanthopsia, chromatopsia

Yellow-green halos around lights, altered colour vision, blurry vision.

Other

GI and CNS

Anorexia (and nausea), agitation.

Treat

Antidote

Normalise K⁺. Digoxin immune Fab (Digibind) binds digoxin 1:1; it may cause heart failure or anaphylaxis.

Art historians suspect Van Gogh had digitalis toxicity: he was reportedly treated with foxglove (which contains cardiac glycosides) for "dropsy", the old word for heart-failure edema. Keep foxglove away from dogs and children.

Board add-on Acute digoxin overdose causes hyperkalemia (pumps blocked), even though hypokalemia predisposes to toxicity. Hyperkalemia in poisoning is an indication for Fab. Typical toxic rhythms: PVCs, bidirectional VT, atrial tachycardia with AV block.

Q: A patient on digoxin is started on furosemide and a week later reports yellow halos and nausea. What is the first lab to check, and why?

Answer

Serum K⁺. The loop diuretic caused hypokalemia, and K⁺ competes with digoxin at the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Low K⁺ ↑ binding → toxicity.

Q: A stable patient on digoxin is prescribed oral erythromycin. Why might digoxin levels rise?

Answer

Erythromycin kills enteric bacteria that normally metabolise digoxin before absorption, so more drug is absorbed than the dose assumed.

⭐ Toxicity → physostigmine (crosses the BBB)

Slides 39–40 · muscarinic antagonist for bradycardia and AV block · the toxidrome

Atropine

AbbreviationsM1 / M2 = muscarinic receptor subtypes · BBB = blood–brain barrier · AV = atrioventricular · ACLS = Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support · AChE = acetylcholinesterase
  • A non-selective muscarinic antagonist that crosses the blood–brain barrier, so it acts on more than the heart.
  • Cardiac indications: symptomatic bradycardia and AV block. It blocks vagal (M2) input → ↑ heart rate.
  • Slide notes (ACLS bradycardia rhythms): sinus bradycardia, 1st-degree AV block, 2nd-degree types I (Wenckebach) and II, 3rd-degree (complete) block.
  • Symptomatic bradycardia: first-line atropine; second-line the catecholamines (sympathetic amines, the inotropes).
🧠 The paradoxical slowing

At low doses, atropine can slow the heart before the expected tachycardia. The slide explains this as M1 receptor blockade showing before the M2 blockade.

Q: After repeated atropine doses, a patient is febrile, flushed, dry, delirious and has dilated pupils. Which antidote, and why that one?

Answer

Physostigmine. It is an AChE inhibitor that crosses the blood–brain barrier, so it reverses central as well as peripheral signs. Pyridostigmine doesn't enter the CNS. Pralidoxime is for organophosphates.

Q: What is first-line for symptomatic bradycardia, and what comes next?

Answer

Atropine, then the catecholamines (sympathetic amines).

The Hockey Player & Summary Tables

A 24-year-old hockey player leaves the ice in distress
  • Racing heartbeat, chest tightness, dizziness → loses consciousness on the boards.
  • Pulse: rapid, bounding, too fast to count. 12-lead: rapid regular rhythm, narrow QRS, no P wave, 192/min.

🩺 Answers
  • Rhythm: narrow + regular + no P + 192 → PSVT (re-entry). Would propranolol "take us down from 192, unresponsive"? Not fast or hard enough. Adenosine or electricity.

  • Expected ECG: brief asystole, severe bradycardia or AV block, then sinus rhythm.
  • The clue that confirms it: the transient bronchoconstriction (stiff bag).

🎯 Antiarrhythmic Drugs — High-Yield One-Pager

Kyle R. Scully, PhD · OMK 2 Cardio · LO 1 classes and action potentials → LO 2 each drug → LO 3 the hockey-player case

LO 1 · ACTION POTENTIALS & CLASSES

Ions

  • Node: phase 4 If (Na⁺, opened by hyperpolarisation) · phase 0 Ca²⁺ · phase 3 K⁺
  • Myocyte: phase 0 Na⁺ · 1 Ito · 2 Ca²⁺ vs K⁺ plateau · 3 K⁺
  • β1 (Gs) and M2 (Gi) both act on adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA
  • Rate ↑ with steeper phase 4, lower threshold, less negative resting potential
  • EAD (long AP → torsades) · DAD (Ca²⁺ overload, NCX) · re-entry · block · bundle of Kent

Rate vs rhythm

  • Rate → nodal tissue → II and IV (+ digoxin, adenosine)
  • Rhythm → myocytes → I and III
  • Levers: max diastolic potential · phase 4 slope · threshold · AP duration
  • All antiarrhythmics are pro-arrhythmic at the wrong dose

Class I mnemonics

  • Phase 0 slowing: "salty CAB" C > A > B
  • Repolarisation (Goldilocks): A too big (↑) · B too short (↓) · C just right (no change)
  • Open/inactivated state binding = use-dependent block
LO 2 · CLASSES I–IV

IA — quinidine, procainamide, disopyramide

  • Na⁺ (open) + K⁺ block → ↑ ERP, ↓ re-entry; torsades
  • Quinidine: cinchonism, anticholinergic, diarrhea; CI myasthenia gravis, long QT; ↑ digoxin; low K⁺ ↓ efficacy
  • Procainamide: SVT/VT, acute MI; NAPA; renal; drug-induced lupus; no digoxin effect

IB — lidocaine, mexiletine · IC — flecainide, propafenone

  • IB: open + inactivated, fast off → ischemic tissue; 2nd-line ventricular; CNS/seizures, methemoglobinemia; CYP
  • IC: marked ↓ phase 0, QRS ↑ with exertion; VT, AF in a normal heart; pro-arrhythmic with ischemia or structural disease; blurred vision, ataxia

II — β-blockers

  • ↓ phase 4, ↑ AV repolarisation; sympathetically triggered arrhythmias, angina, post-MI
  • 1st propranolol · 2nd atenolol, metoprolol, esmolol (short; unstable) · 3rd carvedilol, labetalol (α1), nebivolol (NO)
  • Labetalol = pregnancy; propranolol OK in long QT
  • Bronchospasm, cold extremities, impotence · brady, block · nightmares (lipophilic)

III — ibutilide, dofetilide, sotalol, amiodarone

  • Block K⁺ → long plateau, ↑ QT → torsades (ibutilide 2%, dofetilide 1–3%) → inpatient start
  • Ibutilide terminates AF/AFL · dofetilide converts + maintains, OK with low EF, dose by CrCl · sotalol II + III
  • Amiodarone: unstable VT, recurrent VF, SVT; CI heart block, brady, shock, polymorphic VT with long QT
  • Amiodarone AEs: lung fibrosis, thyroid ↑/↓, LFTs, corneal deposits, blue-gray skin, photosensitivity, neuropathy → baseline + annual PFT, TFT, eye exam

IV — verapamil, diltiazem

  • Block L-type Ca²⁺ → ↓ AV conduction; PSVT, AF with RVR, HTN, angina
  • Brady, AV block, HF, hypotension; constipation (V), edema (D)
  • IV verapamil + β-blocker contraindicated; ↑ digoxin
LO 2–3 · OTHERS & THE CASE

Adenosine

  • P1 → Gi/o → K⁺ channels open → nodal hyperpolarisation → brief asystole / AV block
  • Narrow-complex PSVT after vagal manoeuvres (Valsalva, syringe blow, cold water, carotid massage)
  • Rapid push + flush ("hand grenade"); fails → repeat or cardiovert
  • Doom, flushing, chest pain, headache, bronchospasm ≤30 min in asthma

Digoxin

  • Blocks Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → ↑ Ca²⁺ (inotropy) · ↓ AV automaticity, ↑ vagal tone (rate)
  • HF, AF, AFL · narrow therapeutic index · renal
  • Low K⁺ → toxicity (check K⁺ first); high K⁺ → less effect
  • DDIs: quinidine, verapamil/diltiazem, erythromycin (gut flora), K⁺-wasting diuretics, BB/CCB
  • Toxicity: scooped ST, yellow-green halos, anorexia → normalise K⁺, digoxin Fab

Atropine

  • Non-selective muscarinic antagonist (crosses BBB) → symptomatic bradycardia, AV block (1st line; catecholamines 2nd)
  • Low dose can slow the heart first
  • Toxidrome: mad, dry, blind, red, hot → physostigmine (not pyridostigmine; pralidoxime = organophosphates)

Hockey player

  • Narrow, regular, no P, 192, syncope → PSVT → adenosine (or cardioversion)
  • Expect brief asystole / brady / AV block; stiff bag = adenosine bronchospasm
