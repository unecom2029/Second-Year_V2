Read the ECG — and the Patient

This is a clinical lecture from a Maine Med electrophysiologist, building on Dr. Qazi's EKG I (ECG Basics notes). His message: ECGs are learned by repetition, and they are always read with the clinical picture. Don't be discouraged if it doesn't click on the first pass.

🏝️

The "Love Island" rule

Alone, you get voted off the island. An ECG always needs a partner — the clinical history.

🎬

The "Good Will Hunting" rule

Will has read every book but never experienced anything. ECGs are read at the bedside. An ECG fed to AI or read hours later without the patient is often wrong.

Describe what you see — every time

  1. Fast or slow?Rate first.
  2. Narrow or wide?QRS < or ≥ 120 ms (3 small boxes).
  3. Is there a P wave with every QRS?Where is the P, and does it relate to the QRS?
  4. Are the intervals normal?PR, QRS, QT.
  5. AxisNormal, left, right — or "northwest"?
  6. Symptoms at the time of the ECG?Key for pericarditis and chest pain.
  7. Is the patient stable?This decides treatment.
  8. Prior history?Old ECGs, known heart disease, drugs.
  9. Put it togetherA differential diagnosis and a plan.
🔑 How ECG reads are scored — and what ABIM says
  • Think of each ECG as 10 points: ~8 for the clinical big picture (sinus? heart block? bundle branch block? VT? normal?) and ~2 for details like left atrial enlargement.
  • ABIM: identify only the findings that are definite and matter for management — "not an exercise in identifying minutiae."
🤖 "Not your parents' ECG" — AI
  • Research AI can already estimate EF or predict VT from an ECG. Expect it to matter in 5–10 years.
  • Most hospital reading software dates from the late '90s, and phone apps misread (Topic 6). AI won't read it for you yet.

Topic 1
⭐ Expect a normal and an artifact on the exam

Slides 8–12 · the healthy patient · early repolarisation · garbage in, garbage out

Normal Variants & Artifact

AbbreviationsOV = office visit · ER (here) = early repolarisation · Ito = transient outward K⁺ current (phase 1) · HCM = hypertrophic cardiomyopathy · LQTS = long QT syndrome · WPW = Wolff-Parkinson-White · QT = QT interval

"The hardest ECG you'll ever see is the normal one"

  • Case: a 28-year-old at 28 weeks' pregnancy with palpitations, seen in the office. PR, QRS and QT are all normal: a normal ECG.
  • The fear on an exam: "why are they showing me a healthy young woman?" Sometimes the answer really is normal.
  • Context matters: a routine office visit is not the trauma bay.
🧠 There isn't one normal ECG — there's a family of them
  • Juvenile T waves and early repolarisation in young people.
  • Athlete's ECGs can look "horrifying." When he screened a professional hockey team, none of the ECGs were textbook normal.
  • A "normal" person can also have undiagnosed disease the ECG hints at: HCM, long QT, WPW (Topics 8–9).

Early repolarisation — a mismatch between layers

  • The endocardium, mid-myocardial (M) cells and epicardium depolarise and repolarise at slightly different speeds.
  • In early repolarisation, the transient outward K⁺ current (Ito) is much bigger in the epicardium than the endocardium.
  • That makes a voltage gradient early in the AP → J-point elevation / J wave.
  • In young people it is usually a normal variant. The same mismatch underlies juvenile T waves.

Artifact — garbage in, garbage out

  • Case: an unresponsive 45-year-old. The tracing is artifact.
  • Causes: leads not attached properly, a shivering patient, a tremor from a neurologic condition.
  • The most important clinical move: repeat the ECG. The computer may still print a diagnosis — don't trust it.

Q: An ECG from an unresponsive patient shows chaotic high-frequency deflections in most leads, and the machine prints "VF?". The patient has a pulse. What next?

Answer

Suspect artifact — check the leads and repeat the ECG. Treat the patient, not the printout.

Q: What ion current explains the J-wave of early repolarisation?

Answer

A large transient outward K⁺ current (Ito) in the epicardium (relative to the endocardium). The mismatch creates a voltage gradient early in repolarisation.

Topic 2
⭐ "You never get away from the action potential"

Slides 14–19 · automaticity · triggered activity · re-entry · what else is going on

How Arrhythmias Start

AbbreviationsEAD / DAD = early / delayed afterdepolarisation · AP = action potential · AVNRT = atrioventricular nodal re-entrant tachycardia · VT / VF = ventricular tachycardia / fibrillation · ANS = autonomic nervous system · K⁺ = potassium · Ca²⁺ = calcium
MechanismWhat goes wrongClassic examples
Automaticity (enhanced or abnormal)A focus fires too fast or where it shouldn't — a phase 3/4 problemAtrial tachycardia (irritable focus), junctional ectopic tachycardia
Triggered activitySmall depolarisations during or just after repolarisation (phase 3 or 4) trigger a new APEAD → long QT → torsades · DAD → ischemic VT/VF
Re-entry — the most common, especially in SVTA short circuit between two pathways with different propertiesAVNRT, AVRT, flutter, scar VT

Board add-on DADs are the classic mechanism of digoxin toxicity (intracellular Ca²⁺ overload). EADs are worsened by hypokalemia and QT-prolonging drugs.

Re-entry: slow path vs fast path

🐢

Path 1 — slow

Slow conduction, short refractory period — ready again quickly.

🐇

Path 2 — fast

Fast conduction, long refractory period — needs longer to recover.

  1. A — normalA sinus impulse goes down both paths.
  2. B — early beatA premature impulse finds the fast path still refractory → unidirectional block, so it goes down the slow path.
  3. C — turn aroundBy the time it reaches the bottom, the fast path has recovered, so the impulse runs back up it.
  4. D — loopIt re-enters the slow path, and the circuit keeps spinning → tachycardia.

"What else is going on?" — variables that set up arrhythmias

  • Ischemia / infarct scar (changes the substrate) — boards love an arrhythmia plus an MI.
  • Electrolyte imbalance (K⁺, Mg²⁺).
  • Drug-induced proarrhythmia — digoxin causes a whole host of arrhythmias. It's used less today, but test-writers love it.
  • Autonomic nervous system: it changes QT, heart-rate variability, baroreflex sensitivity, catecholamines — and the slow/fast conduction itself.
  • Areas of slow and fast conduction.

Q: In a re-entry circuit, why does a premature beat start the tachycardia when a normal beat doesn't?

Answer

It arrives while the fast pathway (long refractory period) is still refractory → unidirectional block → conducts down the slow path → returns up the now-recovered fast path → a loop.

Q: Which afterdepolarisation underlies torsades in long QT, and during which AP phase does it occur?

Answer

Early afterdepolarisations, during a prolonged phase 2–3 (before repolarisation finishes).

SVT: AVNRT, AVRT & How to Break It

AbbreviationsSVT = supraventricular tachycardia · AT = atrial tachycardia · AFL = atrial flutter · AF = atrial fibrillation · AVNRT = AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia · JET = junctional ectopic tachycardia · AVRT = atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia (uses an accessory pathway) · WPW = Wolff-Parkinson-White · TIA = transient ischemic attack · CVA = cerebrovascular accident (stroke) · EP study = electrophysiology study · IV = intravenous · CCB = calcium channel blocker · SHD = structural heart disease

The family

RhythmWhereLecture pearls
Sinus tachycardiaSA nodeThe most common SVT — always ask why
Atrial tachycardiaOne irritable atrial focusP wave shape depends on where in the atrium it starts
Atrial flutter / AFRA circuit / many waveletsTopics 4–5
AVNRTSlow and fast pathways inside the AV node2nd most common arrhythmia (per lecture) · P hidden inside the QRS

🔑 Why typical AVNRT has no visible P wave

A premature beat goes down the slow pathway and up the fast one. The atria (retrograde) and ventricles are activated at the same time, so the P is buried in the QRS. A narrow regular tachycardia with no visible P is a very common tip-off for typical AVNRT.

Case: 65-year-old with cough, fever and "worse palpitations"

  • Palpitations "for years" (since age 18), worse with this cold.
  • Decreased breath sounds at the left base and wheezes; a 50-pack-year smoker.
  • No doctor "in years" — "if patients ever say that, you're in trouble." An urgent-care "blood pressure" medicine once didn't help.

Describe it: fast (~170–185; roughly two small boxes between complexes), narrow, no clear P waves (maybe a little something in lead III). Fast + narrow + no visible P → it's coming from above the ventricles: SVT.

Breaking it, step 1: vagal manoeuvres — carotid sinus massage

  • If the patient is stable with a good blood pressure, try carotid massage first.
  • Pressure on the carotid sinus fires a parasympathetic (vagal) reflex, which slows AV-node conduction and can break AVNRT. "Patients think you're a wizard — it's just physiology."
  • Technique: listen to both carotids first, then press firmly for 10–20 s, with ECG, heart rate and blood pressure monitoring.
⚠️ Recording slip: carotid sinus, not carotid body

The recording says the "carotid body" has parasympathetic tone. The massage actually works on the carotid sinus baroreceptors (CN IX afferent → vagal efferent). The carotid body is a chemoreceptor. See the Physiology notes, Topic 10.

Absolute contraindicationsRelative
MI · TIA or stroke in the last 3 months · carotid artery occlusion · history of VF or VT · previous bad reaction to carotid massageCarotid bruit → evaluate first (carotid ultrasound). Stenosis or atheroma = risk of occluding the other side or embolising plaque. Get informed consent.

Step 2: adenosine

Dose

Rapid bolus + flush

6 mg, then 12 mg if no response in 1–2 min. Half-life <5 s, so it doesn't accumulate.

Side effects

Common → uncommon

Facial flushing, chest pain, hypotension (it's a vasodilator), a few seconds of asystole; bronchospasm; non-sustained VT.

Don't use

Contraindication / cautions

Heart transplant (denervated heart → prolonged asystole). WPW: it can trigger AF that degenerates to VF. Caution in severe reactive airway disease and ischemic heart disease.

  • Adenosine acts mainly at the AV node, slowing conduction until the circuit breaks.
  • As it wears off (~30 s) you often see a Wenckebach-like pattern: the PR lengthens, then a beat drops.
  • Board add-on The recording says adenosine works by an "uptick in cAMP." In fact the A1 receptor is Gi-coupled: ↓cAMP and ↑K⁺ efflux → hyperpolarisation of the AV node.

The algorithms

  • Acute, regular SVT: vagal manoeuvres and/or adenosine (class I).
  • If they fail and the patient is stable: IV β-blocker, diltiazem or verapamil (all slow the AV node).
  • If that fails, or the patient is unstable: synchronised cardioversion.
  • Sedate well, then shock. Guidelines list 50–100 J; he says "go big or go home" — being shocked is brutal.
  • Long term: ablation candidate? → EP study + catheter ablation (class I), done from the groin, modifying the AVNRT slow pathway. Or medical therapy with AV-nodal blockers.
  • Flecainide or propafenone only if there's no structural heart disease.
  • Young patients often choose pills first, then come back for ablation after 2–3 years of ER visits.

Q: A stable 30-year-old has a narrow regular tachycardia at 180 with no visible P waves. Vagal manoeuvres fail. Next step, dose, and what might the monitor show as it wears off?

Answer

Adenosine 6 mg rapid IV push with a flush (12 mg if needed). As it wears off, transient AV block (PR lengthening, then a dropped beat) before sinus rhythm.

Q: A heart-transplant recipient develops SVT. Why avoid adenosine?

Answer

The transplanted heart is denervated and supersensitive to adenosine → risk of prolonged asystole.

Q: Name three absolute contraindications to carotid sinus massage.

Answer

Any three: MI; TIA or stroke in the last 3 months; carotid occlusion; history of VT or VF; a previous adverse reaction. A bruit is relative — image the carotids first.

Topic 4
⭐ "You will not escape AF"

Slides 31–41 · recognition · definitions · mechanisms · triggers · stroke risk · rate vs rhythm

Atrial Fibrillation

AbbreviationsAF / Afib = atrial fibrillation · RR = interval between successive R waves · CHA₂DS₂-VASc = stroke-risk score (Congestive heart failure, Hypertension, Age ≥75 ×2, Diabetes, Stroke/TIA ×2, Vascular disease, Age 65–74, Sex category female) · CHA₂DS₂-VA = the same without the sex point · HF = heart failure · MI = myocardial infarction · TSH = thyroid-stimulating hormone · RVR = rapid ventricular response · DOAC = direct oral anticoagulant · ICU / MICU = (medical) intensive care unit

Case: 45-year-old with palpitations after heavy drinking

  • Irregular RR, narrow QRS, and no two "P waves" look the same.
  • Irregular + clean narrow complexes → probably AF. On exams it's a relatively easy ECG: they won't give you a weird atypical one.

Board add-on AF after a binge is called "holiday heart."

ECG features

  • Irregularly irregular rhythm; no isoelectric baseline; no P waves.
  • Variable ventricular rate — fast or slow.
  • QRS usually <120 ms, unless there's a pre-existing bundle branch block, an accessory pathway or rate-related aberrancy.
  • Fibrillatory waves: fine (<0.5 mm) or coarse (>0.5 mm). They can mimic P waves and cause misdiagnosis.

AF is everywhere

  • The most common arrhythmia in the US and the world — surgery (post-op AF), primary care, OB, ICU.
  • Wearables have changed the game. They flag AF from RR irregularity, so premature beats can fool them.
  • The Apple Watch's lowest burden category is "<2 %" — a legal choice, so it never says "no AF."

Definitions — know the first three for the wards

An episode currently counts as AF at ≥30 s. He expects that cut-off to be replaced by a burden definition within ~2 years.

Mechanisms — why there's no 100 % cure

🧑

Young

Triggered activity from the pulmonary veins, which are rich in autonomic nerve tissue. AF here is often a nervous-system or genetic disease (long QT and Brugada can cause AF). Ablation "wraps the veins in electrical tape."

👴

Older

Hypertension, diabetes and valve disease remodel the atrium into a "maze" with scar and autonomic change, so impulses get lost and exit many ways. After 100–150,000 beats a day for decades, ablation can't cure it all.

Triggers and causes (not all)

  • Ischemic heart disease. Hypertension: years of high afterload → LVH → the atria pump against a stiff ventricle, distend and scar.
  • Valve disease, especially mitral stenosis or regurgitation.
  • Electrolytes (low K⁺ or Mg²⁺ after a big night, a viral illness, no fluids) — reversible.
  • Acute infection. Thyrotoxicosis: rapid AF with a suppressed TSH, often reversible, treated with β-blockers.
  • Sympathomimetic drugs. Alcohol (#3 risk factor): direct myocardial toxin plus autonomic effects.
  • PE (distends the RV → RA), pericardial disease, acid–base disturbance, pre-excitation, cardiomyopathy, obesity, diabetes.
⚠️ Board favourites

Test-writers love AF + ST elevation (an MI) — rare in real life — and AF + thyrotoxicosis.

Symptoms — it's not mainly palpitations

Fatigue and shortness of breath are the most common symptoms — heart-failure-type complaints ("can't climb a flight of stairs"). Only ~50–60 % feel palpitations.

Acute management, step by step

  1. Diagnose itIrregular RR, P waves that don't match.
  2. How long?1 hour vs 2 weeks changes everything.
  3. Anticoagulation assessmentCHA₂DS₂-VASc (moving to CHA₂DS₂-VA).
  4. Rate or rhythm controlUsually rate control first.
  5. Treat the underlying causeK⁺ of 2? Thyroid off? Infection?

🧮 CHA₂DS₂-VASc calculator

Tick what applies. Lecture rule: a score of 2 or more usually means a blood thinner. The guideline thresholds are a Board add-on.

  • The two big dangers of AF: stroke and heart failure.
  • A young person in AF for a couple of hours with no risk factors: rate-control or sedate and cardiovert — no long-term blood thinner.
  • Long-term anticoagulation depends on risk factors, duration and whether you plan to cardiovert.
  • Female sex is a "risk modifier," not a big independent risk factor; the next guideline drops it (CHA₂DS₂-VA).

Long-term: treat the whole patient, then usually rhythm control

  • Treat HF, hypertension and diabetes; cut alcohol; address obesity; screen for sleep apnoea. This alone can cut episodes a lot.
  • The AFFIRM trial (late '90s) found rate and rhythm control equivalent.
  • Today rhythm control is favoured long term for most symptomatic patients or those with reduced heart function. Rate control is reasonable in older patients with many comorbidities.
✅ Hemodynamics: why AF hurts sick patients
  • AF is common in the medical ICU (up to 15 % of patients).
  • Losing the atrial kick costs preload — he quotes ~15 % of ventricular filling. (Dr. Morganelli's physiology lecture says ~10 % at rest; both mean "small but it matters.")
  • A stiff ventricle (sepsis, LVH) is especially preload-dependent, and a fast rate shortens filling time.
  • A persistently high rate → tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy.
  • "AF comes out at your worst": with a cold, pneumonia or appendicitis. High sympathetic tone makes the rate hard to control.

Q: A 34-year-old has new AF at 140/min, tremor, weight loss and a suppressed TSH. Most likely trigger, and first-line rate control?

Answer

Thyrotoxicosis — often reversible AF. Rate control with a β-blocker while the thyroid is treated.

Q: AF that stopped on its own after 5 days, vs AF continuous for 3 weeks that needed cardioversion — classify each.

Answer

Paroxysmal (ends within 7 days) vs persistent (>7 days, needed intervention).

Q: A 72-year-old woman with hypertension and diabetes has AF. CHA₂DS₂-VASc score and management?

Answer

Age 65–74 (1) + HTN (1) + DM (1) + female (1) = 4 (CHA₂DS₂-VA = 3) → anticoagulate (usually a DOAC).

Q: What is the most common presenting symptom of AF?

Answer

Fatigue and shortness of breath — not palpitations, which only ~half of patients notice.

Topic 5
⭐ Negative sawtooth in II, III, aVF · positive V1

Slides 42–51 · recognising flutter · why it's fast · the isthmus · ablation · anticoagulation

Atrial Flutter

AbbreviationsAFL = atrial flutter · AF = atrial fibrillation · CTI = cavotricuspid isthmus · IVC = inferior vena cava · RA = right atrium · PFA = pulsed-field ablation · RFA = radiofrequency ablation · ED = emergency department

Case: 56-year-old in the ED with a stroke

  • QRS borderline narrow (~120 ms). The "P waves" all look the same — a sawtooth pattern in the inferior leads.
  • Diagnosis: typical atrial flutter.
  • Flutter and AF are usually "a family": the same atria also have flutter, AF, premature atrial beats and focal firing.

Adenosine is diagnostic here, not a cure

  • In a fast narrow tachycardia with P waves hidden (flutter? AVNRT?), adenosine blocks the AV node for a few seconds and unmasks the flutter waves.
  • It won't stop flutter. In this patient, 6 then 12 mg just made the QRS complexes "peter out."
  • Adenosine breaks AVNRT, which uses the AV node, but not every SVT.

Why flutter is often faster than AF

Atrial rateWhat the AV node doesVentricular rate
AF360–450+, chaoticBombarded; conducts irregularly~120–150, often controllable with AV-nodal blockers
Typical flutter250–300, organised"I can handle half of those" → locks into 2:1~150, fixed → days of this → tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy

Typical (type I) flutter — the right-atrial circuit

  • A macro-re-entry circuit counterclockwise around the tricuspid valve, running up the muscular septum.
  • It needs the cavotricuspid isthmus (tissue between the IVC and the tricuspid annulus) → "isthmus-dependent."
  • Type II (atypical) = any other undulating flutter pattern.
🔑 Why the flutter waves point the way they do
  • An electrode sees current coming toward it as positive.
  • The wave climbs the thick septum away from the feet → negative flutter waves in II, III, aVF.
  • It climbs toward V1, which sits high on the right chest → positive in V1.
  • The thin lateral wall carries too little muscle to register much on the way back down.

Long term: ablate the isthmus

🚧 The traffic-circle analogy

Flutter is like getting stuck in the Malden traffic circle on the way to Logan — you just go around and around. Ablation drops a Jersey barrier into the circle: a line of cautery from the tricuspid valve to the IVC, and the impulse can't loop any more.

  • Catheter ablation is the usual long-term plan, even after one episode, because flutter runs so fast.
  • Pulsed-field ablation of the CTI: 99.2 % acute bidirectional block overall; 93.8 % with PFA alone; 3.8 % recurrence over a median of 114 days.
  • It doesn't prevent AF: about half of typical-flutter patients go on to AF within ~2 years. Warn them.
⚠️ Test trap: "it's only flutter"

Flutter gets the same anticoagulation as AF — use the same CHA₂DS₂-VASc risk profile (guideline class 1).

Q: A regular narrow tachycardia at exactly 150/min with negative sawtooth waves in II, III and aVF. Diagnosis, circuit, and definitive therapy?

Answer

Typical (counterclockwise) atrial flutter with 2:1 block — a right-atrial circuit around the tricuspid annulus through the cavotricuspid isthmus. Definitive therapy: CTI catheter ablation. Anticoagulate as for AF.

Q: Adenosine given for a narrow tachycardia doesn't terminate it; regular sawtooth waves appear during transient AV block. What does that tell you?

Answer

The rhythm doesn't depend on the AV node — it's atrial flutter. Adenosine was diagnostic, not therapeutic.

Topic 6
⭐ Wide + ugly = VT until proven otherwise

Slides 52–77 · why VT is wide · clinical clues · AV dissociation & capture beats · concordance · axis · Brugada steps · cases

Wide-Complex Tachycardia: VT vs SVT

AbbreviationsWCT = wide-complex tachycardia · VT = ventricular tachycardia · SVT = supraventricular tachycardia · BBB / LBBB / RBBB = (left / right) bundle branch block · aberrancy = SVT conducted with a bundle-branch pattern · AV dissociation = atria and ventricles firing independently · VA conduction = ventriculo-atrial (retrograde) conduction · RS = an R wave followed by an S wave · MVP = mitral valve prolapse · CAD = coronary artery disease · TCA = tricyclic antidepressant · SOB = shortness of breath

Why VT is wide

  • Normal beats ride the His–Purkinje "superhighway" → both ventricles activate at once → narrow QRS (<120 ms).
  • VT starts in the ventricular muscle (a focus, or a circuit around scar). It skips the conduction system and creeps cell to cell → QRS ≥120 ms, slurred and "ugly."
  • A bundle branch block is also cell-to-cell for part of the ventricle, so SVT + BBB is also wide. That's what makes WCT hard.
⚠️ The rule that is "constantly on the test"
  • If it's wide and you can't tell, assume VT, treat it as VT, figure it out later.
  • Unstable → sedate and cardiovert (ACLS).
  • "Nobody will ever mark you wrong for treating SVT-with-LBBB as VT."
  • Having a baseline ECG (to know if they already have a BBB) helps enormously.

The clinical scenario favours VT when there is…

  • Organic heart disease — e.g. EF 20 % (normal 55–65 %), old infarct scar.
  • Hypo- or hyperkalemia — hypokalemia is worse for ventricular irritability.
  • Hypoxia (infarct, COPD), acidosis, MVP or other valve disease.
  • Drugs: digoxin toxicity, antiarrhythmics, phenothiazines, TCAs, caffeine, alcohol, nicotine.
  • VT is occasionally seen in structurally normal hearts (behaves more like SVT). But if you don't know the history, treat it as life-threatening.

For now, he says, focus on capture/fusion beats and AV dissociation. The rest is for later.

🧠 Initial deflection: fast = uses the highway, slurred = doesn't
  • SVT with BBB starts on the normal conduction system, so the first part of the QRS is sharp and only the later part slurs.
  • VT is slow and slurred from the very first millisecond.

More VT facts: VT can be irregular at first (~70 % become regular by the 20th beat), so regularity doesn't rule it out. VT can also conduct back to the atria (VA conduction) — look at those P waves carefully.

The Brugada stepwise approach

🪜 VT-vs-SVT stepper (Brugada 1991)

Answer each step in order. The first "yes" diagnoses VT. It was validated in 554 wide tachycardias (sensitivity 0.987, specificity 0.965).

Start at step 1.

Measure R-to-S from the onset of the R to the nadir (deepest point) of the S in any precordial lead. Over 100 ms → VT. Wellens' morphology table ("I don't expect you to know this, nor would it be tested") is for your clinical years:

Favours VTFavours SVT with aberrancy
Fusion / capture beats · AV dissociation · extreme ("northwest") axis · concordance · long QRS · no precordial RS · slow initial depolarisation · V1 & V6 morphologies · the clinical situationStarted by a P wave · RP <0.1 s · fast initial depolarisation · RBBB pattern whose initial QRS looks like the patient's normal beats · same BBB on the baseline ECG

Cases from the lecture

  • Wide tachycardia — not all negative, not all positive, "rabbit ears," and the precordial complexes go down quickly.
  • In sinus rhythm the QRS is still wide with the same LBBB shape → the tachycardia was SVT with LBBB.
  • Treating it as VT would still have been correct.
  • 64-year-old, presyncope, structurally normal heart: wide and ugly, R-to-S ~110–120 ms, some dissociated P waves.
  • One narrow complex = a capture beat: sinus rhythm (~70) sneaks through while the VT circuit (~160) resets → VT.

59-year-old with CAD and shortness of breath: very wide, ugly complexes, R-to-S ≈ 140 ms → VT. After cardioversion: sinus rhythm with a mildly wide QRS.

67-year-old, short of breath and lightheaded: QRS ~200 ms in V5–6, northwest axis (−120°) (very positive in aVR), no fast onset anywhere, and Brugada's sign (QRS onset to S nadir >100 ms) → VT.

Q: During a wide regular tachycardia at 160, one narrow complex appears that looks like the patient's normal beats. What is it, and what does it prove?

Answer

A capture beat — a sinus impulse got through the AV node while the VT circuit was resetting. It proves AV dissociation → VT.

Q: A stable 70-year-old with a prior MI and EF 25 % has a wide regular tachycardia. The QRS is negative in V1–V6. Most likely rhythm?

Answer

VT. Negative precordial concordance is virtually diagnostic, and scar with a low EF sets up re-entrant VT.

Q: Step 2 of the Brugada algorithm: what do you measure, and what cut-off means VT?

Answer

From the onset of the R to the nadir of the S in any precordial lead: >100 ms = VT.

Slides 78–81 · a wide ugly rhythm that isn't VT · class IC drugs · 1:1 flutter

Antiarrhythmic Drug Effect

AbbreviationsIC = Vaughan-Williams class IC antiarrhythmics (flecainide, propafenone) · AFL = atrial flutter · RBBB = right bundle branch block · LAFB = left anterior fascicular block · use-dependence = more drug effect at faster heart rates

Case: 42-year-old athlete in the ED, sweating, known flutter "on meds"

  • The machine read "VT, rate 250."
  • But this QRS deflects rapidly at its onset, and the history is flutter on an antiarrhythmic.
  • Answers from the slide: atrial flutter · antiarrhythmic drug effect · complete RBBB · left anterior fascicular block ("tough call").
🔑 How a class IC drug creates this picture
  • Class IC (flecainide, propafenone) markedly slow phase 0 (Na⁺ channel block) → a wide QRS.
  • They are use-dependent: more block at faster rates, so the QRS widens further in tachycardia.
  • They also slow the flutter circuit, so the AV node can now conduct 1:1.

Always give an AV-nodal blocker with a class IC drug. Class IA/IB drugs (procainamide, mexiletine) also act on phase 0.

Q: A patient on flecainide for flutter presents with a regular wide tachycardia at 250. Mechanism, and how could it have been prevented?

Answer

1:1 atrial flutter with use-dependent QRS widening: flecainide slowed the flutter enough for 1:1 AV conduction. Prevent it with an AV-nodal blocker (β-blocker or CCB) given alongside.

Topic 8
⭐ "Highly tested — and life-threatening in a young person"

Slides 82–92 · pre-excited AF · the toll booth · pattern vs syndrome · associations · management

Wolff-Parkinson-White

AbbreviationsWPW = Wolff-Parkinson-White · AP = accessory pathway (bundle of Kent) · AVRT = atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia (orthodromic = down the AV node / up the AP, narrow; antidromic = down the AP, wide) · AF / AFL = atrial fibrillation / flutter · SCD = sudden cardiac death · EP study = electrophysiology study · HCM = hypertrophic cardiomyopathy · MVP = mitral valve prolapse · CCB = calcium channel blocker

Case: 25-year-old with palpitations while driving

  • Years of self-limited palpitations. He was driving behind his father (an EMT) when the palpitations didn't stop and he felt lightheaded.
  • ECG: wide, bizarre, irregular — "this makes no sense."
  • A young person with a crazy, wide, irregular ECG → think WPW with pre-excited AF.
  • At a small ED he was given AV-nodal blockers → the rate rose to ~227–300. (His BP stayed 125/60, which is unusual.)

The toll booth and the express lane

🚧

AV node = toll booth

"The most passive-aggressive structure you'll ever see": the faster the atria fire, the more it slows. Usually ~150–160 max (maybe 200 in an 18–25-year-old).

🏎️

Accessory pathway = no toll booth

Conducts everything it gets. In AF, block the AV node and more impulses go down the pathway instead → very fast → VF.

  • Bypassing the AV node removes the isoelectric PR segment → short PR.
  • Early ventricular activation creeps cell to cell → a slurred upstroke = the delta wave ("pre-excitation") → a wider QRS.
  • Orthodromic AVRT (down the node, up the pathway) is narrow. Antidromic (down the pathway) is wide.

Pattern vs syndrome

DefinitionNumbers from the slides
WPW patternPre-excitation on the ECG, no symptoms0.15–0.25 % of the population (0.55 % in first-degree relatives) · can be intermittent or disappear with age
WPW syndromePattern plus arrhythmiaAviation study (22,500): pattern 0.25 %, only 1.8 % of those had arrhythmia · ~1 %/yr arrhythmia over 22 years
  • Arrhythmias in the syndrome: ~80 % AVRT, 15–30 % AF, ~5 % flutter.
  • Sudden-death risk 0–0.39 %, especially low in asymptomatic patients. Multiple pathways (up to 13 %) = high risk.
  • Symptomatic (palpitations, lightheadedness) → EP study to test how fast the pathway conducts. Asymptomatic → can watch, with a yearly ECG.
🧠 Associations
  • Most patients have structurally normal hearts.
  • When there is congenital disease it's more often right-sided: Ebstein anomaly (tricuspid valve displaced down into the RV) is the lesion most strongly tied to WPW.
  • MVP ↔ left-sided pathways. Familial WPW ↔ HCM.
  • Some inheritance, but not Mendelian.

Management — the one thing to know

⚠️ No AV-nodal blockers in WPW
  • No adenosine, no calcium channel blockers, no β-blockers — and no vagal manoeuvres.
  • This reverses the usual SVT algorithm, which is why it's tested.
  • When in doubt, sedate and cardiovert.
  • Drug of choice: procainamide.

Procainamide

Dose

30 mg/min infusion to a max of 17 mg/kg, then 2–4 mg/min maintenance.

Stop the infusion if

Endpoints

The arrhythmia is suppressed · hypotension · QRS widens by ≥50 %.

Adverse effects

Watch for

Hypotension, QRS widening, torsades.

Q: A 22-year-old has an irregular, very fast, wide-complex tachycardia whose QRS width varies beat to beat. BP is stable. Which drugs are contraindicated, and what do you give?

Answer

Pre-excited AF (WPW). Contraindicated: adenosine, CCBs (diltiazem, verapamil), β-blockers and vagal manoeuvres. Give procainamide, or sedate and cardiovert. Cardiovert right away if unstable.

Q: Which congenital heart lesion is most strongly associated with WPW?

Answer

Ebstein anomaly (a right-sided lesion; tricuspid valve displaced into the RV).

Q: A pilot applicant has a delta wave on a screening ECG and no symptoms. Pattern or syndrome, and next step?

Answer

WPW pattern (no arrhythmia). Low risk: observe with a yearly ECG and teach the warning symptoms. An EP study is for symptomatic patients or high-risk situations.

Long QT & Genetic Arrhythmias

AbbreviationsQT / QTc = QT interval / heart-rate-corrected QT · LQTS = long QT syndrome (LQT1–3 = main genotypes) · SQTS = short QT syndrome · TdP = torsades de pointes · AED = automated external defibrillator · ARVC = arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy · HCM = hypertrophic cardiomyopathy · SSRI = selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor · SCD = sudden cardiac death · Mg²⁺ = magnesium

Case: 24-year-old runner who "went down with a seizure"

  • Nothing looks the same; a sinusoidal up-and-down pattern with the axis "twisting around a point" = torsades de pointes.
  • Rate ~one big box per beat ≈ 300/min.
  • The "seizure" was cerebral hypoperfusion. Patients in cardiac arrest often seize.

After an AED shock at the stadium: PR and QRS normal, but the QT is ~600 ms (the recording says "QRS" once — he meant QT).

QTc upper limits

Varies by textbook

Women 460–470 ms · men 450–460 ms. Women's QT runs longer.

QT-prolonging drugs

Common culprits

Anti-emetics, antifungals, antidepressants (SSRIs). On top of genetic LQTS they can trigger torsades and sudden death.

Congenital long QT syndrome

  • ~1 in 5,000; mostly autosomal dominant.
  • >15 genes on the slide (he says 19 now); LQT1–3 cover ~85–90 %.
  • Most are K⁺-channel problems in phase 2–3 → a long AP → EADs → torsades.
  • Presents with recurrent syncope ("they just go down" — not subtle) and sudden cardiac death.

Schwartz score per the slide notes: 1 point = low probability, 2–3 = intermediate, ≥4 = high. It combines the ECG, clinical history and family history.

Board add-on Acute torsades treatment: IV magnesium sulfate; cardioversion if unstable. Correct K⁺ and Mg²⁺ and stop QT-prolonging drugs.

Genetic arrhythmic conditions — think of these after a young VT/VF arrest

Q: A 19-year-old faints while swimming. The ECG shows QTc 520 ms; an uncle died suddenly at 25. Diagnosis, mechanism of the fatal rhythm, and drugs to avoid?

Answer

Congenital long QT syndrome (usually a K⁺-channel defect, AD). A long phase 2–3 → EADs → torsades de pointes. Avoid QT-prolonging drugs: anti-emetics, antifungals, SSRIs and others.

Q: Which inherited condition causes fibro-fatty replacement of the right ventricle?

Answer

Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC).

The Pericarditis Mimic

AbbreviationsSTE = ST-segment elevation · PR = PR segment · STEMI = ST-elevation myocardial infarction · TB = tuberculosis · TWI = T-wave inversion · aVR = augmented lead, right arm
  • Case: 35-year-old with chest pain. It looks normal, except the ST segments are up.
  • "Holy — ST elevation, is she having a heart attack?" The problem: it's everywhere, not in one coronary territory.
  • And the PR segment is depressed (clearly in V5–V6).

The history decides it: sharp pain (not pressure), worse with a deep breath and lying flat, better sitting up and leaning forward. That is pericarditis. Pressure-like pain points more to an MI.

Diagnosis

≥2 of 4

① Typical pleuritic chest pain · ② Pericardial friction rub · ③ Widespread STE or PR depression · ④ New or worse pericardial effusion on echo

Causes

Many

Viral / idiopathic (developed countries), TB (developing), bacterial, fungal, post-MI, trauma, cancer, renal failure, radiation, autoimmune, drugs (phenytoin, rifampin)

ECG stages

Over weeks

① Diffuse STE ± PR depression → ② ST and PR normalise → ③ diffuse TWI with isoelectric ST (weeks 2–4) → ④ normal again (1–2 months)

🧠 Concave or convex?
  • Textbooks say the STE in pericarditis is concave ("saddle"). He's seen plenty that don't follow the rule.
  • Lean on distribution (diffuse, not one territory), PR depression and the history.
  • Board add-on Also favouring pericarditis over STEMI: PR elevation in aVR and no reciprocal ST depression (except in aVR/V1).

Q: A 30-year-old has sharp chest pain, worse lying down and better leaning forward, two weeks after a viral illness. The ECG shows diffuse ST elevation with PR depression. Diagnosis, and which feature argues against STEMI?

Answer

Acute (viral) pericarditis. The ST elevation is diffuse — not in one coronary territory — with PR depression and positional pleuritic pain.

APPROACH · MECHANISMS · SVT

Read it the same way every time

  • Fast/slow · narrow/wide · P for every QRS · intervals · axis · symptoms · stable? · history → differential
  • ~8/10 points = the big picture; always pair with the history
  • Normal variants: juvenile T, early repol (epicardial Ito → J wave), athletes
  • Artifact → repeat the ECG

Mechanisms

  • Automaticity · triggered (EAD → long QT/torsades; DAD → ischemia, digoxin) · re-entry = most common
  • Re-entry: slow path = short refractory; fast path = long refractory; early beat blocks fast → down slow → up fast

SVT family

  • Sinus tach = most common SVT · AVNRT: down slow/up fast → P hidden in QRS · AVRT = accessory pathway · JET = kids
  • Stable: vagal (carotid sinus, 10–20 s; not with MI, TIA/CVA <3 mo, occlusion, VT/VF history, bruit → US first) → adenosine 6 → 12 mg → IV BB/diltiazem/verapamil → sync cardioversion
  • Unstable: sedate + sync cardioversion
  • Adenosine: flushing, chest pain, hypotension, brief asystole, bronchospasm; avoid in heart transplant and WPW
  • Long term: EP study + ablation (class I) or AV-nodal blockers
ATRIAL FIBRILLATION · FLUTTER

AF

  • Irregularly irregular, no P / baseline, variable rate, usually narrow
  • Paroxysmal ≤7 d · persistent >7 d + intervention · long-standing >12 mo · permanent = stop trying
  • Young: pulmonary-vein triggers (ANS) · old: remodelled "maze" → no 100 % cure
  • Triggers: HTN, CAD, valves, K⁺/Mg²⁺, infection, thyrotoxicosis, alcohol, PE, sympathomimetics, OSA, obesity. Boards: AF + MI, AF + thyroid
  • Most common symptoms: fatigue and SOB (palpitations only ~50–60 %)
  • Steps: diagnose → duration → CHA₂DS₂-VASc (≥2 → anticoagulate) → rate first → treat the cause; long term rhythm control favoured
  • Loss of kick (~15 %) + tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy

Typical flutter

  • Counterclockwise around the tricuspid annulus via the cavotricuspid isthmus
  • Negative sawtooth II, III, aVF; positive V1
  • Atria 250–300 → 2:1 → locked at ~150 (AF atria 360–450, ventricles ~120–150)
  • Adenosine unmasks, doesn't break flutter (breaks AVNRT)
  • CTI ablation ("Jersey barrier"); ~50 % later get AF; anticoagulate like AF
WIDE · WPW · LONG QT · PERICARDITIS

VT vs SVT

  • VT wide because cell to cell (no His–Purkinje) · unsure → treat as VT
  • Favours VT: AV dissociation (25–33 %), capture/fusion (~5 %), negative concordance (virtually diagnostic), northwest axis, QRS >140/160, slurred onset; clinical: EF↓, scar, K⁺, hypoxia, drugs
  • Brugada: no RS anywhere → R-to-S >100 ms → AV dissociation → morphology
  • SVT + BBB: fast initial deflection; same BBB in sinus rhythm
  • Class IC (flecainide, propafenone): slow phase 0, use-dependent → wide QRS, 1:1 flutter → give an AV-nodal blocker

WPW

  • Short PR + delta wave; AV node = toll booth, pathway = none
  • Pre-excited AF: wide, bizarre, irregular in a young patient
  • No adenosine, CCB, β-blocker or vagal → procainamide (30 mg/min, max 17 mg/kg; stop if hypotension or QRS ↑50 %) or cardiovert
  • Pattern 0.15–0.25 %; syndrome: 80 % AVRT; Ebstein; symptomatic → EP study

Long QT · genetic · pericarditis

  • Torsades (~300, twisting) → "seizure"; QTc limit ♀ 460–470 / ♂ 450–460
  • LQTS 1:5,000, AD, K⁺ channels, LQT1–3; drugs: anti-emetics, antifungals, SSRIs
  • Young VT/VF arrest: LQTS, SQTS, WPW, Brugada (RVOT), ARVC (fibro-fatty RV), HCM (most common)
  • Pericarditis: diffuse STE + PR depression, pleuritic and positional pain; ≥2 of 4 criteria; 4 stages over 1–2 months
