Treat to Live vs Treat to Feel

The lecture focuses on heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), plus the one drug class that works in both HFrEF and HFpEF: the SGLT2 inhibitors. The impaired cardiac function is irreversible, so every drug either prolongs life (slows the disease) or relieves symptoms (edema, congestion, low output). Know which is which.

Why the Failing Heart Gets Worse

AbbreviationsCO = cardiac output · SV = stroke volume · O₂ = oxygen · PV loop = pressure–volume loop · EDPVR = end-diastolic pressure–volume relationship · AT II = angiotensin II · ANP / BNP = atrial / B-type natriuretic peptide · RAAS = renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system

The determinants of cardiac function (slide 5)

DeterminantWhat it isWhy it worsens HF
PreloadVentricular filling↑ preload → ↑ myocardial O₂ demand → worse failure. Supply can't always rise to match, and less so as the disease advances.
AfterloadImpedance to LV ejection (the pressure the ventricle must beat to open the aortic valve)↑ afterload requires ↑ contractility to keep CO → ↑ O₂ demand. As the muscle weakens, SV and CO fall.
ContractilityForce of contraction↑ SV to overcome impedance, but directly ↑ O₂ demand. It is one of the first things to fail.
Heart rate—Extreme rates impair CO.
RemodellingWall stress → hypertrophy↓ LV compliance → worse performance.
🧠 Compliance on a PV loop

A stiff, hypertrophied ventricle shows the bottom (filling) limb of the PV loop kicking up: pressure rises more for each mL of filling. "Here's 4 PV loops — pick the one that demonstrates cardiac remodelling and hypertrophy" is the kind of physiology question this pharmacology rests on.

The neurohumoral cycle (slide 6)

  1. Compromised cardiac function → ↓ arterial pressureThe baroreceptor reflex fires.
  2. ↑ Sympathetic outflowAcutely, α-mediated vasoconstriction and tachycardia.
  3. β1 → ↑ renin → ↑ angiotensin IIAT II vasoconstricts → ↑ afterload.
  4. AT II → ↑ aldosterone → Na⁺ retention → volume expansion→ ↑ preload.
  5. ↑ afterload + ↑ preload → ↑ O₂ demand → worsening failureA spiral: the compensation progresses the disease.

Why the patient swells (slide 7)

  • Atrial stretch should release ANP ("try to make you pee") to cut preload. But chronically dilated atria resist natriuretic peptide, so the response fails.
  • The kidney still senses low pressure and keeps reabsorbing Na⁺ and water. Hydrostatic pressure rises, fluid transudates into tissue (pulmonary and peripheral edema), pressure drops again, and the kidney reabsorbs more.

Q: Trace how β1 stimulation in a failing heart ends up increasing both afterload and preload.

Answer

β1 on juxtaglomerular cells → ↑ renin → ↑ AT II → vasoconstriction (↑ afterload) and ↑ aldosterone → Na⁺/water retention (↑ preload). Both raise O₂ demand and worsen the failure.

Q: Why doesn't the natriuretic-peptide system rescue a patient with chronic HF?

Answer

Chronically dilated chambers become resistant to natriuretic peptide, so the kidney keeps reabsorbing Na⁺ despite volume overload.

⭐ "We have to do just about all of those things"

Slides 8–11 · where the drugs cut the cycle · preload vs afterload vs inotropes · outcomes · combinations

The Pharmacologic Approach

AbbreviationsACE = angiotensin-converting enzyme · AT II = angiotensin II · PDE = phosphodiesterase · ARNI = angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor · MRA = mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist · SGLT2 = sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 · BNP = B-type natriuretic peptide

Slide notes: the drugs modulate the neurohumoral systems switched on by the failing heart. RAAS can be blocked at three points: β-blockers (↓ renin release), ACE inhibitors (↓ AT II) and spironolactone (blocks aldosterone at its receptor). Diuretics counter Na⁺ retention. Venodilators increase venous capacitance (↓ preload). Arterial vasodilators relieve α- and AT II-driven vasoconstriction (↓ afterload). Glycosides, β-agonists and PDE inhibitors ↑ contractility.

⚠️ Combination therapy (slide 11)

Drugs from several classes are needed. Optimise them to avoid additive harm: they all lower blood pressure (hypotension) and many shift electrolytes. Watch for hypotension, arrhythmia, electrolyte imbalance and renal insufficiency.

Q: Which HFrEF drug classes reduce mortality, and which give symptomatic relief only?

Answer

Mortality: ARNI/ACEi/ARB, β-blockers, MRA (spironolactone), SGLT2 inhibitors. Symptoms only: diuretics, digoxin, veno/vasodilators, aquaretics, sympathomimetic amines, PDE inhibitors.

Q: Name the three points at which drugs block the RAAS in heart failure.

Answer

Renin release (β-blockers), AT II formation (ACE inhibitors; ARBs block its receptor) and the aldosterone receptor (spironolactone).

The usual first presentation: decompensation

The patient arrives short of breath, with pulmonary congestion and pitting edema. Assess the airway, assess volume status, and decide whether furosemide is indicated. Once HFrEF is confirmed, start the four pillars, then ask whether they need inotropic support.

  1. Airway assessment and continuous pulse oximetry
  2. Supplemental oxygen and ventilatory support
  3. Vital signsWatch for hypotension or hypertension.
  4. Continuous cardiac monitoring and IV access
  5. Seated posture
  6. Prompt diuretic therapy
  7. Early vasodilator therapy
  8. Urine output monitoring

Hypotension in a decompensated patient (slide 13)

  1. Assess volume statusDiurese, or resuscitate with fluid.
  2. InotropesSympathetic amines (dobutamine), milrinone, digoxin. Slide notes: if SBP < 85 mmHg or there are shock signs (cool extremities, narrow pulse pressure, low urine output, confusion) with adequate preload, add an inotrope.
  3. VasopressorsNorepinephrine, phenylephrine, high-dose dopamine. A temporising measure to perfuse vital organs in persistent shock.
⚠️ The vasopressor trade-off

A vasopressor raises blood pressure and organ perfusion, but "at the cost of" ↑ afterload, a higher impedance the weak ventricle must overcome, which can lower CO. "Very situation specific."

Q: A decompensated HFrEF patient has SBP 80 mmHg, cool extremities and confusion despite adequate filling. Inotrope or vasopressor first, and why?

Answer

Inotrope first (e.g. dobutamine) to raise contractility and CO. Add a vasopressor only for persistent shock, accepting the ↑ afterload.

ARNI — sacubitril/valsartan

Oral · first-line RAAS drug in HFrEF

MOA: sacubitril inhibits neprilysin, a neutral endopeptidase → natriuretic peptides, bradykinin and substance P accumulate. Natriuretic peptides promote diuresis; bradykinin is a potent vasodilator (and causes cough). Valsartan blocks AT1.

Indication: HFrEF — reduces cardiovascular death and hospitalisation in chronic HF.

AEs: hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, hyperkalemia.

Boxed warning: fetal toxicity (below).

Board add-on Stop an ACE inhibitor 36 h before starting an ARNI (combined bradykinin → angioedema). Never combine ARNI with an ACEi.

ACE inhibitor (enalapril) or ARB

Alternatives to ARNI

MOA: ↓ AT II → ↓ vasoconstriction (afterload) and ↓ aldosterone (a small diuretic effect, so a little preload too).

AEs: hyperkalemia, cough and angioedema (ACEi, bradykinin), hypotension. Teratogenic. Never two RAAS drugs together.

Full detail: Hypertension notes.

β-blocker (carvedilol)

Mortality ↓

↓ heart rate and contractility → ↓ O₂ demand; blunts the chronic sympathetic drive. Carvedilol (3rd generation) also blocks α1 → ↓ afterload.

Board add-on The evidence-based three are carvedilol, metoprolol succinate and bisoprolol. Start low, titrate slowly, and don't start during acute decompensation.

MRA — spironolactone

Mortality ↓ · K⁺-sparing

Blocks the mineralocorticoid (aldosterone) receptor → less ENaC synthesis → less Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ wasting.

AEs: hyperkalemia; not selective, so it also blocks androgen receptors → gynecomastia, impotence, abnormal menses.

Board add-on Eplerenone is the selective MRA without the antiandrogen effects. Avoid if K⁺ > 5.0 or eGFR < 30.

SGLT2 inhibitors — dapagliflozin, empagliflozin ("-gliflozin")

Oral · the one class for both HFrEF and HFpEF

MOA: selective inhibition of the sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 (proximal tubule). That explains the glucose lowering, but the cardio-renal benefit also seems to involve anti-inflammatory, renoprotective, cardioprotective and anti-remodelling effects that "we don't truly understand". This is an active research area.

Indications: glycemic control in DM-II · HFrEF: ↓ hospitalisation and mortality with or without diabetes · HFpEF.

AEs: renal impairment · infection · back pain · difficulty urinating.

The one to counsel on: gangrene of the perineum (said fast in TV adverts). "If you have any swelling or redness around the perineum, you need to come see me right away."

Board add-on That is Fournier gangrene (necrotising fasciitis). Also genital mycotic infections, UTIs, volume depletion and euglycemic DKA.

☐ US Boxed Warning — sacubitril/valsartan

Fetal toxicity. Discontinue as soon as pregnancy is detected. The warning comes from the valsartan, but the ARNI carries it: anything targeting the RAAS is contraindicated in pregnancy.

⚖️ Race and the RAAS drugs (end of lecture)

HF was one of the first conditions where avoiding RAAS drugs in Black patients was debunked. You may still see the old recommendation in some materials. It has been disproven repeatedly and removed from the risk calculators, and studies show race-agnostic prescribing closes racial disparities in cardiovascular outcomes.

Q: Sacubitril inhibits which enzyme, and which three substances accumulate?

Answer

Neprilysin (a neutral endopeptidase) → natriuretic peptides, bradykinin and substance P accumulate (natriuresis + vasodilation).

Q: A man on spironolactone for HFrEF develops tender breast enlargement. Why, and what is the alternative?

Answer

Spironolactone also blocks androgen receptors → gynecomastia. Switch to eplerenone (selective MRA) Board add-on.

Q: A diabetic HFrEF patient on empagliflozin reports perineal pain, redness and swelling. Concern?

Answer

Necrotising infection of the perineum (Fournier gangrene), a counselled SGLT2-inhibitor risk. See them immediately.

Q: Furosemide relieves a congested patient's dyspnea but the fatigue and cool extremities remain. Explain using the Frank-Starling curve.

Answer

Preload reduction slides the patient down the same curve: congestion (high EDP) improves but cardiac output doesn't rise. Low-output symptoms need afterload reduction or an inotrope to move onto a higher curve.

Q: Why does an ACE inhibitor move the patient to a new curve and also reduce preload slightly?

Answer

↓ AT II → vasodilation (↓ afterload) → more CO at each preload (new curve). ↓ aldosterone → less ENaC → a small diuretic effect (↓ preload).

Case 1 — Congestion: Loop Diuretics

AbbreviationsJVD = jugular venous distension · SpO₂ = oxygen saturation · TAL = thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle · NKCC2 = Na⁺–K⁺–2Cl⁻ cotransporter · ROMK = renal outer medullary K⁺ channel · DCT = distal convoluted tubule · NCC = Na⁺–Cl⁻ cotransporter · ENaC = epithelial Na⁺ channel · MI = myocardial infarction
60-year-old woman, short of breath, upright and anxious
  • BP 148/108 · HR 132 · RR 18 shallow, wet rales · SpO₂ 88% on room air.
  • Two previous MIs. Pitting edema of the legs and JVD.
  • Questions: the mechanism of the drug most likely given, and the electrolyte problems to expect.
🩺 Reading the case

Wet rales (pulmonary congestion), poor gas exchange (88%), pitting edema and JVD all point to increased preload backing up. Reduce preload with a diuretic: furosemide, a loop diuretic.

Furosemide — why loops are so potent

Furosemide (loop diuretic)

IV / oral · symptomatic relief of congestion

MOA: inhibits NKCC2 on the luminal membrane of the thick ascending limb. The TAL reabsorbs about 25–30% of the filtered Na⁺ load (he said "about 30%"), so blocking it gives a robust diuresis.

Why not for hypertension? Not mainly the electrolytes (every diuretic shifts those). It is tolerance: Na⁺ that escapes the TAL is reabsorbed downstream, and those segments increase their capacity, so the diuresis fades. Good for an initial robust diuresis in fluid overload; not used alone for long-term BP control.

AEs: hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis, dose-dependent ototoxicity (a board favourite).

Sulfa allergy: boards still want ethacrynic acid (a non-sulfonamide loop diuretic), although cross-reactivity data suggest it may not matter in practice.

Where each electrolyte goes

LossMechanism
Na⁺ (hyponatremia)Water follows the Na⁺ out.
Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺In the TAL some K⁺ recycles back into the lumen through ROMK, making a ~10 mV lumen-positive potential that drives Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ paracellularly (electroneutrality, not a transporter). Block NKCC2 → no recycling → no potential → Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ lost.
K⁺ (hypokalemia)Two sources. (1) Less K⁺ reabsorbed in the TAL. (2) The extra Na⁺ reaches the collecting duct, where ENaC reabsorbs Na⁺ alone. That positive charge has to be balanced, so K⁺ is secreted through ROMK. The same mechanism makes thiazides K⁺-wasting. NCC in the DCT is electroneutral, so it adds no K⁺ loss.
H⁺ (metabolic alkalosis)Low K⁺ → the intercalated cells swap K⁺ for H⁺ ("positive for positive") → H⁺ lost in urine. Dr. Johnson covered this in OMK 1 Block 4.
⚠️ Recording slip: α-, not β-intercalated cells

The recording says the K⁺-for-H⁺ swap happens in "beta intercalated cells". It is the α-intercalated cells that secrete H⁺ (H⁺-ATPase and H⁺/K⁺-ATPase). β-intercalated cells secrete HCO₃⁻. Board add-on Volume contraction (↑ AT II and aldosterone) adds a "contraction alkalosis".

Board add-on Other loops: bumetanide, torsemide. Also hyperuricemia (gout). "Loops lose calcium" (thiazides keep it). Digoxin patients on a loop diuretic → hypokalemia → digoxin toxicity.

Q: Why does furosemide cause hypocalcemia and hypomagnesemia when NKCC2 transports neither ion?

Answer

Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ are reabsorbed paracellularly, driven by the lumen-positive potential from K⁺ recycling via ROMK. Blocking NKCC2 removes that potential.

Q: A congested patient needs a loop diuretic but reports an anaphylactic sulfonamide allergy. What is the board answer?

Answer

Ethacrynic acid, the non-sulfonamide loop diuretic (even though true cross-reactivity is questioned in practice).

Q: Why isn't furosemide used as monotherapy for chronic hypertension?

Answer

Tolerance: downstream nephron segments raise their Na⁺ reabsorption capacity, so the diuretic effect fades over time.

Case 2 — Hypotension: Inotropes

Abbreviationsβ1 / β2 = beta adrenergic receptors · α1 = alpha-1 receptor · Gs = stimulatory G-protein · AC = adenylyl cyclase · cAMP = cyclic AMP · PKA = protein kinase A · SERCA = SR Ca²⁺ pump · PDE3 = phosphodiesterase-3 · NE / Epi = norepinephrine / epinephrine · ICU = intensive care unit
OL, 64-year-old woman with 3 months of HF: pulmonary edema and hypotension

Decompensating; needs immediate inotropic therapy → dobutamine, then ICU. Questions: dobutamine's mechanism, the alternatives, and how their receptor selectivity differs. (OL is just the patient's initials.)

Dobutamine

IV infusion · top of most HFrEF inotrope algorithms

MOA: β1 agonist, "considered a pure β1 agonist" clinically. It is a racemic mixture: one enantiomer is an α agonist and the other an α antagonist, so the α effects wash out.

Effect: ↑ contractility (inotropy) to support forward flow in a decompensated patient. Symptomatic, not a mortality drug.

β1→Gs → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP→PKA phosphorylates: L-type Ca²⁺ channels (bigger influx) · phospholamban (↑ SERCA, more SR Ca²⁺) · phospholemman / exchanger→more Ca²⁺ per beat → bigger Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release→↑ contractile force

The alternatives — and why selectivity matters

Board add-on Dopamine by dose: low → D1 (renal and mesenteric vasodilation), medium → β1 (inotropy), high → α1 (vasoconstriction). Milrinone is an "inodilator": PDE3 inhibition also relaxes vascular smooth muscle, so watch for hypotension and arrhythmias.

Q: Dobutamine binds α receptors, yet clinically it acts as a pure β1 agonist. Why?

Answer

It is a racemic mixture: one enantiomer is an α agonist and the other an α antagonist, so the α effects cancel.

Q: Why does norepinephrine raise contractility but often slow the heart rate?

Answer

Its α1 vasoconstriction (with no β2 dilation) ↑ afterload and BP → the baroreceptor reflex ↑ vagal outflow → reflex bradycardia, despite the β1 effect.

Q: How does milrinone raise contractility without a receptor?

Answer

It inhibits PDE3, so cAMP persists → PKA phosphorylates the Ca²⁺-handling proteins, the same endpoint as β1 activation.

⭐ cGMP + cyanide = nitroprusside

Slide 20 · organic nitrates vs sodium nitroprusside · PDE5 interaction · hydralazine

Case 3 — cGMP & Cyanide: Nitroprusside

AbbreviationscGMP = cyclic guanosine monophosphate · NO = nitric oxide · sGC = soluble guanylyl cyclase · ALDH = aldehyde dehydrogenase · PDE5 = phosphodiesterase-5 · ICU = intensive care unit · HFrEF = heart failure with reduced ejection fraction · NYHA = New York Heart Association class
OL in the ICU: continuous infusion → metabolic acidosis → fatal arrhythmia

Several days on a continuous IV infusion that increases cGMP. She develops acute metabolic acidosis and dies of an arrhythmia. Toxicology: cyanide poisoning. Which drug, and how does its class treat CHF?

🩸

Organic nitrates (nitroglycerin, isosorbide)

Must be activated by an enzyme (aldehyde dehydrogenase) in neighbouring endothelium to free NO. That machinery is on the venous (capacitance) side → mainly venodilators → ↓ preload.

☠️

Sodium nitroprusside

Breaks down spontaneously → NO with no enzyme needed → dilates arterioles and veins → ↓ afterload and ↓ preload. A "universal dilator". Cyanide sits at the core of its structure, so prolonged infusion → cyanide toxicity → metabolic acidosis.

NO (from the nitrate or nitroprusside)→soluble guanylyl cyclase→↑ cGMP → protein kinase G→smooth muscle relaxation→vasodilation
🔑 What gives it away

"↑ cGMP" alone says nitrate class, a preload reducer. The cyanide and the spontaneous breakdown say nitroprusside, which therefore reduces afterload as well.

⚠️ The interaction to always ask about

PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) + any nitrate → nitrates make cGMP and PDE5 inhibitors stop its breakdown → profound, refractory hypotension.

Hydralazine + long-acting nitrate (slide 12)

An alternative to the RAAS drugs in initial therapy and a secondary option. Hydralazine is an arteriolar dilator (↓ afterload); the nitrate ↓ preload.

Board add-on Hydralazine + isosorbide dinitrate reduced mortality in the A-HeFT trial (self-identified Black patients, NYHA III–IV HFrEF), so guidelines list it as an add-on there. Hydralazine AEs: reflex tachycardia, drug-induced lupus. Cyanide toxicity antidotes: hydroxocobalamin, sodium thiosulfate.

Q: Why does nitroprusside dilate arterioles while nitroglycerin mainly dilates veins?

Answer

Nitroglycerin needs enzymatic bioactivation in endothelium found mainly on the venous side. Nitroprusside releases NO spontaneously, so it acts on arterioles and veins alike.

Q: A patient on a prolonged nitroprusside infusion develops a rising lactate and anion-gap metabolic acidosis. What toxicity?

Answer

Cyanide toxicity: nitroprusside has cyanide at its core.

Cases 4 & 5 — Building the Regimen

AbbreviationsICU = intensive care unit · AT II = angiotensin II · ENaC = epithelial Na⁺ channel · ROMK = renal outer medullary K⁺ channel · α1 = alpha-1 receptor · MRA = mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist · HFrEF = HF with reduced EF · EF = ejection fraction · CO = cardiac output · ARNI = angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor · SGLT2i = SGLT2 inhibitor
Case 4 — 68-year-old woman, acute decompensated HF, 11 years of untreated hypertension

Stabilised with a loop diuretic, then started on furosemide, enalapril, carvedilol and a mineralocorticoid-receptor antagonist.

Enalapril → which electrolyte?

"-pril" = ACE inhibitor

↓ AT II → ↓ aldosterone → less ENaC synthesis → less Na⁺ entry to drive K⁺ out through ROMK → hyperkalemia.

Why carvedilol?

3rd-generation β-blocker

β-blockade ↓ O₂ demand (rate, contractility) and the elevated sympathetic drive. α1 blockade ↓ afterload → more CO from the contractility the patient has: a hemodynamic benefit.

MRA adverse effects

Spironolactone

The mineralocorticoid receptor = the aldosterone receptor, so this adds to the anti-aldosterone effect. It isn't selective, so androgen-receptor blockade → gynecomastia, impotence, abnormal menses. Plus hyperkalemia, additive with enalapril.

⚠️ Put the regimen together

Enalapril + spironolactone both raise K⁺; furosemide lowers it. The net K⁺ is unpredictable, so monitor potassium and renal function. All four lower BP: watch for hypotension.

Case 5 — 68-year-old Black woman, HFrEF for 6 months: edema, rales, acutely depressed EF

Central and peripheral edema; wet rales; echo shows an acutely depressed EF. Questions: the drug given immediately to raise CO, the drugs for the pulmonary findings, and the drugs likely on her medication list (and why).

📝 Worked answer — this case wasn't reached in the recording

  • ↑ cardiac output now: an IV inotrope, e.g. dobutamine (β1 → cAMP → PKA → more Ca²⁺ per beat).
  • Pulmonary findings (rales, edema): an IV loop diuretic, furosemide (preload reduction), plus early vasodilator therapy (a nitrate) per the decompensation checklist.
  • Her home list (the four pillars, for mortality): an ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan) or ACEi/ARB · an evidence-based β-blocker (carvedilol) · spironolactone · an SGLT2 inhibitor (dapagliflozin) · furosemide as needed.
  • Race: per the lecture, her race does not argue against RAAS drugs. Board add-on Boards and guidelines still list hydralazine + isosorbide dinitrate as an add-on for self-identified Black patients with NYHA III–IV symptoms on full therapy (A-HeFT), not as a replacement.

Q: A patient on enalapril and spironolactone develops peaked T waves. Mechanism?

Answer

Both reduce aldosterone action (↓ production; receptor blockade) → less ENaC-driven K⁺ secretion → hyperkalemia.

Q: Why is carvedilol preferred over atenolol in this HFrEF regimen?

Answer

Carvedilol adds α1 blockade → ↓ afterload to the β-blocker's ↓ O₂ demand, and it is one of the mortality-proven HF β-blockers.

PHYSIOLOGY & STRATEGY

The vicious cycle

  • ↓ function → baroreflex → sympathetic: α vasoconstriction, β1 → renin → AT II (↑ afterload) → aldosterone (Na⁺ → ↑ preload) → ↑ O₂ demand → worse HF
  • Dilated atria resist natriuretic peptide → kidney keeps retaining Na⁺ → edema
  • Remodelling → ↓ compliance (PV-loop filling limb rises)
  • EF < 40% reduced · ≥ 50% preserved · in between unclear

Mortality vs symptoms

  • Pillars (↓ mortality): ARNI/ACEi/ARB · β-blocker · spironolactone · SGLT2i (also HFpEF)
  • Symptoms only: diuretics, digoxin, veno/vasodilators, aquaretics, sympathomimetic amines, PDE inhibitors
  • Combining → additive hypotension, arrhythmia, electrolytes, renal insufficiency

Sequence (slides 12–13)

  • Initial: ARNI/ACEi/ARB (or hydralazine + nitrate), β-blocker, spironolactone, SGLT2i, furosemide as needed
  • Secondary: digoxin; hydralazine + long-acting nitrate
  • Decompensation: airway, O₂, monitor, IV, sit up, prompt diuretic, early vasodilator, urine output
  • Hypotension: volume → inotrope (SBP < 85 / shock with adequate preload) → vasopressor (↑ afterload cost)

Frank-Starling

  • A→B failure · B→C congestion · inotrope C→D→E · ACEi C→F (new curve) · diuretic C→G (same curve)
THE PILLARS

ARNI — sacubitril/valsartan

  • Neprilysin inhibition → ↑ natriuretic peptides, bradykinin, substance P + AT1 block
  • ↓ CV death and hospitalisation in HFrEF
  • Hypotension, orthostasis, hyperkalemia; boxed: fetal toxicity

ACEi (enalapril) / ARB

  • ↓ AT II → ↓ afterload + small diuresis (↓ aldosterone)
  • Hyperkalemia; ACEi cough/angioedema; teratogenic; never two RAAS drugs

β-blocker (carvedilol)

  • ↓ O₂ demand, blunts sympathetic drive; α1 block → ↓ afterload

MRA (spironolactone)

  • Aldosterone receptor → ↓ ENaC; hyperkalemia; androgen block → gynecomastia, impotence, abnormal menses

SGLT2i (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin)

  • SGLT2 block + poorly understood cardio-renal effects; T2DM, HFrEF with or without DM, HFpEF
  • Renal impairment, infection, back pain, dysuria; perineal gangrene (counsel)

Race

  • Avoiding RAAS drugs in Black patients = debunked; race-agnostic prescribing closes disparities
THE CASES

1 · Congestion → furosemide

  • Rales, JVD, pitting edema, SpO₂ 88% = ↑ preload
  • NKCC2 in the TAL (~25–30% of Na⁺) → robust; tolerance → not for HTN
  • ↓ K⁺, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ (lost lumen-positive potential), hypokalemic alkalosis; K⁺ also lost via ENaC/ROMK
  • Ototoxicity · sulfa allergy → ethacrynic acid

2 · Hypotension → dobutamine

  • Racemic → clinically pure β1 → cAMP → PKA → Ca²⁺ channels, phospholamban/SERCA → more Ca²⁺ per beat
  • NE: α1 + β1, no β2 → ↑ afterload, reflex bradycardia · dopamine dose-dependent (renal → α) · milrinone/inamrinone = PDE3

3 · cGMP + cyanide → nitroprusside

  • Spontaneous NO → arteries + veins (↓ afterload + preload); cyanide → metabolic acidosis
  • Organic nitrates need enzymatic activation (venous) → preload only
  • PDE5 inhibitor + nitrate → refractory hypotension

4–5 · The regimen

  • Enalapril → hyperkalemia (↓ aldosterone → ↓ ENaC) · carvedilol = ↓ demand + ↓ afterload · spironolactone → gynecomastia
  • Case 5: dobutamine now; furosemide (+ nitrate) for the lungs; four pillars at home
