His advice for board questions

Topic 1
⭐ Troponin C, not tropomyosin

Slides 3–6 · sliding filaments, Ca²⁺, cross-bridges, preload vs afterload

Striated Muscle & Force Development

AbbreviationsCa²⁺ = calcium · ATP / ADP = adenosine tri- / diphosphate · Pi = inorganic phosphate · SR = sarcoplasmic reticulum · RyR2 = ryanodine receptor 2 (SR Ca²⁺-release channel) · DHPR = dihydropyridine receptor (L-type Ca²⁺ channel) · SERCA = SR Ca²⁺-ATPase · E-C coupling = excitation–contraction coupling · MLCK = myosin light-chain kinase

Cardiac vs skeletal muscle

  • Both are striated and contract by sliding filaments.
  • Cardiac fibres are branched and joined end to end by gap junctions — electrically coupled, so one SA-node impulse sweeps through the whole myocardium.

Sliding filaments

  • Force comes from actin (thin) filaments sliding past myosin (thick) filaments.
  • Contraction shortens the sarcomere (Z-disc to Z-disc).
  • The A band stays the same width; the H zone and I band narrow.

Calcium turns it on

  1. Ca²⁺ binds troponin CThe troponin complex changes shape.
  2. Tropomyosin movesIt uncovers the myosin-binding site on actin.
  3. Cross-bridges form → power stroke at onceThe thin filaments are pulled inward. Force depends on how many cross-bridges are active at one time.

The cross-bridge cycle needs ATP

  • At rest: no cross-bridges; myosin heads are energised and cocked at 90°.
  • Power stroke: happens as soon as the bridge forms; ADP + Pi are released.
  • Detach: a new ATP binds and the head lets go of actin.
  • Re-cock: myosin ATPase hydrolyses ATP and returns the head to 90°.
  • Cycling continues as long as Ca²⁺ stays high and ATP is available.
🧠 Where the trigger Ca²⁺ comes from
  • Skeletal muscle: depolarisation-induced Ca²⁺ release — the voltage sensor pulls the SR channel open directly.
  • Cardiac muscle: Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release. Ca²⁺ entering through L-type channels in the plateau (phase 2) opens ryanodine receptors (RyR2) on the SR.

Board add-on Relaxation: SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR, and the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger pushes it out of the cell.

Preload, afterload, isotonic, isometric

TermMeaningIn the ventricle
PreloadStretch on the muscle before it contracts. It sets the thin–thick filament overlap.Filling: EDV / end-diastolic pressure
AfterloadThe load that opposes shortening, met once contraction startsMainly aortic diastolic pressure
IsometricTension develops but the muscle can't shorten (like pushing a wall)Isovolumic contraction
IsotonicThe muscle shortens and moves the load at constant tensionEjection
🔑 Why the heart can use preload and skeletal muscle can't
  • Skeletal muscle in the body already sits at maximal filament overlap. Stretching it more only pulls filaments apart → less force.
  • Cardiac muscle works on the ascending limb of the length–tension curve. More filling → more stretch → more overlap → more force.
  • That is Starling's law (Topic 5).

Q (shown in class): A woman has had her thyroid removed. She now has perioral and fingertip tingling and hyperactive reflexes; hypocalcemia is suspected. Which step of skeletal-muscle contraction is most directly affected? (Options included guanylyl cyclase activity, Ca²⁺ binding to tropomyosin, a conformational change in troponin, and phosphorylation by myosin light-chain kinase.)

Answer

The Ca²⁺-driven conformational change in troponin (troponin C), which moves tropomyosin.

  • Ca²⁺ never binds tropomyosin. Half of last year's class fell for this.
  • MLCK phosphorylation is how smooth muscle works.
  • Guanylyl cyclase plays no part in skeletal muscle contraction.

Q: On an electron micrograph, which sarcomere band keeps the same width during contraction?

Answer

The A band (the length of the thick filaments). The I band and H zone narrow, and Z-discs move closer together.

Topic 2
⭐ MAP = CO × TPR

Slides 7–8 · MAP is the dependent variable

MAP = CO × TPR

AbbreviationsMAP = mean arterial pressure · CO = cardiac output · TPR = total peripheral resistance (= SVR, systemic vascular resistance; excludes the lungs) · PRU = peripheral resistance unit (mmHg ÷ mL/s) · LV / RV = left / right ventricle · GI = gastrointestinal
🔑 The equation he told you to know (three exclamation marks on the slide)

MAP (mmHg) = CO (L/min) × TPR. The body adjusts CO and TPR to hold MAP where it is; MAP itself is the dependent variable.

  • The circulation is a closed system. At any steady state, LV output = RV output.
  • A tissue gets more flow by vasodilating (↓ its resistance) → TPR falls → CO rises to keep MAP up.
  • The share of CO each organ gets moves with its metabolic need.

Exercise redistributes the same pressure

  • Heart, skin and skeletal muscle flow rise a lot.
  • Brain flow stays about constant.
  • Splanchnic (gut) and renal flow fall — that helps defend pressure.
  • Exercise too hard or too long and GI blood flow can fall enough to injure the gut.
🧠 Student question: MAP vs "perfusion pressure"?
  • MAP is the pressure in the large arteries — the upstream pressure driving flow into every organ.
  • It falls only a little along the large arteries, so each vascular bed sees nearly the same pressure.
  • "Perfusion pressure" in a question usually means an isolated part of the circulation (its inflow minus outflow pressure).

Q: EDV 160 mL, ESV 110 mL, heart rate 90. Give SV, EF and CO, and say whether EF is normal.

Answer

SV = 50 mL · EF = 50/160 = 31 % (reduced; normal 55–75 %) · CO = 50 × 90 = 4.5 L/min. CO is kept up by a faster rate and a bigger EDV — the classic HFrEF pattern.

Q: During exercise TPR falls by half. What must happen to CO for MAP to stay the same?

Answer

CO must double. MAP = CO × TPR, so a halved TPR needs a doubled CO. That is why the sympathetic system drives HR and contractility up.

Topic 3
⭐ "You'll see this on the boards, I guarantee it"

Slides 9–13 · conduction, pacemaker vs fast-response APs, refractory periods

Conduction & Action Potentials

AbbreviationsAP = action potential · SA / AV node = sinoatrial / atrioventricular node · RA / LA = right / left atrium · If = "funny" current (inward Na⁺) · ICa = L-type Ca²⁺ current · Ito (Kto) = transient outward K⁺ current · IKr = rapid delayed-rectifier K⁺ current · Kir = inward-rectifier K⁺ channel · ACh = acetylcholine · NE = norepinephrine · M2 = muscarinic-2 receptor · β1 = beta-1 adrenergic receptor · ARP / ERP = absolute / effective refractory period · RRP = relative refractory period · SVT = supraventricular tachycardia

The conduction path

SA node→RA → septum → LA (Bachmann's bundle)→AV node (≈0.1 s delay)→Bundle of His→Bundle branches→Purkinje fibres → ventricles
  • The atria are electrically insulated from the ventricles. The only way through is the AV node.
  • The ~0.1 s AV-node delay lets the ventricles fill completely before they contract.
  • Conduction velocity is fastest where resistance is lowest: Purkinje ≈ 4 m/s, AV node ≈ 0.05 m/s.
  • Sympathetic input speeds conduction and parasympathetic input slows it (through Ca²⁺ channels and gap junctions). This matters when you pick drugs for heart failure and arrhythmias.
  • SA-node pacemaker cells fire automatically, without any nerve input.
🧠 Why the atrial plateau is so short

Most ventricular filling is passive, through open AV valves. The atria add only the last ~10 % (the "atrial kick"), so they don't need long. The plateau is when Ca²⁺ enters for E-C coupling, so atria have a short plateau and ventricles — especially the LV, which does most of the work — a long one.

Pacemaker (slow-response) AP — 3 phases, no true resting potential

PhaseCurrentWhat happens
4If — "funny" current (slow inward Na⁺)Gradual depolarisation from ~−60 mV. Its slope sets the heart rate.

The "funny" current is a Na⁺ current activated by hyperpolarisation, not depolarisation — that's why it's called funny.

  • No autonomic input (e.g. a transplanted, denervated heart): HR ≈ 100/min. Resting vagal tone normally holds it lower.
  • Overdrive suppression: the fastest SA-node cells reach threshold first and suppress slower backup pacemakers before they can fire.

Fast-response AP (atria, ventricles, Purkinje) — 5 phases

PhaseChannelsMeaning
0 rapid depolarisationVoltage-gated Na⁺ open at threshold (~−70 mV); inactivate just before phase 1Ions flowing in through gap junctions bring the cell to threshold fast → Na⁺ spike

Systole ≈ phase 0 through most of phase 3 · diastole ≈ late phase 3 through phase 4.

Refractory periods protect the pump

  • Absolute (effective) refractory period: phase 0/1 to mid-phase 3. Most Na⁺ channels are inactivated, so no new AP is possible.
  • Relative refractory period: mid to end of phase 3. A stronger-than-normal stimulus can fire the cell. A premature beat landing here can start an arrhythmia.
  • The long ARP prevents summation and tetany (which skeletal muscle can do) and guarantees time to fill.
⚠️ Faster isn't always more output

In SVT at ~200/min, diastole becomes too short to fill the ventricles. Stroke volume collapses, so CO and MAP can fall even though the rate went up.

Q: A drug blocks the funny current (If) in SA-node cells. What happens to the phase-4 slope and heart rate?

Answer

The phase-4 slope flattens → slower heart rate. If is the inward Na⁺ current of phase 4. (Board add-on ivabradine blocks it.)

Q: Which ion carries phase 0 of an SA-node action potential, and which carries phase 0 of a ventricular myocyte?

Answer

SA node: Ca²⁺ through L-type channels (slow upstroke) · ventricle: Na⁺ through fast voltage-gated channels.

Q: Vagal stimulation slows the SA node in two ways. Name both.

Answer

ACh on M2: (1) ↓ If and ICa → flatter phase 4; (2) ↑ K⁺ conductance → hyperpolarises the cell, moving it further from threshold.

Topic 4
⭐ Know all 5 phases cold

Slides 15–18 · pressures, valves, five phases, Wiggers diagram

The Cardiac Cycle

AbbreviationsAV valves = atrioventricular valves (tricuspid, mitral) · IVC (here) = isovolumic contraction · IVR = isovolumic relaxation · S1 / S2 = first / second heart sound · PA = pulmonary artery · RA / RV / LA / LV = right / left atrium and ventricle · JVP = jugular venous pressure

The pressures to know

Aorta120/80
Left ventricle120/8
Left atrium~8
Pulmonary artery25/10
Right ventricle~30/4
Right atrium0–4

Values from the slide, in mmHg. Left side = high-pressure systemic circuit; right side = low-pressure pulmonary circuit.

  • Systemic circuit: high pressure, LV → arteries → back to the RA. Pulmonary circuit: low pressure, RV → lungs → LA.
  • AV valves: tricuspid (right), mitral/bicuspid (left). Semilunar valves (three leaflets each): pulmonic and aortic.
  • LV pressure drops to ~8 in diastole. The aorta always holds blood, so its diastolic pressure stays ~80.
  • Aortic diastolic pressure is what the LV must overcome every beat — a key part of afterload. Boards test this indirectly: "longstanding hypertension… what happens to afterload?"

Five phases

  1. Atrial contractionEnd of diastole; semilunar valves closed. It tops off the ventricles with ~10 % of EDV at rest.
  2. Isovolumic contraction (early systole)AV valves snap shut (S1) while semilunar valves are still closed. Pressure rises, volume doesn't change, no flow.
  3. Systolic ejectionSemilunar valves open once ventricular pressure exceeds aortic/pulmonary pressure.
  4. Isovolumic relaxation (early diastole)Semilunar valves close (S2) as ventricular pressure falls below arterial pressure. AV valves are still closed, so there's no filling.
  5. Rapid fillingAV valves open when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure. This is most of diastole.

Wiggers diagram (pressure vs time)

  • Red (LV): it peaks at 120, so it must be the left ventricle. The RV peaks near 25.
  • Mitral valve closes when LV > LA → S1 → isovolumic contraction (isometric) → aortic valve opens → ejection (isotonic).
  • Aortic valve closes when LV < aorta → S2 → isovolumic relaxation → mitral opens → filling.
  • LA pressure rises during IVR, which helps filling. The atrial kick is the small bump just before systole.
  • Time is on the x-axis, so two beats let you calculate heart rate.
✅ Where to spend your time

In all the board questions Dr. Morganelli reviewed, he never saw a Wiggers diagram — but PV loops came up a lot. Know the phases well enough to picture them on either graph.

Q: During which phase does the LV contract with no change in volume, and which valves are closed?

Answer

Isovolumic contraction (early systole): both mitral and aortic valves are closed. It starts at S1 (mitral closure) and ends when the aortic valve opens.

Q: What event makes the second heart sound, and what phase follows it?

Answer

Aortic (and pulmonic) valve closure → isovolumic relaxation, until the mitral valve opens.

Topic 5
⭐ "PV loops are a lot more fun" — and all over the boards

Slides 19–23 · reading the loop · Starling · afterload · inotropy

PV Loops: Preload, Afterload & Contractility

AbbreviationsPV = pressure–volume · ESPVR = end-systolic pressure–volume relationship (slope = contractility) · EDPVR = end-diastolic pressure–volume relationship (slope = stiffness; reflects compliance) · EDP = end-diastolic pressure · dP/dt = rate of pressure rise · IVC / IVR = isovolumic contraction / relaxation · NE = norepinephrine · Epi = epinephrine

Reading the loop (no time axis)

SegmentStarts atWhat's happening
a filling④ mitral opensVolume rises with only a small pressure rise — the relaxed ventricle fills passively
b isovolumic contraction① mitral closes (EDV)Straight up: pressure rises, volume unchanged
c ejection② aortic opensRight to left: volume leaves into the aorta and slows toward the end
d isovolumic relaxation③ aortic closes (ESV)Straight down: pressure falls, volume unchanged
  • Loop width = SV (EDV − ESV). EF = SV ÷ EDV.
  • ESPVR = the maximum pressure the LV can make at any volume. Its slope = contractility (inotropy).
  • EDPVR = the passive filling curve. Steeper = stiffer = less compliant.
  • Board loops won't be labelled. Start at mitral opening and go around counter-clockwise.

Preload → Starling's law

  • Preload = stretch from filling. EDV and EDP are its indices.
  • Preload sets sarcomere length ("heterometric" regulation). More overlap → more cross-bridges → stronger contraction → bigger SV.
🔑 Why Starling matters: it balances the two sides

A surge of venous return (posture change, Valsalva release) raises RV preload → RV pumps more → LA fills more → LV preload rises and the LV pumps more. Starling's law keeps right and left outputs matched with no nerve or hormone involved.

Afterload

  • Afterload = the load the ventricle meets during systole. It must be overcome before ejection starts.
  • The heart is an afterloaded muscle: isometric until the aortic valve opens, then isotonic.
  • Aortic diastolic pressure is a major determinant (not the only one — e.g. aortic stenosis adds afterload too).
  • ↑Afterload → ↓SV at any preload: more energy goes into opening the valve, so less is left for ejection.
  • A change in afterload moves the heart to a new Starling curve, but the heart still depends on preload on that curve.

Contractility (inotropy)

  • Contractile force independent of preload. It reflects the rate of cross-bridge cycling: intracellular Ca²⁺ and troponin C's Ca²⁺ sensitivity.
  • Best measure: max dP/dt during isovolumic contraction — how steeply LV pressure rises.
  • ↑ by sympathetic activity (NE) and many drugs. ↑Contractility → bigger SV at any preload and afterload.
  • ↓ contractility in failure: e.g. losing ~30 % of healthy myocytes to infarction, or ischemic segments that can't contract.

The ESPVR is found by recording loops at many different EDVs with the same afterload (e.g. while briefly occluding the vena cava). Their end-systolic corners line up on one line. Add epinephrine and that line gets steeper.

Board add-on: valve lesions on the PV loop

Board add-on Not covered in this lecture, but these loops are common on boards.

  • Aortic stenosis: very tall loop (LV must beat the valve), ↓SV, and a steeper filling curve from LV hypertrophy.
  • Mitral regurgitation: no true isovolumic phases (blood leaks back to the LA) → rounded loop, ↑EDV.

Q (shown in class): A long, wordy vignette really asks: how does the LV loop look after a period of hypertension? Two answer loops (B and E) reach higher LV pressures than control.

Answer

Hypertension = ↑afterload → ↓SV. Pick the loop that is taller and narrower (loop B).

The other high-pressure loop has a bigger SV, so it can't be afterload alone.

Q: Dobutamine is given at constant preload and afterload. Which line on the PV diagram changes, and what happens to ESV and SV?

Answer

The ESPVR gets steeper (↑inotropy) → ESV falls, SV rises. EDV doesn't change at first.

Topic 6
⭐ "Heart failure → think ↓SV"

Slides 24–25 · systolic vs diastolic failure on the loop and the Starling curve

Heart Failure: HFrEF vs HFpEF

AbbreviationsHF = heart failure · HFrEF = HF with reduced ejection fraction (systolic HF) · HFpEF = HF with preserved ejection fraction (diastolic HF) · SHF / DHF = systolic / diastolic heart failure · ESPVR / EDPVR = end-systolic / end-diastolic pressure–volume relationship · EDP = end-diastolic pressure · MI = myocardial infarction · JVD = jugular venous distension

Heart failure = cardiac output is reduced, and MAP may be too low to deliver enough O₂. Two mechanical flavours:

💔

HFrEF — systolic

  • ↓ Contractility — the ESPVR slope falls. Usually from myocyte loss (ischemic heart disease) and/or ↑afterload.
  • ↓SV → blood left behind → ↑ESV, then ↑EDV → LV dilates.
  • ↓SV with ↑EDV → EF falls.
  • The Starling curve shifts down and to the right.
🧱

HFpEF — diastolic

  • Filling is impaired: ↓ LV compliance (fibrosis, hypertrophy) or higher LV pressure at the start of diastole (smaller filling gradient).
  • Steep EDPVR → less EDV at a higher EDP — a diastolic pressure load.
  • SV falls because EDV falls, but EF is preserved: contractility and Starling are intact, at least early on.
  • Now thought to be about as common as HFrEF; causes are multifactorial.
  • Compensations that raise CO in HFrEF: ↑EDV (preload), ↑HR, ↑inotropy, ↓afterload — including a thicker wall and drug therapy. On the rotations: "he's on afterload-reducing therapy — name the drugs."
  • Starling-sensitive vs Starling-insensitive: some failing hearts still gain SV from more filling. The worst don't — extra volume only raises venous pressures (Topic 8).
⚠️ Recording slip to ignore

In the recording, the HFpEF explanation ends "…so in this case you would see a reduced ejection fraction." That was a slip. The slide is right: in HFpEF the SV is reduced but the EF is preserved, because contractility is unchanged.

Q (sent to Dr. Morganelli by a second-year before USMLE): A man has 10 minutes of crushing chest pain radiating to the left arm, JVD and a raised troponin I. A graph shows CO vs EDV with a normal curve and a lower curve, and points W, X, Y, Z. Which change best describes cardiac function in the first 10 seconds?

Answer
  • An acute MI means ↓contractility → the heart drops to the lower (failure) curve.
  • ↓SV leaves more blood behind → EDV rises.
  • Go from normal W to the failure-curve point at the higher EDV (Z). That point has a higher CO than X (same EDV as normal) — the Starling mechanism partly compensates.
  • Rule out Y: a healthy heart wouldn't be sitting at a high EDV.
  • "10 seconds" is there to exclude remodelling and slower compensation.

Q: A patient's EDPVR is much steeper than normal while the ESPVR is unchanged. What type of failure, and what happens to SV and EF?

Answer

HFpEF (diastolic dysfunction) — a stiff ventricle fills less → ↓EDV → ↓SV, with EF preserved.

Topic 7
⭐ Radius to the 4th power

Slides 27–30 · Q = ΔP/R · continuity · Poiseuille · series vs parallel

Flow, Resistance & Poiseuille's Law

AbbreviationsQ = flow · ΔP = pressure difference (P1 − P2) · R = resistance · A = cross-sectional area · v = velocity · r = radius · η or μ = viscosity · l = vessel length · RT = total resistance · Hct = hematocrit

Flow through a system — Ohm's law for blood

🔑 One equation, three forms

ΔP = Q × R · Q = ΔP / R · Q = (P1 − P2) / R. For the whole circulation the upstream pressure is MAP (venous pressure is ~0, so ignore it).

  • An organ needs more flow → its resistance falls (arterioles dilate) → with ΔP held, flow rises.
  • MAP would dip as TPR falls, so CO rises to hold the gradient.

Law of continuity

  • Q = A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ = … — cm² × cm/s = cm³/s, so it is just a flow rate.
  • Where total cross-sectional area is largest, velocity is lowest.
  • Capillary networks have the largest total area → the slowest flow (~0.03 cm/s), which gives time for gas and nutrient exchange. On the venous side area shrinks again, so velocity rises.
⚠️ The trap

A single capillary is tiny, but the question is about the whole network. Capillary flow velocity is the lowest in the body in both the systemic and pulmonary circuits — unless the stem explicitly asks about one isolated vessel.

Laminar flow and Poiseuille

  • Laminar flow (the most energy-efficient): layers parallel to the vessel. The wall layer is still; the centre is fastest (a parabolic front).
  • Poiseuille: Q = ΔP·π·r⁴ / 8ηl, so R = 8ηl / πr⁴.
  • Radius dominates: double r → R falls 16-fold → flow rises 16-fold. Halve r → flow falls 16-fold.
  • Viscosity is mostly hematocrit: it rises with dehydration, blood doping and polycythemia.
  • Length matters in lab tubes; in a real vascular bed the vessels are similar lengths, so r and η are what change.

Series vs parallel

ArrangementWhereRuleAdding a vessel…
SeriesAorta → large → medium arteriesRT = R₁ + R₂ + R₃↑ total resistance
ParallelSmall arteries, arterioles, capillaries1/RT = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃↓ total resistance

That's why a capillary network offers the least resistance even though each capillary on its own is very resistant. Dr. Morganelli saw a board question that made you calculate a parallel resistance.

Q: Arteriolar radius falls by half with no other change. What happens to flow through that bed at constant ΔP?

Answer

R ∝ 1/r⁴ → R rises 16-fold → flow falls to 1/16.

Q: Where in the systemic circulation is flow velocity lowest, and why?

Answer

Capillaries — they have the largest total cross-sectional area, and v = Q/A.

Q: Three vascular beds with resistances 2, 4 and 4 units sit in parallel. Total resistance?

Answer

1/RT = 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 1 → RT = 1 — lower than any single bed.

Slides 32–36 · capacitance · VR gradient · Pmsf · stressed vs unstressed volume

Venous Return & Mean Systemic Filling Pressure

AbbreviationsVR = venous return · Pv = peripheral venous pressure (at the venules furthest from the heart) · Pra = right atrial pressure · CVP = central venous pressure (vena cava near the RA; clinical stand-in for Pra) · Rv = venous resistance · ΔPvr = pressure gradient for venous return · Pmsf = mean systemic filling pressure · Cv = venous compliance
🧠 Which drives which?
  • Outside sources argue whether CO depends on VR or the other way round.
  • Dr. Morganelli: the energy comes from the cardiac output — CO drives VR.
  • The two are interdependent, and at steady state VR = CO.
  • Anything that transiently forces blood back to the right heart transiently raises CO.

The veins are a reservoir

  • ~70 % of total blood volume sits on the venous side, much of it in the splanchnic beds.
  • This reservoir can be mobilised to keep venous return up — vital in hemorrhage and shock.
  • Veins are highly compliant: they take whatever volume they get, even a transfusion nobody needed.

The gradient for venous return

🔑 VR = (Pv − Pra) / Rv
  • Normally about 8 − 0 mmHg lying down. The gradient is tiny, so small changes in Pv or Pra swing VR a lot.
  • Pra opposes venous return.
  • CVP (~0–6 mmHg) is easy to measure (catheter in a femoral or jugular vein + manometer), so questions use it in place of Pra.
  • Rv is normally low and rarely changes, but some vasodilators lower it along with arteriolar tone.

Mean systemic filling pressure (Pmsf)

  • Pmsf = the pressure throughout the circulation when flow stops. Stop the heart, let pressures equalise, measure a vein: ~7–8 mmHg.
  • Pmsf makes up most of Pv, so it is the main upstream push for venous return. The body can regulate it.

Unstressed vs stressed volume

VolumeDefinitionMakes pressure?
UnstressedBlood that fills the vessels up to the point where their walls start to stretch (the reservoir). ~70 % of venous volume at rest.No
StressedBlood above the unstressed volume. It stretches the walls, creates pressure and is the volume that flows. Set by total blood volume and venous compliance.Yes — it sets Pmsf
🎈 The air-mattress analogy

Pump air into a flat air mattress: for a while nothing seems to happen — that's the unstressed volume. Then it suddenly starts to inflate and push back — that's stressed volume. Veins behave the same way.

Three ways to raise the VR gradient

ScenarioStressed volumePmsf − PraVR
A. Normal~30 % of venous volume8 − 0Normal
B. Transfusion / fluid load in a normovolemic person (or a big night of beer and wings)↑ (mostly stressed; unstressed rises a little)12 − 1↑ → more CO, renal flow and GFR → the kidneys excrete the excess
C. ↑ Venomotor tone (sympathetic)↑ — ↓compliance shifts unstressed → stressed10 − 1↑

After a hemorrhage or severe dehydration, both volumes fall. A transfusion then restores both, and with them the pressure that drives venous return.

✅ How the body keeps VR up with CO during exercise

The sympathetic response ↑venomotor tone → veins stiffen (↓compliance) → less blood fits in the unstressed pool → stressed volume and Pmsf rise → VR keeps pace with the rising CO.

The vascular function curve

  • Built in dogs with a pump setting the flow (CO = independent variable) and Pra measured (dependent variable).
  • At CO = 0, Pra = Pmsf (~8) — the y-intercept.
  • As CO rises, Pra falls: the heart pulls blood from the venous side to the arterial side, widening the VR gradient.
  • Below a Pra of ~0 the vena cava collapses, so CO can't rise further (the curve goes flat).
⚠️ Heart failure read off this curve
  • The failing heart can't move up the curve → Pra rises (e.g. gradient 8 − 0 becomes 8 − 4) → less VR.
  • Blood shifts to the venous side (↑venous pressure → JVD, edema).
  • Blood leaves the arterial side (→ hypotension, poor tissue perfusion).
  • ↑ Blood volume or ↓ venous compliance (↑venomotor tone) → ↑stressed volume → ↑Pmsf → curve shifts right, and max CO is higher.
  • ↓ Volume (hemorrhage) or ↑ compliance → curve shifts left.
  • Nitrates + sildenafil (Viagra): dramatic ↑venous compliance → Pmsf collapses → VR and pressure drop. That's why Dr. Scully said never to combine them.
  • Slide 36 also lists TPR as a determinant of VR. Board add-on A TPR change rotates the curve rather than shifting it: ↓TPR (exercise, AV shunt) → steeper, higher, same Pmsf.

Q (shown in class): A patient is in decompensated heart failure. Which statement about giving IV fluid is most accurate? (Choices: fluid raises stressed volume and Pmsf and so VR · a transfusion mainly raises unstressed volume · fluid can make the patient worse · fluid reliably raises CO by raising preload · fluid stretches veins and enhances venomotor tone.)

Answer

Fluid can make things worse.

  • A failing heart may be Starling-insensitive: extra volume only raises venous pressures and loads a heart that can't pump what it already gets.
  • "Raises stressed volume, Pmsf and VR" is a true statement but doesn't answer the question.
  • "Mainly unstressed" is false — it is mostly stressed.
  • "Reliably raises CO" is only the hope; it isn't guaranteed. Papers still argue about it.

Q: What is Pmsf, how is it measured, and what sets it?

Answer

The circulatory pressure with no flow (stop the heart and let pressures equalise; ~7–8 mmHg). It is set by the stressed volume, which depends on total blood volume and venous compliance.

Q: On a vascular function curve, what does the y-intercept represent, and what happens to it after a large hemorrhage?

Answer

The intercept (Pra at CO = 0) is Pmsf. Hemorrhage ↓stressed volume → Pmsf falls → the curve shifts left and max CO falls.

⭐ "Find the new equilibrium point"

Slides 37–40 · cardiac function curve · Guyton's crossing · heart failure · exercise

Cardiac + Vascular Function Curves

AbbreviationsCO = cardiac output · VR = venous return · Pra = right atrial pressure · CVP = central venous pressure · Pmsf = mean systemic filling pressure · TPR = total peripheral resistance · HF = heart failure · NE = norepinephrine

Cardiac function curve = Starling with heart rate built in

  • Pra is the independent variable (an index of RV preload): just before systole there is an open column of blood from the vena cava into the RV.
  • CO is the dependent variable. CO rises as Pra rises.
  • There's a separate curve for each level of contractility and afterload: enhanced → up and left, depressed (HF) → down and right.

Guyton's crossing

  • Overlay both curves (Pra on the x-axis for both): where they cross is the steady state, where CO = VR.
  • Board versions often label the x-axis CVP.
  • Anything that moves one or both curves gives a new equilibrium point.
  • Bottom line: CO and VR are interdependent, but CO can be limited by factors that regulate the vascular curve.
  • A → B: ↓contractility (HF) drops the cardiac curve; the operating point slides down and right (lower CO, higher CVP).
  • Compensation: hypotension triggers sympathetic output → ↑venomotor tone and fluid retention → the vascular curve shifts right (↑Pmsf) → points D/E: CO partly restored at an even higher CVP. A transfusion does the same thing.
  • Moderate vs serious failure = two depressed cardiac curves.
✅ Exercise — both curves have to move
  • Sympathetic ↑HR, inotropy and lusitropy (faster relaxation) → cardiac curve up and left.
  • Arteriolar dilation in muscle, skin and heart → TPR falls sharply.
  • ↑Venomotor tone → ↑Pmsf → vascular curve shifts right.
  • Operating point goes from A (~5 L/min) to B (>20 L/min).
  • A high CO is only possible if venous return is regulated to keep up.

Q: A patient with systolic HF is given a positive inotrope. What happens to the cardiac function curve, the operating point's CO and its Pra?

Answer

The cardiac curve shifts up and left → the new crossing has a higher CO and a lower Pra (the heart pulls more blood out of the venous side).

Q: Why does a healthy person's CO fail to rise much if they only receive a strong inotrope, with no change in the veins?

Answer

The operating point runs down the unchanged vascular curve toward the point where the vena cava collapses (Pra ≈ 0). Without a higher Pmsf (↑volume or venomotor tone) VR can't keep up, so CO is capped.

Slides 42–48 · baroreflexes · ADH · RAAS · shock · the hyponatremia question

Regulating MAP — and the Heart-Failure Paradox

AbbreviationsADH = antidiuretic hormone (= AVP, arginine vasopressin) · RAAS = renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system · AI / AII = angiotensin I / II · ACE = angiotensin-converting enzyme · JG cells = juxtaglomerular cells · ANF / ANP = atrial natriuretic factor / peptide · GFR = glomerular filtration rate · IVC / SVC = inferior / superior vena cava · NIDCM = non-ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy · BUN = blood urea nitrogen · CN IX / X = glossopharyngeal / vagus nerve
SystemTime scaleSensor → triggerMain effect
High-pressure baroreflexSeconds (moment to moment)Stretch receptors in carotid sinus and aortic archSympathetic / vagal output → HR, contractility, TPR, venomotor tone
Low-pressure (volume) baroreflexLonger termStretch receptors in atria, IVC, SVC — ↑venous pressureDiuresis (lowers volume)

High-pressure baroreflex — negative feedback

  • ↓MAP → less stretch → ↓firing → ↑sympathetic, ↓vagal → ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑TPR (and venomotor tone) → pressure rises until firing is back to normal.
  • ↑MAP → more stretch → ↑firing → ↓sympathetic, ↑vagal → pressure falls.
  • In heart disease this reflex can stay switched on and amplified indefinitely.

Low-pressure (volume) reflex

  • Mostly quiet; it switches on when venous pressure rises: heart failure, IV infusions, posture changes, loss of gravity, too much salt and water.
  • Atrial stretch → ↑HR (Bainbridge reflex — the HR rise with an infusion or ↑blood volume).
  • ↓Renal sympathetic activity → ↑GFR and inhibited RAAS.
  • ↓ADH and ↑ANF.
  • Net: excrete salt and water — "the body thinks it is lowering pressure."

ADH

  • Inserts water channels in the collecting duct. The medulla's hyperosmotic gradient then pulls water back into the blood. Water is retained without sodium.
  • At normal levels it hardly constricts vessels. Pharmacological vasopressin (and newer, expensive analogues) is used as a vasopressor.

RAAS

Hypotension · hemorrhage · dehydration · stress→↑Sympathetic to JG cells (afferent arteriole) · ↓renal perfusion · ↓Na⁺ at macula densa→Renin→Angiotensinogen → AI→ ACE (lung capillaries) →AII
  • AII → arteriolar constriction (↑TPR), aldosterone from the adrenal cortex (↑NaCl + water reabsorption → ↑volume), ADH from the posterior pituitary, and thirst.
  • Everything together raises blood volume to support venous return and CO.
⚠️ Two direction slips in the recording
  • Renin: the recording says the macula densa cells "stop making renin." The slide is correct: ↓Na⁺ delivery sensed by the macula densa ↑renin release from the JG cells.
  • ADH: the recording says baroreceptors "increase their firing rate" to raise ADH. It is falling pressure → ↓baroreceptor firing → ↑ADH.

Shock as a worked example

  • Hypovolemic shock: fluid loss (hemorrhage, GI, renal, skin) → ↓volume → ↓CO → hypotension.
  • Acute defence: sympathetic → ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑venous return (venoconstriction), ↑TPR.
  • Longer-term defence: RAAS, ADH and fluid shifts rebuild volume.
🔑 The heart-failure paradox — "you just have to know this"

In HF, venous pressures rise, so the volume reflex tries to dump salt and water. At the same time the arterial side is underfilled, so the pressure-defending systems (high-pressure baroreflex → sympathetic, RAAS, ADH) switch on. The pressure-defending systems override the volume reflex → salt and water retention → more congestion. Normal physiology leads you into a dead end here; you have to know the clinical outcome.

Board add-on Preview: each heart-failure drug class cuts one link in this loop (↓preload, ↓afterload, ↓RAAS, ↓sympathetic drive).

Q (the last question in class): A woman with non-ischemic dilated cardiomyopathy (normal coronaries; viral, autoimmune or genetic cause) has EF 30 %, tachycardia, hypotension, raised JVP, bibasilar crackles, 2+ pitting edema and low urine output. Labs: low serum Na⁺, raised BUN and creatinine, and a very low urine Na⁺ (no normal range given — normal is at least ~5× higher). What explains the low serum sodium?

Answer

Dilutional hyponatremia.

  • Hypotension and poor renal perfusion (azotemia) drive RAAS: aldosterone holds on to Na⁺, which is why urine Na⁺ is so low.
  • ADH adds water retention on top, without sodium.
  • Water is retained out of proportion to salt, so the plasma is diluted even though total body sodium and volume are high.

Q: A patient receives a rapid 2-L saline infusion and their heart rate rises. Which reflex, and what else does the same receptor do?

Answer

The Bainbridge reflex (atrial stretch → ↑HR). The same low-pressure receptors also cause ↓renal sympathetic activity, ↓ADH and ↑ANF → diuresis.

Q: Carotid sinus stretch suddenly falls. Trace the reflex response.

Answer

↓Firing (via CN IX) → medulla ↑sympathetic and ↓vagal output → ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑TPR, ↑venomotor tone → MAP rises back toward normal.

MUSCLE · NUMBERS · ELECTRICITY

Contraction

  • Contraction: A band constant; H zone + I band narrow; Z-discs approach
  • Ca²⁺ → troponin C → tropomyosin moves (Ca²⁺ never binds tropomyosin; MLCK = smooth muscle)
  • ATP detaches the head; hydrolysis re-cocks it to 90°
  • Cardiac = Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release (L-type → RyR2); gap junctions, branched fibres
  • Heart on the ascending limb of length–tension → Starling; skeletal already at max overlap

Normal values (rest)

  • MAP 90–95 · HR 60–90 (~100 denervated) · SV ~70 · EDV ~120 · EF 55–75 % · CO 4–5 L/min · CI 2.5–4.2
  • MAP = CO × TPR; MAP is the dependent variable; LV output = RV output
  • Aorta 120/80 · LV 120/8 · LA 8 · PA 25/10 · RV ~30/4 · RA 0–4

Action potentials

  • Pacemaker: ph 4 If (Na⁺, opened by hyperpolarisation, from −60) → ph 0 Ca²⁺ at −40 → ph 3 K⁺
  • β1 ↑If/ICa (steeper ph 4) · M2 ↓If/ICa + ↑K⁺ conductance
  • Fast: 0 Na⁺ · 1 Ito · 2 Ca²⁺ plateau (E-C coupling) · 3 IKr · 4 Kir
  • AV delay ~0.1 s (fill) · Purkinje fastest · atria short plateau (kick ~10 %)
  • ARP: ph 0 → mid-3 (Na⁺ inactivated) — no tetany; RRP late 3 = arrhythmia window
  • Overdrive suppression; SVT ~200 → less filling → ↓CO
CYCLE · PV LOOPS · HEART FAILURE

Five phases

  • Atrial kick → IVC (S1, all valves shut) → ejection → IVR (S2) → rapid filling
  • Boards: PV loops ≫ Wiggers

PV loop

  • Width = SV; ESPVR slope = contractility; EDPVR = stiffness (compliance)
  • ↑Preload → wider right, ↑SV (Starling balances R and L)
  • ↑Afterload (aortic diastolic P, HTN) → taller + narrower, ↑ESV ↓SV; new Starling curve
  • ↑Inotropy (NE) → steeper ESPVR, ↓ESV ↑SV; best index = max dP/dt

Heart failure

  • HFrEF: ↓ESPVR → ↑ESV → ↑EDV (dilated), ↓SV, ↓EF; Starling curve down/right
  • HFpEF: steep EDPVR → ↓EDV at ↑EDP, ↓SV, EF preserved; ≈ as common
  • Compensation: ↑EDV, HR, inotropy, wall thickness, ↓afterload drugs
  • Acute MI (10 s): W → Z = failure curve at the higher EDV
  • Starling-insensitive heart: fluid only ↑venous pressure
FLOW · VENOUS RETURN · CONTROL

Flow

  • Q = ΔP/R · continuity Q = A·v → capillaries slowest (largest total area)
  • R = 8ηl/πr⁴ → 2× radius = 16× flow; viscosity ≈ Hct
  • Series add; parallel lowers RT (1/RT = Σ1/R)

Venous return

  • ~70 % of blood in veins; VR = (Pv − Pra)/Rv, ~8 − 0; CVP ≈ Pra
  • Pmsf = no-flow pressure ~7–8, set by stressed volume (volume + venous compliance)
  • Transfusion / ↑venomotor tone → ↑Pmsf; hemorrhage / nitrate + sildenafil → ↓Pmsf
  • VFC: Pra = Pmsf at CO 0; Pra ↓ as CO ↑; flat below ~0 (cava collapse)
  • Guyton crossing = steady state; HF A → B, fluid retention → D/E; exercise: cardiac up/left + vascular right

MAP control

  • High-P baroreflex (carotid IX, arch X): ↓P → ↓firing → ↑symp ↓vagal
  • Low-P (atria/cavae): ↓renal symp, ↓ADH, ↑ANF → diuresis; Bainbridge ↑HR
  • RAAS: ↓Na⁺ at macula densa / β1 / ↓perfusion → renin → AII (↑TPR) + aldosterone
  • HF: pressure systems override the volume reflex → retention; RAAS + ADH → dilutional hyponatremia (low urine Na⁺)
