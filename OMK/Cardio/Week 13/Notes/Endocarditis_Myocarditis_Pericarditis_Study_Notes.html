Three Layers, Three Infections

One lecture over two sessions: infective endocarditis (the big one — lining and valves), then a "fairly brief" pass through myocarditis and pericarditis ("Myo and Peri — sounds like a sixties singing group that didn't make it"). Endocarditis

Endocardium — mostly valves. Bacteria (staph, strep, enterococci) > fungi. Viruses: no. Usually >50 y.

Myocardium. Viruses are the main cause. Any age, peak 20–50 y. Often undiagnosed.

Pericardial sac. Mostly viral; bacterial = "purulent". Sharp pain better leaning forward, rub, ST↑/PR↓.

Topic 1

Definition · acute vs subacute · the numbers

Infective Endocarditis: What It Is

AbbreviationsIE = infective endocarditis · MRSA / MSSA = methicillin-resistant / -sensitive Staphylococcus aureus · CHF = congestive heart failure · WBC = white blood cells

Infective endocarditis = relatively rare, non-contagious, chronic, often life-threatening infection and inflammation of the endocardial surface — mostly native or prosthetic valves, but also mural endocardium, septal defects (usually ventricular), arteriovenous/arterial shunts and intravascular devices. Untreated, it is almost invariably fatal; survivors often carry lasting effects.

Acute vs subacute — "not used anymore", still used everywhere

FeatureAcuteSubacute
CourseFulminant, abruptIndolent
Fever / signsHigh fever, systemic toxicity, ↑ WBCLow-grade fever, night sweats, weight loss
ValveCan hit a normal valvePreviously damaged valve (historically rheumatic)
Organisms"Professional" pathogens: S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, S. pyogenesLess virulent, often normal flora: viridans strep, enterococci
Death (untreated)< 6 weeks — catastrophic heart failure6 weeks → months — CHF
Empiric antibiotics it triggersBroad (think MRSA)More targeted (viridans, enterococci)

The "official" modern scheme is etiology-based (organism → course, likelihood of pre-existing heart disease, antibiotic choice), but clinicians, UpToDate and even the 2023 Duke criteria still use acute/subacute as a bedside read of speed and expected virulence. Don't correct your attending.

The numbers

Incidence

US

~1–3/100,000 (lecture; outline 3–7), rising slightly. The US has the highest incidence among Western countries.

Mortality

Hospital vs 1 year

In-hospital 15–20%; 1-year up to 40%.

Surgery

Valve

About half eventually need valve surgery (usually replacement).

The flip

Antibiotic era

Acute was ~20%, subacute ~75%. Now in industrialised countries ~75% is acute — mostly S. aureus (plus S. epidermidis, enterococci).

Q: A patient develops high fever, rigors and rapidly worsening heart failure over 10 days; the valve was previously normal. Which organism class tops the differential, and how broad should empiric therapy be?

Answer

Acute IE — Staphylococcus aureus (a "professional pathogen" that attacks normal valves). Empiric therapy must be broad, covering MRSA.

Who gets it — and why IDU is its own story

💉 Why injection drug use is such a strong risk (a USMLE favourite)
  • Younger patients (20–30 y), men > women.
  • Many injections a day → skin contamination; contaminated diluents and fillers; shared needles/syringes (early studies: up to 108 bacteria/mL).
  • Saliva as diluent → oral flora (viridans strep).
  • More likely colonised with S. aureus (skin, anterior nares) → S. aureus is the most common IDU organism.
  • Particulates (talc) directly damage tricuspid and pulmonic endothelium; many drugs blunt the immune response.

Q: Why do people who inject drugs get right-sided (tricuspid) endocarditis, and which organism is most likely?

Answer

Injected particulates such as talc damage the tricuspid/pulmonic endothelium, and injected skin bacteria reach the right heart first. S. aureus (skin/nares colonisation) is most common.

Topic 3
⭐ Vegetation = hallmark

Injury → sterile clot → bacteremia → biofilm → vegetation

Pathogenesis & the Vegetation

AbbreviationsNBTE = non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (sterile vegetation) · CNS = central nervous system · IDU = injection drug use

Normal endocardium resists bacteria and fungi (which is why IE is hard to reproduce in animal models). You need two things: an endocardial injury and a bacteremia.

  1. Endocardial injurycongenital defect, turbulent flow (high → low velocity), faulty or leaky valves, catheter trauma, IDU particulates (talc).
  2. Platelets + fibrin stickto exposed collagen → coagulation cascade → more fibrin. No bugs yet — a sterile nidus ("sterile vegetation"), native or prosthetic valve.
  3. Transient bacteremiaany traumatised colonised mucosa: dental work, toothbrushing, GI/GU/OB procedures. Usually cleared.
  4. Bacteria adhere to the platelet–fibrin nidus and colonise→ more platelets/fibrin → more coagulation; aggressive species (S. aureus) invade contiguous tissue. Antibodies form → immune complexes.
  5. Biofilm + vegetationa sheath of fibrin, leukocytes, RBCs, platelets, organisms and biofilm. Up to 109 bacteria per gram. Cytokines and proteases injure valve tissue.
🧠 Why IE is so hard to cure — biofilm

The best IE pathogens (S. aureus, S. epidermidis) are biofilm producers. Biofilm bacteria are ~500× more antibiotic-resistant: drugs and antibodies penetrate poorly, and the bacteria slow or stop growing — while most antibiotics (cell-wall, protein-synthesis, nucleic-acid inhibitors) need actively growing bacteria.

The vegetation drives the disease — three ways

1 · SEEDING

Continuous bacteremia

High-inoculum seeding of the blood → metastatic infection (esp. S. aureus, which infects anything it hits).

2 · DESTRUCTION

Valve invasion

Cytokines + proteases + bacteria destroy the valve → perforation, rupture, abscess, aneurysm.

3 · EMBOLI

Septic emboli

Friable vegetations break off → septic emboli: ischemic stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, meningitis, brain abscess, mycotic aneurysm, organ infarcts.

Where

Other organs

  • Kidney: embolic infarcts, abscesses (and immune-complex glomerulonephritis).
  • CNS: cerebral emboli (common).
  • Lung: right-sided IE → septic pulmonary emboli; left-sided → pulmonary edema, pleural effusion.
  • Systemic: can present as severe sepsis / septic shock / multi-organ failure — especially toxin/superantigen producers (S. aureus, S. pyogenes).

Q: Why does IE need prolonged bactericidal therapy even with a susceptible organism?

Answer

Organisms sit inside a fibrin–platelet vegetation and biofilm at up to 109/g, poorly penetrated and metabolically slow, so drugs that need growing bacteria work poorly — you must kill, not just inhibit, for weeks.

Staphylococcus aureus

GPC · catalase + · coagulase +

GPC + catalase-positive + coagulase-positive = S. aureus — sometimes that's all a board question gives you.

Most common cause; fulminant/acute; normal, prosthetic and right-sided valves; survival <70%.

S. epidermidis (CoNS)

Coagulase − · biofilm

Most common bacterium on skin → anything touching skin then going inside (valve, line, Foley, CIED) carries it in. Prosthetic valve IE (outline: ~40% of PVE); subacute; nosocomial; most are methicillin-resistant.

Viridans group streptococci

α-hemolytic · a group, not a species

There is no "Streptococcus viridans." Subgroups: sanguinis, bovis, mutans, mitior. Pre-antibiotic era #1 cause (RHD, subacute) — now <9% in the US, 17% worldwide.

S. gallolyticus (bovis group)

Only 5–7% — but a board favourite

IE with S. gallolyticus → look for colon cancer or IBD ("I'm giving you the answer to a board question"). Board add-on → colonoscopy.

Enterococci (mostly E. faecalis)

Nosocomial

Urinary/renal catheters, IV lines; usually damaged valves.

Gram-negative rods & HACEK

~2% each

Non-HACEK: E. coli, Klebsiella — elderly with undiagnosed UTIs. HACEK: slow-growing oral/GI gram-negatives on damaged valves — rare but over-tested.

Anaerobes

<10% · often odontogenic

Propionibacterium*, Lactobacillus*, Clostridium*, Bacteroides fragilis, Fusobacterium*, Porphyromonas* (*oral origin) — periodontitis, gingivitis, dental abscess. Mostly prosthetic valves; mortality 21–43%.

Fungi

<2% · immunocompromised

Candida, Aspergillus (± Trichosporon). Large, bulky vegetations that embolise and invade myocardium; antifungals penetrate poorly and are toxic → survival <50%, usually needs surgery.

🧠 HACEK

Haemophilus · Aggregatibacter · Cardiobacterium · Eikenella · Kingella — slow-growing gram-negatives, pre-existing valve disease, ~2%.

Culture-negative IE

  • Most common reason: antibiotics given before blood cultures mask ordinary staph/strep/enterococci → always draw 3 sets first.
  • Oddballs that won't grow on agar: Coxiella burnetii (Q fever — needs cell culture) and Bartonella (henselae, quintana — very slow); also Chlamydophila, Tropheryma whipplei, Legionella, Mycoplasma, Brucella.
  • PCR has cut the culture-negative rate and shown Coxiella and Bartonella are the commonest.
  • ~25% of IE is now polymicrobial.

Q: A 68-year-old with iron-deficiency anemia has endocarditis; blood cultures grow a non-enterococcal group D streptococcus. What else must you investigate?

Answer

Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis) IE is associated with colorectal cancer (and IBD) → colonoscopy.

Q: Three months after an aortic valve replacement, a patient develops subacute IE. Most likely organism and its key virulence factor?

Answer

Staphylococcus epidermidis (coagulase-negative) introduced from skin — protected by biofilm on the prosthetic material.

Q: A shepherd with fevers has echo-proven vegetations but three negative blood cultures. Likely organism and how is it confirmed?

Answer

Coxiella burnetii (Q fever) — culture-negative IE from animal contact; confirm by PCR or phase I IgG titre >1:800 (a Duke major criterion).

Topic 5

"It's a mess" — fever, murmur, signs

Clinical Findings & Right-Sided IE

AbbreviationsMR / AR / TR = mitral / aortic / tricuspid regurgitation · IDU = injection drug use · HCV / HBV = hepatitis C / B virus

Presentation ranges from rapidly progressive to indolent, so diagnosis is often delayed — clinicians chase the embolic or immune consequence (e.g. a "renal problem") instead of the source.

🌡️

Fever — 90%

With chills, anorexia, headache, myalgia/arthralgia, night sweats, dyspnea, pleuritic pain. Common — but not diagnostic.

🩺

New / changed murmur — 85%

1. Holosystolic (MR) · 2. Diastolic (AR) · 3. Holosystolic TR (right-sided).

Signs — supportive vs the rare "question-writer" ones

🧠 Immune complex vs embolus

Immune complexes: Osler nodes, Roth spots, glomerulonephritis, positive rheumatoid factor. Emboli: Janeway lesions, splinters, petechiae, organ infarcts. Board add-on "Osler = Ouch".

Complications that may be the presenting problem

  • Heart: regurgitation, heart failure, conduction block, valve/ring abscess; pericarditis (spread from mitral valve); myocarditis (contiguous spread).
  • Neuro: embolic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, brain abscess.
  • Septic emboli: infarcts of spleen, kidney, any organ; septic pulmonary emboli (right-sided).
  • Metastatic infection: osteomyelitis, septic arthritis.
  • Systemic immune reaction: immune-complex glomerulonephritis.
➡️ Right-sided IE
  • ~10% of IE; tricuspid 90%, pulmonic 10%.
  • Causes: IDU, intracardiac devices, central lines, dialysis, immunosuppression, congenital disease.
  • Organisms: S. aureus 70–80%; strep/enterococci ~25%; GNR, fungi <2% each.
  • Slower, mimics other disease: high fever, chills, weight loss, headache, abdominal pain; septic pulmonary emboli (~75%: dyspnea, cough, hemoptysis, chest pain).
  • Often no murmur. IDU patients often co-infected with HCV/HBV (HIV less now).
  • Diagnosis: positive blood cultures + echo showing right-sided vegetations.

Q: A febrile man who injects heroin has cough, hemoptysis and multiple peripheral cavitating nodules on chest CT, but no murmur. Diagnosis?

Answer

Right-sided (tricuspid) IE with septic pulmonary emboli, most likely S. aureus; right-sided IE often has no audible murmur.

Q: Which two peripheral IE signs are caused by immune complexes rather than emboli?

Answer

Osler nodes (painful finger/toe pads) and Roth spots (retina). Janeway lesions are embolic micro-abscesses.

Topic 6

Duke 2023 · labs · imaging

Diagnosing IE

AbbreviationsTTE / TOE = transthoracic / transesophageal echocardiography · CT = computed tomography · FDG-PET/CT = ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT · CIED = cardiac implantable electronic device · HOCM = hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy · RF = rheumatoid factor · ESR / CRP = erythrocyte sedimentation rate / C-reactive protein · IgG = immunoglobulin G

Suspect IE in any patient with bacteremia with a typical IE organism (staph, strep, enterococci) plus cardiac or non-cardiac risk factors (IDU, immunosuppression, recent surgery or dental work). Diagnosis = clinical picture + blood cultures + imaging, organised by the Duke criteria (2023 version — most sensitive; best for left-sided).

🧠 Definite = "2 · 1+3 · 5"

Two majors, one major plus three minors, or five minors. Possible = "1+1 or 3".

Laboratory findings — mostly non-specific

  • Blood: anemia; thrombocytopenia (15–20%); leukopenia — lecture: not usual; outline lists it; ↑ ESR/CRP; hypergammaglobulinemia (~25%); positive rheumatoid factor (50–60%) — antibody against the Fc of IgG, a response to circulating immune complexes.
  • Urine: proteinuria, hematuria, pyuria, (white-cell) casts, bacteriuria.
  • Blood cultures: bacteremia is continuous but low-level (<100/mL) → draw 3 separate sets, before antibiotics.

Q: Fever 38.6 °C, known MVP with regurgitation, and Osler nodes; blood cultures and echo are pending. How many Duke criteria are met, and what's the category so far?

Answer

Three minor (predisposition, fever, immunologic phenomenon) → possible IE. One major (≥2 positive typical cultures or a vegetation on echo) would make it definite (1 major + 3 minor).

Q: TTE shows no vegetation in a patient with high suspicion of IE. Next imaging step and why?

Answer

Transesophageal echo — ~95% sensitive vs ~65% for TTE; also sizes the vegetation to estimate embolic risk.

Topic 7

Bactericidal, long, often surgical

Managing IE

AbbreviationsMSSA / MRSA = methicillin-sensitive / -resistant S. aureus · PCN = penicillin · IV = intravenous · GNR = gram-negative rods
🔑 Principles
  • Aggressive — response is slow (biofilm, ongoing septic emboli).
  • Bactericidal, not bacteriostatic — you must kill the organisms already there.
  • Cultures before antibiotics. Stable patient → wait for the 3 blood cultures. Acutely ill → draw 3 sets, then start empiric therapy immediately.
  • Empiric regimen: cover staph (MRSA + MSSA, incl. S. epidermidis), strep (incl. viridans) and enterococci → vancomycin + cefepime or ceftriaxone.
  • Surgery frequently: valve replacement (~half of patients eventually), drain exudate, remove splenic/renal abscesses.

Q: An acutely ill patient with suspected IE is in the ED. What must happen before the first antibiotic dose, and what is the empiric regimen?

Answer

Draw three separate blood-culture sets (pre-treatment antibiotics are the commonest cause of culture-negative IE), then start vancomycin + cefepime (or ceftriaxone) to cover MRSA/MSSA, CoNS, streptococci and enterococci.

Q: Why are bactericidal rather than bacteriostatic drugs required for IE?

Answer

The dense, poorly perfused vegetation and biofilm protect organisms from host defences, so the drug itself must kill the bacteria; suppression alone lets them regrow.

Topic 8

Mostly viral · often missed · any age

Infectious Myocarditis

AbbreviationsHHV-6 = human herpesvirus 6 · CAR = coxsackie–adenovirus receptor · CMV / EBV = cytomegalovirus / Epstein–Barr virus · RSV = respiratory syncytial virus · EMB = endomyocardial biopsy · BNP = B-type natriuretic peptide · IVIG = intravenous immunoglobulin · ECG = electrocardiogram

Inflammation of the myocardium — infectious, immune-mediated or toxic — hitting myocytes (necrosis, contractile dysfunction), the conduction system, autonomic nerves and interstitium. Often unsuspected: ~4% of unrelated autopsies show it, more in young sudden deaths. Unlike IE it affects all ages, mostly 20–50 years.

Four mechanisms of damage

1

Direct cell damage

By the agent itself (mostly viruses).

2

Cytokines / cell-mediated immunity

A general immune response (e.g. cytokine storm) injures myocytes.

3

Antigen-specific cytotoxicity

A focused immune attack on one microbial antigen.

4

Toxin cytotoxicity

A microbial toxin — best example diphtheria.

Viral agents — the main cause

VirusGenomePoint

HHV-6ds DNAAlso most common now (roseola)
Coxsackie B (enterovirus)ss RNAClassic answer from 1960s–90s (mostly children) — older board questions still use it
Adenovirusds DNAWith Coxsackie B, shares the CAR receptor on myocytes
CMV / EBV, influenza A, RSV, HIV, SARS-CoV-2—Less common

Caveat: many cases are idiopathic and get assumed viral, so the true share is unknown.

Non-viral agents

  • Spread from IE: contiguous extension from an infected valve or septic emboli.
  • Corynebacterium diphtheriae toxin — historically the leading killer of children <10, and death was usually from toxin myocarditis, not the pseudomembrane.
  • Clostridium perfringens toxin.
  • Borrelia burgdorferi (Lyme): ~10% of Lyme patients develop conduction disturbances (heart block).
  • Any bacteremic pathogen, rarely: Salmonella, Campylobacter, S. pyogenes, S. aureus, N. meningitidis, Listeria, T. pallidum.
  • Fungi (immunocompromised): Cryptococcus, Aspergillus, Candida, Coccidioides.
  • Parasites: Toxoplasma (cat owner), Plasmodium, Trypanosoma cruzi (Chagas).

Often asymptomatic. Common features: fever, chest pain, unexplained sinus tachycardia, dyspnea, heart failure, arrhythmias/palpitations, syncope, sudden death; also S3/S4 gallop, heart block or new bundle-branch block, new cardiomegaly, hepatomegaly, fatigue, arthralgias. Ask about a preceding viral prodrome (flu-like, URI or GI).

Diagnosis

  • Suspect with unexplained HF, chest pain or arrhythmia after a presumed viral illness, in a febrile systemic setting.
  • Endomyocardial biopsy = definitive (histology + immunohistochemistry; PCR for viruses) — but invasive; reserved for rapidly progressive HF. PCR can find bystander viruses.
  • ECG: ST elevation, tachy/bradyarrhythmias. Echo: dilation of all 4 chambers, diffuse ventricular hypokinesis.
  • Troponin T (longer-lasting) and troponin I (more cardiac-specific); ↑ CRP/ESR; BNP (heart working too hard).
  • Virus from another site or rising titres/IgM — circumstantial only.

Treatment

Mostly supportive — treat the heart failure and arrhythmias: diuretics, RAS blockers, β-blockers. Sometimes IVIG or interferon for infectious forms. Specific antibacterial/antifungal/antiparasitic drugs when the agent is known. Antivirals have no proven efficacy.

Q: A 28-year-old develops new heart failure two weeks after a febrile illness; EMB shows lymphocytic infiltrates with myocyte necrosis. Which viruses are most often found in adults today, and which older answer shares a receptor with adenovirus?

Answer

Parvovirus B19 and HHV-6 in current data; Coxsackie B is the classic older answer — it and adenovirus use the coxsackie–adenovirus receptor (CAR).

Q: A child with a grey pharyngeal pseudomembrane dies a week later of arrhythmia. Mechanism?

Answer

Diphtheria toxin myocarditis — historically the main cause of diphtheria deaths, more than airway obstruction.

Q: A hiker in Connecticut with a recent expanding rash presents with syncope and complete heart block. Diagnosis?

Answer

Lyme carditis (Borrelia burgdorferi) — ~10% of Lyme patients develop conduction disease.

⭐ Distinctive pain pattern

The most common pericardial disorder

Infectious (Acute) Pericarditis

AbbreviationsCMV / EBV = cytomegalovirus / Epstein–Barr virus · TB = tuberculosis · ECG = electrocardiogram · TTE = transthoracic echo · NSAID = non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug · ESR / CRP = inflammatory markers · CBC = complete blood count · SLE = systemic lupus erythematosus · RA = rheumatoid arthritis

The sac

Double-walled: outer fibrous (inelastic) layer + inner serous membrane, with ~15–50 mL of fluid between. Jobs: keeps the heart positioned in the mediastinum, prevents over-filling, lubricates, and (sometimes) blocks spread of infection from neighbouring organs.

Causes

Infection, direct injury, radiation, drugs/toxins, malignancy, autoimmune (SLE, RA). In the US most acute pericarditis is attributed to viruses (again: no agent found → "viral").

ClassAgents
Viruses (most common)Historic (children): enteroviruses (Coxsackie, echo), influenza · Adults: Parvo B19, CMV, EBV, HIV, SARS-CoV-2
Bacteria → "purulent pericarditis"S. aureus, streptococci (pneumococcus, viridans), Haemophilus; M. tuberculosis in endemic countries (needs supporting findings, not just geography)
Fungi (immunocompromised)Candida, Aspergillus; rarely environmental Blastomyces, Histoplasma, Coccidioides
Parasites (rare)Toxoplasma, Entamoeba, Echinococcus (tapeworm)

Clinical picture

Acute onset, ~3-week course, can recur. Non-specific prodrome: low-grade fever, fatigue, cough, dyspnea (worse lying down), hiccups.

🔑 The pain is the giveaway
  • Sharp / stabbing, anterior chest
  • Worse with coughing, inspiration, and lying flat
  • Better sitting up and leaning forward
  • Radiates to the left trapezius ridge
  • Pericardial friction rub — scratchy, squeaky; essentially only in acute pericarditis ("that's the rub").
  • ECG: ST elevation + PR depression.
  • Effusion: not required, but restricts cardiac filling when present.
🎈

Myopericarditis

Pericarditis predominates; myocardial involvement present; normal ventricular function.

💪

Perimyocarditis

Myocarditis predominates → reduced ventricular function, with pericardial features.

Diagnosis

History (recent viral illness) + exam; ↑ ESR/CRP/CBC; troponin for myocardial damage; chest X-ray, ECG, TTE (effusion). Diagnose with ≥2 of 4:

  1. Sharp chest pain improved by sitting up
  2. Pericardial friction rub
  3. ECG changesST elevation + PR depression
  4. Pericardial effusion(not necessary on its own)

Treatment

Reduce inflammation: NSAIDs + colchicine (glucocorticoids if NSAIDs can't be used). Empiric parenteral antimicrobials if bacterial or fungal is suspected. Antivirals: efficacy not proven.

Q: A 30-year-old has sharp chest pain radiating to the left trapezius, worse lying flat and better leaning forward, a week after a cold; ECG shows diffuse ST elevation with PR depression. Diagnosis and first-line treatment?

Answer

Acute (viral) pericarditis — treat with NSAIDs + colchicine.

Q: How do myopericarditis and perimyocarditis differ?

Answer

Myopericarditis: pericarditis dominates, ventricular function normal. Perimyocarditis: myocarditis dominates, ventricular function reduced.

IE — WHO, WHY, HOW

Basics

  • Non-contagious; valves (native/prosthetic), septal defects, shunts, devices; untreated ≈ fatal
  • Acute: S. aureus, normal valve, <6 wk, broad Abx · Subacute: viridans, damaged valve, weeks–months
  • Now ~75% acute; US highest incidence; hospital mortality 15–20%, 1-y 40%; ~½ need surgery

Risk

  • >50 y, men; dental disease/procedures; structural disease (75%); RHD 75%→9%; MVP ~30%
  • Devices/CIED ~23%; hemodialysis 300/100k; diabetes; immunosuppression; prior IE
  • IDU: 20–30 y, S. aureus, talc → tricuspid (60%), saliva → oral flora

Pathogenesis

  • Injury (turbulence) → platelet–fibrin nidus (sterile) → bacteremia → adhere → colonise → biofilm (500× resistant, slow growth) → vegetation (10⁹/g)
  • Vegetation → seeding, valve destruction, septic emboli
  • Mitral 28–45% > aortic; tricuspid rare except IDU
MICROBES · SIGNS · DUKE

Organisms

  • S. aureus 31% (GPC, catalase+, coagulase+) · viridans 17% (sanguinis, bovis, mutans, mitior) · CoNS 11% (prosthetic) · enterococci 11% (catheters)
  • S. gallolyticus → colon cancer/IBD
  • HACEK, GNR 2% each · anaerobes (odontogenic) · fungi (Candida, Aspergillus; immunocompromised; <50% survive) · viruses: no
  • Culture-neg: Abx before cultures; Coxiella, Bartonella (PCR)

Clinical

  • Fever 90% · murmur 85% (MR > AR > TR)
  • Splenomegaly, petechiae ~40%; splinters
  • <10%: clubbing; Osler (painful, immune complex); Janeway (painless, septic emboli); Roth (immune complex)
  • Right-sided: S. aureus 70–80%, septic pulmonary emboli, often no murmur

Diagnosis

  • Definite: 2 major · 1 major + 3 minor · 5 minor · Possible: 1+1 or 3 minor
  • Major: typical organism ≥2 sets (rare ≥3); Coxiella/Bartonella PCR or IgG >1:800; echo/CT vegetation, abscess, perforation, new regurgitation; PET/CT
  • Minor: predisposition (incl. IDU, HOCM), fever ≥38 °C, vascular, immunologic, micro
  • RF+ 50–60%; low-level bacteremia → 3 sets · TTE 65% → TOE 95%
TREATMENT · MYO · PERI

IE treatment

  • Bactericidal; cultures first; empiric vancomycin + cefepime/ceftriaxone
  • MSSA: nafcillin/oxacillin · MRSA, S. epidermidis: vanc + rifampin (≥6 wk)
  • Viridans: PCN G or ceftriaxone 4 wk · enterococci: PCN/amp + gent, or vanc · HACEK: ceftriaxone
  • Coxiella: quinolone + doxy · Bartonella: doxy + rifampin
  • Fungi: ampho/echinocandin → azole for life

Myocarditis

  • 20–50 y; 4% of autopsies; mechanisms: direct, cytokine, antigen-specific, toxin
  • Parvo B19, HHV-6 now; Coxsackie B, adeno (CAR) classic
  • Diphtheria toxin (main diphtheria killer), C. perfringens, Lyme 10% heart block, T. cruzi, Toxo
  • Lymphocytic = viral · eosinophilic = parasite
  • Forms: subacute, acute ≤3 mo, chronic >3 mo, fulminant
  • EMB definitive; ECG ST↑; echo 4-chamber dilation; troponin, BNP · Tx supportive (diuretics, RASi, β-blockers); antivirals unproven

Pericarditis

  • Viral most (enteroviruses, Parvo, CMV); purulent = S. aureus, pneumococcus; TB endemic
  • Sharp pain, worse lying/inspiration, better leaning forward, left trapezius; rub; ST↑ + PR↓
  • Dx ≥2 of: pain, rub, ECG, effusion · ~3 wk, recurs
  • Myopericarditis (normal EF) vs perimyocarditis (↓ EF)
  • NSAIDs + colchicine (steroids if no NSAIDs)
