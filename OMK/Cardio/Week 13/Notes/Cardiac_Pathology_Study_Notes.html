Orientation

Normal heart → six ways it fails

The Pump & How It Breaks

Cardiac pathology is mostly about diseases that damage the heart's pump function, and their downstream consequences. As with the vascular lecture, Dr. Gardner asks you to read the Robbins heart chapter once straight through.

How to read the colours Red bold = lecturer emphasis / likely tested Blue bold = key fact or mechanism Yellow = number to memorise Board add-on = from First Aid / Pathoma / AMBOSS, not said in lecture
AbbreviationsLV / RV = left / right ventricle · SA / AV node = sinoatrial / atrioventricular node · ANP = atrial natriuretic peptide · RCA = right coronary artery · LAD = left anterior descending · LCx = left circumflex

Normal heart — the facts the lecture leans on later

  • About 1/200 of body weight. LV myocytes are arranged in a spiral; RV less ordered.
  • Stretch → ANP release → kidney loses sodium → volume falls. The heart has a say in blood volume.
  • Valves are exquisitely thin, have no blood supply of their own, are supported at their roots and (AV valves) by papillary muscles, and are immune-privileged — one reason valve replacements work so well.
  • Conduction: SA node → AV node → bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje network. Every level beats on its own, each slower than the one above; the SA node leads only because it is fastest. Ablate it and the AV node takes over (relative bradycardia).
  • The myocardium is perfused during diastole. So prolonged high heart rates (short diastole) can themselves cause ischemia.
  • All coronary arteries (RCA, left main → LAD + LCx) arise in the shadow of the aortic valve cusps, with very little redundancy — blocking one is a major event.

Six modes of cardiac dysfunction

1

Pump failure

Weak or stiff muscle.

2

Obstruction to flow

→ pressure overload (e.g. stenosis, hypertension).

3

Regurgitant flow

→ volume overload — the heart does the same job twice.

4

Shunts

Also volume overload; may mix blood.

5

Conduction problems

Arrhythmias, block, sudden death.

6

Rupture

Wall, septum, papillary muscle, aorta.

Topic 1

An end-stage disease with a cancer-level prognosis

Hypertrophy & Congestive Heart Failure

AbbreviationsCHF = congestive heart failure · HFrEF = heart failure with reduced ejection fraction · HFpEF = heart failure with preserved ejection fraction · EF = ejection fraction · LVH = left ventricular hypertrophy · LHF / RHF = left / right heart failure · MI = myocardial infarction · COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease · NO = nitric oxide
🔑 Definition

Congestive heart failure = the heart cannot meet the body's metabolic demands. 50% five-year survival — comparable to many cancers. Hypertrophy + dysfunction, loss of elasticity, increased stiffness.

How overload becomes failure

  1. Three common causes, two kinds of overloadHypertension → pressure overload · valve disease → pressure (stenosis) or volume (regurgitation) overload · MI → regional dysfunction with volume overload.
  2. ↑ Cardiac work → ↑ wall stress → myocyte stretch
  3. Hypertrophy and/or dilationbigger, heavier heart; ↑ protein synthesis; immediate-early genes and fetal gene programme switch on; cell-stress programmes.
  4. Fibroblast hyperplasia → fibrosisand the existing blood supply can't keep up with the bigger muscle.
  5. Cardiac dysfunctionheart failure (systolic/diastolic), arrhythmias (loss of vascularity, conduction interruption), neurohumoral stimulation.

Gross: concentric LVH = massively thick LV wall, normal RV. When dilation joins in, total mass rises but the wall looks normal or even thin. Histology at the same magnification: hypertrophic myocytes are huge with enormous "boxcar" nuclei Board add-on — endothelial nuclei next to them stay normal-sized, which is how you know it's the same magnification.

Left vs right heart failure

🫁

Left HF — "short of breath"

Usually from MI or hypertension. Blood backs up into the lungs → pulmonary edema, ↑ pulmonary pressure; forward: poor peripheral perfusion. Board add-on "Heart failure cells" = hemosiderin-laden alveolar macrophages.

🦶

Right HF — "swollen ankles"

Usually secondary to left HF. Venous back-up into the viscera → congestive hepatomegaly ("nutmeg liver") → eventually cardiac cirrhosis; splenomegaly; peripheral edema and effusions.

One side failing eventually drags the other down, so most patients end up with both.

Q: A 78-year-old woman with long-standing hypertension, obesity and COPD has dyspnea and edema. Echo: thick LV, EF 62%. Which form of heart failure, and what hypertrophy pattern?

Answer

HFpEF (diastolic failure from a stiff ventricle) with concentric hypertrophy — the pattern favoured in older women with these comorbidities.

Q: At autopsy a liver has a mottled red-and-tan cut surface. What is the most likely cardiac cause, and what can it progress to?

Answer

Right heart failure (usually secondary to left) → chronic passive congestion = nutmeg liver → cardiac cirrhosis if prolonged.

Q: Pressure overload vs volume overload: which adds sarcomeres in parallel and which in series?

Answer

Pressure overload → in parallel → concentric hypertrophy (thick wall, small cavity). Volume overload → in series → eccentric hypertrophy (dilated cavity).

Shunts left→right vs right→left

Congenital Heart Disease

AbbreviationsVSD / ASD = ventricular / atrial septal defect · PDA = patent ductus arteriosus · PFO = patent foramen ovale · ToF = tetralogy of Fallot · TGA = transposition of the great arteries · AVSD = atrioventricular septal defect · RVH = right ventricular hypertrophy · L→R / R→L = left-to-right / right-to-left
🧠 The organising rule

Acyanotic (L→R) defects: extra blood goes from the left heart back to the right, but everything that reaches the body is oxygenated → excess pulmonary flow, volume overload. Cyanotic (R→L) defects: deoxygenated blood reaches the systemic circulation → cyanosis.

Any long-standing L→R shunt (VSD, ASD, PDA) → pulmonary hypertension → RV pressure exceeds LV → shunt reverses to R→L → late cyanosis, clubbing, polycythemia.

Q: A 40-year-old with a leg DVT suddenly develops a stroke. Echo bubble study shows right-to-left passage of contrast at the atrial level. Mechanism?

Answer

Paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale / ASD: a venous clot crosses to the left atrium and enters the systemic circulation.

Q: Name the four components of tetralogy of Fallot and the one that determines how cyanotic the child is.

Answer

VSD, RV outflow tract stenosis, overriding aorta, RVH. The degree of RV outflow obstruction sets how much blood shunts right-to-left.

Q: A newborn with transposition of the great arteries and an intact ventricular septum turns deeply cyanotic on day 2. Why now?

Answer

The ductus arteriosus is closing: without a VSD, the PDA was the only (unstable) place where the two parallel circulations could mix.

Ischemic Heart Disease & the MI Timeline

AbbreviationsMI = myocardial infarction · MINOCA / INOCA = myocardial infarction / ischemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries · IHD = ischemic heart disease · NOS3 = nitric oxide synthase 3 (endothelial) · EM = electron microscope · LV = left ventricle

The spectrum

SyndromeMechanism
Angina pectoris (stable)Exertional chest pain from a stable stenosis: supply can't rise with demand
Crescendo (unstable) anginaWorsening over days–months: unstable plaques, progressive stenosis
Acute MIThrombosis in a coronary artery, usually from plaque rupture
MINOCA / INOCAInfarct or ischemia without obstructive coronary disease — recently recognised, ~10–15% of MIs
Chronic IHDProgressive myocyte drop-out from many small, silent, healed infarcts; common in diabetics who don't feel the pain
🧠 What causes MINOCA / INOCA?

Associations: hypertension, aortic stenosis, anemia. Core idea: microvascular / endothelial dysfunction showing up as reduced flow reserve — e.g. diabetic small-vessel disease, and a genetic failure of small-vessel relaxation from NOS3 polymorphisms.

Symptoms — and who gets missed

👨

Classic (men)

Chest pain, diaphoresis (sweating), nausea, dyspnea.

🩸

Diabetics

Pain often attenuated by neuropathy → silent infarcts.

👩

Women

Persistently under-diagnosed: lower index of suspicion (low premenopausal incidence) + higher pain tolerance / attributing pain elsewhere.

⚠️ Why the timeline matters clinically

The wall is softest at ~3–7 days (neutrophils and macrophages have digested dead muscle but collagen hasn't arrived) — that's the rupture window. The lecturer also admitted the 0–½ h EM changes would be hard to call blind.

At autopsy, examine the coronaries serially — you usually find a fresh thrombus, often in the LAD.

Q: A man dies 2 days after a large anterior MI. What will the infarct look like grossly and microscopically?

Answer

Grossly mottled with a yellow-tan centre; microscopically coagulative necrosis with loss of nuclei/striations and a brisk neutrophil infiltrate (1–3 day window).

Q: A patient gets a stent 3 hours after LAD occlusion. Biopsy of the zone shows hypereosinophilic myocytes with dense transverse bands. What is this and why?

Answer

Contraction band necrosis — reperfusion injury: calcium floods into damaged myocytes when flow returns, causing hypercontraction.

Q: A 60-year-old diabetic woman has progressive heart failure but has never reported chest pain. Autopsy shows many patchy scars. Name the entity.

Answer

Chronic ischemic heart disease from multiple silent, healed infarcts — diabetic neuropathy blunted the pain.

Troponin, ECG, anatomy, complications

Diagnosing MI & What Goes Wrong After

AbbreviationsTnT / TnI = cardiac troponin T / I · CK-MB = creatine kinase MB · STEMI / NSTEMI = ST-elevation / non-ST-elevation MI · PE = pulmonary embolism · RCA / LAD / LCx = right coronary / left anterior descending / left circumflex · RBBB = right bundle branch block · SVT = supraventricular tachycardia · VF = ventricular fibrillation · AKI = acute kidney injury · ARDS = acute respiratory distress syndrome · CPR = cardiopulmonary resuscitation

Laboratory diagnosis — troponin

  • Dying myocytes release cardiac troponins — more sensitive and specific than the historical CK-MB.
  • Rise is rapid after symptoms; the size of the rise tracks the amount of injury. STEMIs peak far higher than NSTEMIs.
  • Modest troponin release also happens with a marathon, PE, myocarditis, chronic heart failure — but without the peak height or the steep rise of an MI.
  • High-sensitivity TnT or TnI (choice depends on your lab): most patients are ruled in/out on the first test; equivocal ones are re-tested at 1 or 3 h — the rate of change is diagnostic. Bedside rapid tests exist in some markets.

ECG diagnosis

Less definitive than troponin, but useful in the field and for following the course: ST elevation (hence STEMI) → later flipped T waves → Q waves. Abnormal leads localise the infarct; rhythm changes signal ischemia of the conduction system.

Anatomy dictates outcome

💀

Occlude the LAD

  • Kills the LV anterior wall — the main source of cardiac output
  • "The widowmaker" (also makes widowers)
  • Right bundle branch block (septal branches supply the right bundle)
  • More distal = smaller, better-defined infarct
🐢

Occlude the RCA

  • Ischemia of the conduction system: RCA feeds the SA node (~60%) and AV node (~90%)
  • Bradycardia / heart block
  • Inferior wall and papillary muscle infarcts → risk of valve failure
  • More distal = better defined

Complications

ComplicationTiming / detail
Contractile dysfunction → CHFCardiogenic shock in ~10%, typically when ≥40% of the LV is infarcted
ArrhythmiaEarly; RCA infarcts → bradycardia; SVTs, VF. The strongest reason to keep MI patients in hospital
Papillary muscle dysfunction / rupture→ acute mitral regurgitation — volume overload on an already weak heart; not an uncommon cause of death
RV infarct→ right heart failure
Myocardial rupture3–7 days — the soft, neutrophil-digested phase before fibroblasts repair it (free wall → tamponade; septum → L→R shunt)
PericarditisEarly fibrinous 2–3 days; autoimmune Dressler syndrome >2 weeks
Chamber dilationFrom muscle loss
Mural thrombus → embolismDamaged endocardium → LV clot → stroke etc.

Mortality: ~30% in year 1, then ~3% per year.

🩺 Other organs after MI / cardiogenic shock

Low flow everywhere → ischemia-reperfusion injury and systemic inflammation: acute kidney injury (in ~40% of patients who survive CPR), acute hepatocellular injury (↑ liver enzymes), acute lung injury / ARDS.

Q: Five days after an MI, a patient suddenly becomes hypotensive with muffled heart sounds and distended neck veins. What happened, and why at this time?

Answer

Free-wall rupture → hemopericardium → tamponade. Days 3–7: the infarct is maximally soft (neutrophils/macrophages have removed muscle, collagen not yet laid down).

Q: An inferior-wall STEMI patient develops a heart rate of 38. Which artery, and why the slow rate?

Answer

RCA occlusion — it supplies the SA node (~60%) and AV node (~90%), so ischemia causes sinus bradycardia/AV block.

Q: Troponin is mildly elevated in a healthy runner the morning after a marathon. How does this differ from an MI curve?

Answer

A low, flat, short-lived bump — no steep rise and no high peak. Serial testing (1–3 h) showing the rate of change separates it from infarction.

Topic 5

Right heart failure that starts in the lungs

Cor Pulmonale

AbbreviationsRV = right ventricle · RHF / LHF = right / left heart failure · COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease · GPA = granulomatosis with polyangiitis

Cor pulmonale (an "ancient term") = increased resistance in the pulmonary circulation → RV pressure overload and hypertrophy → right heart failure. Most right heart failure is secondary to left failure; cor pulmonale is the rest, caused by lung disease.

CategoryExamples
Lung parenchymaCOPD, diffuse interstitial fibrosis, pneumoconioses, cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis
Pulmonary vesselsRecurrent thromboemboli, primary pulmonary hypertension, arteritis (e.g. GPA), drug/toxin/radiation-induced obstruction (incl. chemotherapy), tumour microemboli
Chest movementKyphoscoliosis, marked obesity (sleep apnea, Pickwickian), neuromuscular disease
Pulmonary arterial constrictionMetabolic acidosis, hypoxemia, chronic altitude sickness (best-known), airway obstruction, idiopathic alveolar hypoventilation

Q: A 65-year-old heavy smoker with severe COPD develops ankle edema, JVD and an enlarged tender liver; echo shows a thick, dilated RV with a normal LV. Diagnosis?

Answer

Cor pulmonale — hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and lung destruction raise pulmonary vascular resistance → RV hypertrophy and right heart failure without left-sided disease.

Topic 6

Stenosis = pressure load · regurgitation = volume load

Valvular Disease & Rheumatic Heart Disease

AbbreviationsAS / AR = aortic stenosis / regurgitation · MS / MR = mitral stenosis / regurgitation · MVP = mitral valve prolapse · RHD = rheumatic heart disease · GAS = group A streptococcus · ARF = acute rheumatic fever
🔑 The lecture's two-line summary

The important valve diseases are on the left (it does the cardiac output).
Aortic: stenosis = bicuspid valve or ageing (calcification); insufficiency = expanding aortic root.
Mitral: stenosis = rheumatic heart disease; insufficiency = myxomatous degeneration (MVP) or heart failure.

LesionCauses (slide 71)
Mitral stenosisPost-inflammatory scarring — rheumatic heart disease
Aortic stenosisRheumatic scarring · senile calcific AS · calcification of a congenitally bicuspid valve
Mitral regurgitationLeaflets: rheumatic, infective endocarditis, MVP, fen-phen fibrosis · Tensor apparatus: papillary muscle rupture/dysfunction, chordae rupture · Cavity/annulus: LV enlargement (myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy), calcified mitral ring
Aortic regurgitationValve: rheumatic, infective endocarditis · Aortic root: degenerative dilation, syphilitic aortitis, ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, Marfan

MVP (myxomatous degeneration) — a not-uncommon disease of young women of unclear cause; also a cause of sudden death (Topic 10).

Rheumatic heart disease

Now rare in the West, largely because strep throats are treated promptly with antibiotics.

  1. Group A strep pharyngitisin a child/teen.
  2. Molecular mimicryanti-streptococcal M-protein antibodies (and T cells) cross-react with cardiac self-antigens in ~3%.
  3. Acute rheumatic feverpolyarthritis, pancarditis, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum, chorea.
  4. Healing = fibrosisof the attacked tissue → chronic valve scarring → mitral stenosis.
🔬 Histology

Aschoff bodies — clumps of T cells, plasma cells and macrophages. Board add-on The activated macrophages with wavy "caterpillar" chromatin are Anitschkow cells. Chronic valves: thick leaflets, fused commissures, short thick chordae → "fish-mouth" mitral stenosis.

🧠 Jones major criteria = "J♥NES" Board add-on

Joints (migratory polyarthritis), ♥ carditis, Nodules (subcutaneous), Erythema marginatum, Sydenham chorea — exactly the five features on the slide.

Q: A 35-year-old immigrant with a childhood history of "joint pains and a heart murmur" now has atrial fibrillation and an opening snap with a diastolic rumble. Which valve, and what is the pathology?

Answer

Mitral stenosis from chronic rheumatic heart disease: fibrotic, fused commissures after cross-reactive anti-M-protein immunity to group A strep.

Q: A 55-year-old has severe calcific aortic stenosis — "early" for senile AS. Most likely underlying valve anatomy?

Answer

A congenitally bicuspid aortic valve, which calcifies and stenoses decades earlier than a normal tricuspid valve.

Topic 7
⭐ "Easily missed, easy to treat"

Vegetations, emboli and immune complexes

Infective Endocarditis

AbbreviationsIE = infective endocarditis · S. aureus = Staphylococcus aureus · S. viridans = viridans-group streptococci · IVDU = intravenous drug use · SLE = systemic lupus erythematosus · CNS = central nervous system · RF = rheumatoid factor
🩺 Keep a high index of suspicion

IE is easily missed and usually easy to treat. Ask about prior valve disease (rheumatic fever) or previous surgery for congenital disease (e.g. coarctation repair). Watch for small wedge-shaped infarcts in organs on scans, and the peripheral signs below.

Acute vs subacute

⚡

Acute

S. aureus on a normal valve. Rapid, destructive; needs urgent surgery to survive.

🐌

Subacute (most common)

Strep viridans on an abnormal valve (e.g. old rheumatic damage). Protracted; sometimes cured with antibiotics alone, valve left OK.

  • Can also seed other vascular structures — vessel and valve repairs.
  • Non-infective immune-complex vegetations occur in lupus (Libman–Sacks) and malignancy (marantic Board add-on).

From vegetation to signs

  1. Vegetationsemi-organised fibrin clot with bacteria and immune cells on the valve.
  2. Fragments break off → septic embolithrough the arterial tree → infarcts, splinter hemorrhages, CNS abscesses/lesions, Janeway lesions.
  3. Systemic inflammation → immune complexeslodge in small vessels → Roth spots (retina), Osler nodes, glomerulonephritis, positive rheumatoid factor.
🧠 Osler vs Janeway Board add-on

Osler = "Ouch": painful raised nodules on finger/toe pads (immune complex). Janeway = painless flat macules on palms/soles (septic emboli). FROM JANE: Fever, Roth, Osler, Murmur, Janeway, Anemia, Nail-bed (splinter) hemorrhage, Emboli.

Diagnostic criteria (modified Duke, as on slide 73)

TierCriteria
PathologicMicroorganisms (culture or histology) in a vegetation, an embolus from one, or an intracardiac abscess; or histologic active endocarditis

Minor clinicalPredisposing lesion or IV drug use · fever (may wax and wane) · vascular lesions: major emboli, septic pulmonary infarcts, mycotic aneurysm, intracranial hemorrhage, conjunctival hemorrhage, Janeway lesions · immunologic: glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots, rheumatoid factor · single positive culture for an unusual organism
🦷 "Why does the dentist ask about heart disease?"

A tooth extraction throws a large bolus of mouth bacteria — including strep viridans — into the blood. Patients with prior valve injury get prophylactic antibiotics.

Q: An IV drug user has fever, a new murmur, and septic pulmonary emboli; blood cultures grow gram-positive cocci in clusters within 24 h. Acute or subacute, and which valve is typical?

Answer

Acute IE from S. aureus, attacking a normal valve — in IV drug users classically the tricuspid Board add-on. Rapidly destructive; may need surgery.

Q: Three weeks after dental work, a man with old rheumatic mitral disease has low-grade fevers, painful nodules on his finger pads and retinal hemorrhages with pale centres. Organism and the mechanism of the skin and eye findings?

Answer

Subacute IE from strep viridans on a previously damaged valve. Osler nodes and Roth spots = immune-complex deposition in small vessels.

Cardiomyopathies

HCM / HOCM — sudden death in young athletes

Genetics

Largely inherited

Sarcomere mutations: MYBPC3, MYH7 (β-myosin heavy chain), TnI, TnT and others. Board add-on Autosomal dominant.

Physiology

Two problems at once

Thick wall limits diastolic filling + the hypertrophied septum interferes with mitral closure → too little in, and some regurgitated back to the LA in systole.

Histology

Pathognomonic look

Myofibre disarray — hugely hypertrophied myocytes in a swirling, spiral pattern.

Why they die

Sudden cardiac death

Probably transient hypoxia → fatal arrhythmia.

Treatment

Rx

"Do less" (stop strenuous exercise) · septal reduction by surgery or ethanol ablation of septal vessels · implanted defibrillator (essential).

🍺 Historical artifact (explicitly not on the exam)

Cobalt-beer cardiomyopathy, 1964–66: cobalt salts were added to canned beer to stabilise the foam head, and heavy drinkers developed a specific cardiomyopathy.

Q: A 17-year-old collapses and dies during a basketball game. His father died suddenly at 35. Autopsy: asymmetric septal hypertrophy. What will histology show, and what gene family is mutated?

Answer

Myofibre disarray of hypertrophied myocytes; sarcomere genes (β-myosin heavy chain MYH7, myosin-binding protein C MYBPC3, troponins) — hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Q: A woman treated for breast cancer with doxorubicin develops dyspnea two years later; echo shows a dilated LV with EF 25%. Classification?

Answer

Dilated cardiomyopathy (systolic dysfunction) from anthracycline cardiotoxicity — dose-dependent, hence lifetime dose limits.

Q: Which cardiomyopathy pattern does cardiac amyloidosis produce, and what is its pericardial mimic?

Answer

Restrictive (diastolic, impaired relaxation, EF often preserved). Mimic: constrictive pericarditis.

🦠

Viral myocarditis — most common in North America

  • Typical patient: otherwise healthy young person with URI symptoms plus cardiac symptoms (chest pain, dyspnea)
  • Mild troponin rise
  • Management: bed rest — "get them off their feet" is the single most important step, plus supportive care (fluids → up to transplant)
📊

COVID numbers

Myocarditis after COVID-19 infection ≈ 1 in 5,000. After vaccination ≈ 1 in 15,000–50,000, with a very benign course.

Chagas disease (American trypanosomiasis)

Called out because its epidemiology is changing: endemic to South America and now considered endemic to the US.

  1. Entrythe parasite gets in from the feces of the triatomine ("kissing") bug.
  2. Acute phasetransient, nonspecific systemic inflammation.
  3. Amastigotes lodge and multiplyespecially in heart muscle and the nerves of the GI tract.
  4. Chronic phasedecades later in ~20%: cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, sudden death, megacolon, megaesophagus.

Think of it in a patient in late middle age with unexplained cardiac symptoms and relevant exposure.

Q: A 22-year-old has a week of cold symptoms, then pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea; troponin is mildly raised and coronaries are clean. Most likely virus and the key management step?

Answer

Coxsackie B viral myocarditis; bed rest (no exertion) plus supportive care.

Q: A 55-year-old who grew up in rural Bolivia has dilated cardiomyopathy, a right bundle branch block and dysphagia. Myocardial biopsy shows intracellular parasite nests. Organism and form?

Answer

Trypanosoma cruzi amastigotes — chronic Chagas disease (cardiomyopathy + megaesophagus).

Ischemic rhythms · channelopathies · sudden death · damage we cause

Rhythm Disorders, Sudden Death & Iatrogenic Injury

AbbreviationsAF = atrial fibrillation · SSS = sick sinus syndrome · LQTS / SQTS = long / short QT syndrome · CPVT = catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia · LOF / GOF = loss / gain of function · WPW = Wolff–Parkinson–White · SCD = sudden cardiac death · MVP = mitral valve prolapse · HER2 = human epidermal growth factor receptor 2

Predominantly ischemic

  • Atrial fibrillation — very common in the elderly. Two consequences: atria contribute 20–30% of output, so AF can tip a weak ventricle into failure; and fibrillating atria don't empty → clots → embolic stroke (the classic stroke of the elderly; now well managed with modern anticoagulants).
  • Sick sinus syndrome → bradycardia.
  • Heart block → PR prolongation through to bradycardia.

Q: A 19-year-old faints when startled by an alarm clock; ECG shows QTc 520 ms. Which channel is most commonly defective, and in which direction?

Answer

Long QT syndrome — most often loss of function of a K⁺ channel (KCNQ1 or KCNH2), delaying repolarisation → risk of fatal arrhythmia.

Q: Why does atrial fibrillation cause strokes?

Answer

Fibrillating atria don't empty efficiently → stasis → thrombus (left atrial appendage) → systemic embolism to the brain. Treated with anticoagulation.

Full etiology list (slide 84): infections (viruses, pyogenic bacteria, TB, fungi, parasites) · immune (rheumatic fever, SLE, scleroderma, post-cardiotomy, post-MI Dressler, drug hypersensitivity) · miscellaneous (MI, uremia, cardiac surgery, neoplasia, trauma, radiation).

⚠️ Two post-MI pericarditides

Early (2–3 days): fibrinous, over a transmural infarct. Dressler (>2 weeks): autoimmune, against newly exposed cardiac antigens.

Q: When a pathologist peels the pericardium off at autopsy, shaggy strands connect it to the heart. Name the pattern and two common causes.

Answer

Fibrinous ("bread-and-butter") pericarditis — after MI (early or Dressler) or cardiac surgery; also uremia.

Topic 12

Only one is common

Tumours of the Heart

AbbreviationsLA = left atrium · IL-6 = interleukin 6
🎈

Left atrial myxoma — the one to know

  • Benign; grows near the valve root
  • Ball-valve effect: symptoms are positional — lying down lets it flop into the mitral orifice (case reports of collapse or death on lying down)
  • Releases lots of IL-6 → constitutional symptoms: intermittent fevers etc.
  • Board add-on "Tumour plop" in diastole; histology = stellate cells in myxoid stroma
📋

Everything else

  • Metastases — usually incidental
  • Sarcomas — vanishingly rare
  • Mesothelioma — ~1% involve the pericardium → constrictive state; occupational (asbestos)

Q: A 50-year-old woman has weight loss, intermittent fevers, a high ESR, and dizziness that comes on when she lies down. Echo shows a pedunculated LA mass. Diagnosis, and which cytokine explains the systemic symptoms?

Answer

Left atrial myxoma (positional ball-valve obstruction of the mitral orifice); IL-6 release causes the constitutional symptoms.

NORMAL · HEART FAILURE · CONGENITAL

Normal heart facts

  • Myocardium perfused in diastole → tachycardia can cause ischemia
  • SA node leads because fastest; valves avascular, immune-privileged
  • All coronaries arise behind aortic cusps; little redundancy
  • Six failure modes: pump, obstruction (pressure), regurgitation (volume), shunt, conduction, rupture

Heart failure

  • 50% 5-year survival
  • HTN → pressure · valves → pressure/volume · MI → volume → wall stress → hypertrophy/dilation → fibrosis → failure + arrhythmia
  • Pressure = concentric (parallel); volume = eccentric (series); boxcar nuclei
  • HFrEF: male, MI, eccentric, myocyte death, titin cleavage · HFpEF: older women, COPD, inflammation, concentric, ↑↑ perivascular fibrosis, stiff N2B titin, under-treated
  • LHF → pulmonary edema, dyspnea · RHF (usually from LHF) → nutmeg liver → cardiac cirrhosis, edema

Congenital

  • VSD commonest (42%); ~90% genetically unexplained; genes mostly transcription factors (NKX2.5, GATA4, TBX5 Holt–Oram, TBX1 DiGeorge)
  • Acyanotic L→R: VSD, ASD, PDA, PS, coarctation, AVSD, AS · Cyanotic R→L: ToF, TGA, truncus, TAPVC, tricuspid atresia
  • ASD: late diagnosis, paradoxical embolus (PFO) · VSD: RVH + pulm HTN · PDA: machinery murmur
  • ToF = VSD + RVOT stenosis + overriding aorta + RVH; cyanosis with feeds
  • TGA needs VSD (stable) or PDA (unstable) to live
ISCHEMIA · MI · COR PULMONALE

Ischemic spectrum

  • Stable angina = fixed stenosis · crescendo = unstable plaque · MI = thrombus on ruptured plaque
  • MINOCA/INOCA ~10–15%: microvascular/endothelial dysfunction, HTN, AS, anemia, diabetes, NOS3
  • Chronic IHD = many silent healed infarcts (diabetics)
  • Under-diagnosed in women; blunted pain in diabetics

Infarct clock

  • 0–½ h reversible (EM only) · ½–4 h wavy fibres
  • 4–24 h coagulative necrosis, contraction bands, dark mottling
  • 1–3 d neutrophils, yellow-tan centre
  • 3–7 d macrophages, softest → rupture
  • 7–14 d granulation tissue · 2–8 wk collagen · >2 mo dense scar
  • Reperfusion → contraction band necrosis + hemorrhage

Diagnosis & anatomy

  • Troponin (hs-TnT/TnI) replaced CK-MB; rise ∝ injury; repeat at 1–3 h — rate of change
  • Small bumps: marathon, PE, myocarditis, CHF
  • ECG: ST↑ → T inversion → Q waves; leads localise
  • LAD = widowmaker, anterior LV, RBBB · RCA → SA (60%) + AV (90%) nodes → bradycardia, inferior + papillary muscle

Complications

  • Shock ~10% (≥40% LV) · arrhythmia early · papillary rupture → MR
  • Rupture 3–7 d · pericarditis 2–3 d; Dressler >2 wk
  • Mural thrombus → stroke · aneurysm → failure
  • Mortality 30% yr 1, then 3%/yr · AKI in ~40% after CPR, ARDS, liver injury

Cor pulmonale

  • ↑ pulmonary resistance → RVH → RHF: COPD, fibrosis, recurrent PE, primary pulm HTN, kyphoscoliosis, sleep apnea, altitude
VALVES · IE · CM · MYOCARDITIS · RHYTHM · PERICARDIUM

Valves & RHD

  • AS: bicuspid, ageing · AR: root dilation (Marfan, syphilis) · MS: rheumatic · MR: MVP (young women), HF, papillary rupture
  • RHD: GAS throat → anti-M protein cross-reaction (~3%) → J♥NES → fibrosis → MS; Aschoff bodies

Infective endocarditis

  • Acute: S. aureus, normal valve, surgery · Subacute: S. viridans, abnormal valve, antibiotics
  • Emboli → infarcts, splinter, Janeway, CNS · immune complexes → Roth, Osler, GN, RF
  • Non-infective: lupus (Libman–Sacks), malignancy · dentist prophylaxis

Cardiomyopathy

  • Dilated EF <40%, systolic: alcohol, anthracyclines, peripartum, myocarditis, hemochromatosis, genetic
  • HCM 1/500, diastolic: sarcomere genes (MYH7, MYBPC3), disarray, septum blocks mitral closure, young athlete SCD → less exercise, septal reduction, ICD
  • Restrictive: amyloid, radiation; mimic = constriction

Myocarditis

  • Coxsackie B; COVID 1/5,000 (vaccine 1/15–50k, benign); URI + chest pain, mild troponin → bed rest
  • Chagas (T. cruzi, kissing bug, now US-endemic): 20% decades later → cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, megacolon/-esophagus

Rhythm & SCD

  • AF → lost atrial kick + clot → stroke
  • Long QT: K⁺ LOF (KCNQ1, KCNH2) or Na⁺ GOF (SCN5A) · Brugada: SCN5A LOF · CPVT: RYR2
  • SCD non-MI: WPW, cardiomyopathy, coronary anomalies, myocarditis, MVP, cocaine/meth
  • Iatrogenic: anthracyclines, trastuzumab, radiation, checkpoint inhibitors, QT drugs, fen-phen

Pericardium & tumours

  • Serous: SLE, RF · Fibrinous "bread-and-butter": MI, Dressler, surgery · purulent: TB, bacteria · hemorrhagic: malignancy · constrictive: scarring
  • LA myxoma: positional ball-valve, IL-6 fevers
