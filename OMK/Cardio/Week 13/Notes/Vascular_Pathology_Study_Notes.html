Orientation

How to read this lecture

High-Pressure Plumbing Meets Cell Pathology

Dr. Gardner's framing: almost every vascular disease is ordinary cell pathology (inflammation, necrosis, repair) happening inside a high-pressure plumbing system. Small problems early in life, like a collagen mutation or a low-grade infection of a vessel, can take decades of blood flow to show up. And because the pressure is so high, a minor lesion can become a catastrophe.

How to read the colours Red bold = lecturer emphasis / likely tested Blue bold = key fact or mechanism Yellow = number to memorise Board add-on = from First Aid / Pathoma / AMBOSS, not said in lecture

The cast of cells

Endothelial cells

Line every vessel + heart

Keep blood from clotting; control what crosses the wall.

Vascular smooth muscle cells

Media

Contract and relax to set vascular tone → blood pressure and flow.

Fibroblasts

Adventitia + heart

Make the collagen matrix that gives vessels their structural strength.

Cardiomyocytes

Heart

Contract rhythmically on their own; pass action potentials cell to cell in a branching pattern.

Monocytes / macrophages

Repair crew

Repair vessel damage, but can also perpetuate it, as in atherosclerotic plaque.

Platelets

Megakaryocyte fragments

Stick to any surface not covered by intact endothelium and start clotting.

🔑 The one idea that runs through everything

Intact endothelium = no clot, no inflammation, relaxed vessel. Damaged or "activated" endothelium = clotting, leak, inflammation and constriction. Atherosclerosis, hypertension, vasculitis and thrombosis are all versions of that switch being flipped.

The lecturer also asked you to read (not study) the Robbins vascular chapter once, straight through, "like a novel": the same themes repeat and will stick.

Topic 1

Normal structure → how injury starts

The Vessel Wall & the Endothelium

💪

Arteries — pressure tolerance

Elasticity is key. Aorta = elastic artery; then muscular arteries.

🔁

Capillaries — exchange

Selective permeability is key. Just endothelium + supporting pericytes.

🎈

Veins — capacitance

Expansibility is key. Thin, attenuated media; they hold the "back capacity" of blood for the next beat.

What endothelium does

  • Forms one continuous, intact surface from the cardiac chambers to every capillary and back — extremely thin, huge area, resists shear, held together by tight junctions.
  • Thinnest where gas exchange happens (alveoli). Stores clotting factors in Weibel–Palade bodies.
  • Controls white-cell traffic by opening and closing tight junctions locally.
  • Two extremes of permeability: brain (maximal sealing = BBB) vs most end-organ capillaries (permissive to macromolecules and cells).
  • Strip the endothelium off and collagen is exposed → platelets bind immediately and clotting starts.

Flow decides endothelial behaviour

Classic experiment: under laminar (smooth) flow cultured endothelial cells line up like paving stones. Turbulent flow with local high-flow spots makes them stressed, turn over faster, undergo some apoptosis and develop a rough surface. That is why plaques form at branch points.

Endothelial dysfunction

Healthy endothelium: relaxed vessel, tight permeability, controlled clotting and inflammation. Dysfunctional endothelium: vasoconstriction + hypoxia, leak/edema, thrombosis, white-cell accumulation (inflammation) — seen on labs as prolonged clotting times, raised D-dimer (fibrin breakdown product) and raised CRP (C-reactive protein).

Diseases with major endothelial dysfunction: diabetes (ultimately a microvascular disease), inflammatory bowel disease, hypertension, heart and kidney failure, stroke, atherosclerosis. Newer idea: amyloid-β is toxic to brain endothelium and may add a thrombotic route to Alzheimer disease.

The stereotyped response to injury

🧠 Mechanism

Any injury that strips endothelium (or reaches the media) → smooth muscle cells migrate into the intima, proliferate and lay down matrix → intimal thickening. It partly resolves, but the long-run tendency is toward stenosis. In large vessels, repeated injury → atherosclerosis.

Q: A surgeon scrapes the endothelium off a segment of artery. What is the very first cellular event at the exposed surface, and what does the vessel wall look like weeks later?

Answer

Platelets adhere to exposed subendothelial collagen (via von Willebrand factor) and trigger clotting. Weeks later: intimal thickening from smooth muscle cells that migrated in from the media and deposited matrix — the stereotyped response that trends toward stenosis.

Q: Why do atherosclerotic plaques cluster at arterial branch points and ostia?

Answer

Turbulent flow there activates and injures endothelium (higher turnover, rough surface, loss of the laminar-flow "quiet" state), while laminar flow elsewhere is protective.

"Hardening of the arteries" — four subtypes

Arteriosclerosis vs Atherosclerosis

AbbreviationsSMC = smooth muscle cell · IEL = internal elastic lamina · FMD = fibromuscular dysplasia · HTN = hypertension
🔑 "Two annoying words"

Arteriosclerosis = any hardening of arteries (the umbrella). Atherosclerosis = the subset caused by atheroma (atherosclerotic plaque). All atherosclerosis is arteriosclerosis; not the reverse.

⚠️ Don't confuse the look-alike names

Fibromuscular intimal hyperplasia = acquired, after stents/balloons/grafts. Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) = a rare, largely inherited condition where segments of artery wall are replaced by connective tissue → "string of beads" angiogram and multisite aneurysms. Board add-on FMD classically hits the renal arteries of young women → secondary hypertension.

Q: An 80-year-old has an incidental plain film showing "tram-track" calcification of the femoral arteries. Pulses are normal and he has no claudication. What lesion is this and why is he asymptomatic?

Answer

Mönckeberg medial calcific sclerosis — calcium sits in the media, so the lumen is not narrowed. It is a consequence of ageing and usually asymptomatic.

Q: A kidney biopsy from a patient with BP 230/140, headache and papilledema shows arterioles with concentric, laminated wall thickening and fibrinoid necrosis. Name the lesion.

Answer

Hyperplastic ("onion-skin") arteriolosclerosis with necrotizing arteriolitis — the lesion of malignant hypertension.

Topic 3

"A gift from the West" · a chronic inflammatory lesion

Atherosclerosis

AbbreviationsLDL / HDL = low- / high-density lipoprotein · CV = cardiovascular · LVH = left ventricular hypertrophy · ECM = extracellular matrix · SMC = smooth muscle cell · MI = myocardial infarction

Risk factors

DRIVER

Hyperlipidemia

The main driver; LDL is directly toxic to endothelium.

INJURY

Smoking

Major contributor to the initial endothelial injury.

INJURY

Hypertension

Pressure and turbulent flow damage endothelium.

INJURY

Diabetes

High sugar → glycation of endothelial surface proteins.

FIXED

Age, sex, family history

Family history ≈ inherited lipid handling and clotting responses.

Stack the risk factors at age 55 (high systolic BP + high cholesterol + low HDL + diabetes + smoking + LVH on ECG) and the 10-year CV risk climbs past 50%. Online risk estimators show a typical person's 10-year CV risk is about the same as their 10-year cancer risk.

Pathogenesis — response to injury, step by step

  1. Endothelial injuryHyperlipidemia, hypertension/turbulent flow, smoking, glycation, viruses that "nick" endothelium.
  2. LDL gets into the intimaand is oxidized; injured endothelium releases cytokines and adhesion molecules.
  3. Monocytes enter → macrophages → foam cellsby eating the lipid — an aberrant wound-healing response.
  4. Platelets adhere and release factors(e.g. PDGF).
  5. SMCs migrate in from the media and proliferatewith more ECM and T cells → fibrous tissue.
  6. Lipid accumulates; cells die; calcium deposits→ necrotic core, often visible on X-ray.

Anatomy of the plaque

Everything sits inside the intima, inside the internal elastic lamina: endothelium on top → fibrous cap (SMCs, macrophages, foam cells, collagen) → necrotic core (cholesterol crystals seen as clefts on histology, cell debris, foam cells, calcium). Components in one line: cells, matrix, lipids, calcium.

Where plaques form

Plaques are patchy, not circumferential, and favour branch points with turbulent flow. Commonest sites, in order the lecture gave them:

1 · Abdominal aorta & iliacs2 · Coronary arteries3 · Popliteal arteries4 · Internal carotids5 · Circle of Willis

Thin cap vs thick cap

💥

Thin-cap plaque (vulnerable)

Prone to rupture and hemorrhage → sudden thrombosis of the vessel → MI, sudden cardiac death.

🧱

Thick-cap / fibrocalcific plaque (stable)

More collagen; chronic injury, surface erosion, calcification → slowly progressive critical stenosis → angina.

Outcomes of a plaque

  • Thrombosis on the surface → occlusion
  • Hemorrhage into the plaque → sudden instability or occlusion
  • Rupture + thrombosis → embolism downstream
  • Ongoing inflammatory remodelling → aneurysm → rupture or dissection
  • Net result: MI, stroke and peripheral vascular disease are all consequences of atherosclerosis
🩺 "Atherosclerotic cardiovascular death in one slide"

Left main coronary: lipid, foam cells, cholesterol clefts under a thin cap → hemorrhage into the plaque leaks into the lumen → thrombus occludes the artery → fatal MI. Forty years of slow injury, then a sudden event.

Q: Which type of atherosclerotic plaque is most likely to cause sudden cardiac death, and by what mechanism?

Answer

A thin-cap (vulnerable) plaque: it ruptures or bleeds, exposing thrombogenic core → acute occlusive thrombus. Thick-cap fibrocalcific plaques cause gradual critical stenosis (stable angina) instead.

Q: In which layer of the artery does an atheroma — including its necrotic core — sit?

Answer

The intima (inside the internal elastic lamina). The media may secondarily thin under it.

Topic 4

Physiology you need → primary vs secondary → damage

Blood Pressure & Hypertension

AbbreviationsBP = blood pressure · CO = cardiac output · SVR = systemic vascular resistance · SV = stroke volume · HR = heart rate · RAAS = renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system · AI / AII = angiotensin I / II · ACE = angiotensin-converting enzyme · Na⁺ = sodium · ICP = intracranial pressure

The equation

BP = CO × SVRlikeVoltage = Current × ResistanceandCO = SV × HR
  • Resistance is set at the arterioles (smooth muscle tone), by nerves and hormones.
  • Cardiac output depends on heart rate and stroke volume, which depends heavily on blood volume.
  • Blood volume is set mainly by the kidney's sodium handling — water follows sodium.

RAAS in five steps

  1. Low pressure at the afferent arteriole→ kidney releases renin.
  2. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen → angiotensin I
  3. Endothelial cells (ACE) convert AI → AII
  4. Angiotensin IIraises SMC tone (vasoconstriction) and makes the adrenal secrete aldosterone.
  5. Aldosterone → renal Na⁺ reabsorption→ water follows → blood volume and BP rise.

Primary (essential) vs secondary

🧬

Primary = essential 90–95%

Many small genetic variants in BP control (e.g. sodium sensitivity, angiotensin-receptor promoter variants), all moderated by lifestyle — mostly sodium intake — and by medication. "Primary" and "essential" mean the same thing.

🔎

Secondary = rare, don't-miss

Think vascular, renal, endocrine, intracranial.

CategoryCauses
RenalGlomerulonephritis, chronic kidney disease, polycystic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, renal vasculitis, renin-producing tumours
EndocrineCushing syndrome, primary aldosteronism, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, licorice; exogenous glucocorticoids, estrogen/oral contraceptives, sympathomimetics, tyramine + MAO inhibitors; pheochromocytoma; acromegaly; hyperthyroidism; pre-eclampsia (probably the commonest secondary cause)
CardiovascularCoarctation of the aorta (kidneys under-perfused → volume up; high pressure above the narrowing), polyarteritis nodosa, ↑ intravascular volume, ↑ cardiac output, rigid aorta
NeurologicPsychogenic, raised intracranial pressure (tumour, encephalitis…), sleep apnea, acute stress/surgery
🩺 Clinical pearl — Cushing reflex

High systolic, normal diastolic + bradycardia = think intracranial cause (raised ICP). Named for Harvey Cushing, head of neurosurgery at Johns Hopkins at 32. Board add-on Full Cushing triad = hypertension (wide pulse pressure), bradycardia, irregular respirations.

What chronic hypertension does to vessels

  1. Chronic endothelial injurybarrier fails → plasma proteins pile up under the endothelium.
  2. SMC proliferationthe vessel "fights back" and thickens.
  3. Arterioles can no longer relax→ loss of end-organ BP control, higher capillary pressure, end-organ damage and, paradoxically, end-organ hypoxia.
⚠️ The treatment trap

Because the arterioles are fixed-narrow, dropping BP quickly to "normal" can under-perfuse the brain — common when elderly patients start antihypertensives for the first time.

🚨 Malignant hypertension

A medical emergency. Classic patient in lecture: African American men in their 30s–40s. Lesion: hyperplastic onion-skin arteriolosclerosis ± fibrinoid necrosis (kidney). Now very treatable with modern drugs; pre-eclampsia is the bigger clinical challenge today.

Pulmonary hypertension is a separate set of pathologies covered elsewhere.

Q: A patient with a posterior fossa tumour develops BP 190/80 and HR 48. Why?

Answer

Cushing reflex from raised intracranial pressure: the body raises systolic pressure to perfuse the brain, and baroreceptors respond with bradycardia.

Q: A 28-year-old woman has resistant hypertension and an abdominal bruit. Angiography shows a beaded renal artery. Mechanism of her hypertension?

Answer

Renovascular (secondary) hypertension from fibromuscular dysplasia: low afferent pressure → renin ↑ → angiotensin II ↑ → aldosterone ↑ → vasoconstriction and Na⁺/water retention.

Aneurysms & Aortic Dissection

AbbreviationsAAA = abdominal aortic aneurysm · TAA = thoracic aortic aneurysm · EDS = Ehlers–Danlos syndrome · TGF-β = transforming growth factor beta · MMP = matrix metalloproteinase · SAH = subarachnoid hemorrhage · MVP = mitral valve prolapse

Vocabulary

TermDefinition
True aneurysm — saccularOutpouching on one side of the wall; all layers intact
True aneurysm — fusiformCircumferential dilation of a segment
False aneurysm (pseudoaneurysm)Wall breached (trauma, local atherosclerosis) → extravascular hematoma held in by adventitia/surrounding tissue; the vessel layers themselves have not dilated
DissectionBlood enters through an intimal tear and tracks within the media, splitting the wall; the flap can block the lumen fast

Why vessel walls give way

1 · WEAK MATERIAL

Poor connective tissue

Ehlers–Danlos (collagen, vascular type = collagen III), Marfan (fibrillin-1), altered TGF-β signalling.

2 · REMODELLING

Inflammation + matrix turnover

MMP (metalloproteinase) polymorphisms, chronic plaque inflammation, hypertension lifting damaged endothelium.

3 · ISCHEMIA

Vasa vasorum narrowing

Thick walls need their own blood supply; constrict it → media dies back → weak wall.

4 · INFECTION

Syphilis & mycotic

Tertiary syphilis inflames and blocks the vasa vasorum (thoracic aorta). Mycotic aneurysm = infected wall.

Aortic dissection — a high-stakes emergency

  1. Intimal tearoften traumatic (e.g. deceleration injury in a car crash) or on a diseased/hypertensive wall.
  2. Blood tracks through the mediaforward, and sometimes backwards to the aortic valve ring.
  3. Three bad endingsbreach of the adventitia → rapid fatal hemorrhage; extension into great vessels or coronary ostia → stroke or MI; or recanalization → a chronic "double-barreled aorta" (sometimes managed conservatively, esp. DeBakey III).
🩺 Presentation

Excruciating chest pain that moves to the back and downward as the dissection propagates. Medical and surgical emergency.

Q: A tall 25-year-old with long fingers and lens dislocation has sudden tearing chest pain radiating to the back and a new diastolic murmur. Diagnosis and defective protein?

Answer

Type A aortic dissection (with aortic regurgitation from root involvement) in Marfan syndrome — fibrillin-1. Surgical emergency.

Q: A 70-year-old smoker has a pulsatile abdominal mass. Where is the aneurysm, and what is the main underlying process?

Answer

Infrarenal abdominal aorta; driven by atherosclerosis (risk factors smoking, hypertension, age).

Q: Tertiary syphilis causes aneurysms of which part of the aorta, and how?

Answer

The thoracic (ascending) aorta — obliterative inflammation of the vasa vasorum starves the media, weakening the wall. Board add-on Gross: "tree-bark" intima.

Sort by vessel size first

Vasculitis I — Large & Medium Vessels

AbbreviationsGCA = giant cell arteritis · PMR = polymyalgia rheumatica · ESR = erythrocyte sedimentation rate · PAN = polyarteritis nodosa · HBV = hepatitis B virus · IVIG = intravenous immunoglobulin
🔑 Why learn a list of rare diseases?

Most vasculitides are rare, but they mimic other diseases, are devastating if untreated, and are mostly immune-driven and highly treatable if caught early (usually steroids or other anti-inflammatories first). Lecturer's advice: in the ER, just thinking of vasculitis and calling the right specialist is a win — the boards will still want the details.

Large vessel

👴

Giant cell (temporal) arteritis

  • Older adults (>50), modest male preponderance in lecture Board add-on most sources say female > male
  • Scalp arteries, esp. temporal artery
  • Headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness
  • Ophthalmic artery involvement → blindness if untreated
  • Associated with polymyalgia rheumatica
  • Diagnosed by temporal artery biopsy: granulomas, giant cells, fragmented internal elastic lamina; thrombosis possible
  • Common (for a vasculitis) and very treatable
🫥

Takayasu arteritis — "pulseless disease"

  • Younger women, esp. Asian descent
  • Aorta and its main branches (carotids, subclavians)
  • Arm/leg claudication, weak or absent pulses, hypertension, bruits
  • Angiogram: focal narrowings of arch branches; gross: massively thickened wall

Medium vessel

🧠 Memory hooks Board add-on

Kawasaki = "CRASH and burn": Conjunctivitis, Rash, Adenopathy (cervical), Strawberry tongue, Hands/feet swelling + burn = fever ≥5 days. PAN = "Pulmonary-Avoiding, No ANCA", with hepatitis B.

Q: A 72-year-old woman has new temporal headaches, pain chewing, and shoulder/hip stiffness. What must be started today, and why?

Answer

High-dose steroids for giant cell arteritis (with polymyalgia rheumatica) — before biopsy results — because ophthalmic artery involvement causes irreversible blindness.

Q: A 25-year-old Japanese woman has arm claudication, unequal arm BPs and absent radial pulse. Diagnosis and vessels involved?

Answer

Takayasu arteritis ("pulseless disease") — granulomatous inflammation of the aortic arch and its major branches.

Q: A 3-year-old has 6 days of fever, cracked red lips, conjunctival injection and peeling fingertips. What complication are you screening for?

Answer

Coronary artery aneurysm (Kawasaki disease, medium-vessel vasculitis) — get an echocardiogram.

🧠 How ANCA actually hurts

The names are just lab staining patterns: anti-MPO lights up around the nucleus (perinuclear), anti-PR3 fills the cytoplasm. But the antibodies are also pathogenic: they bind the surface of cytokine-primed neutrophils → positive feedback of neutrophil + complement activation → endothelial damage → T cells and monocytes follow; activated neutrophils throw out NETs that add inflammation and thrombosis.

Group 2 — immune-complex ("leukocytoclastic")

Leukocytoclastic = leukocyte + klasis (Greek, "breaking"): on histology you see neutrophils blown apart into tiny nuclear fragments ("nuclear dust") around small vessels.

Variable-vessel vasculitis

🌍

Behçet disease

Any size artery or vein. Recurrent oral + genital ulcers, skin lesions, uveitis; GI, CNS; aneurysms, renal failure (biggest single consequence per lecture). Rare overall but very common in the Middle East/Turkey — HLA-B51 (~500/100,000 there vs ~5 in the US).

👁️👂

Cogan syndrome

Very rare. Interstitial keratitis + inner-ear disease (hearing loss, vertigo); sometimes large/medium arteritis. Driven by antibody to a reovirus peptide via molecular mimicry. Differential: syphilis, psittacosis.

Q: A 45-year-old has chronic bloody sinusitis, cavitating lung nodules and red cell casts in the urine. Which antibody and which histology?

Answer

GPA (Wegener): c-ANCA / anti-PR3; necrotizing granulomas + small-vessel vasculitis in upper airway, lung and kidney.

Q: An asthmatic adult develops foot drop, purpura and eosinophils of 20%. Diagnosis?

Answer

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg–Strauss) — often p-ANCA (MPO), about half ANCA-negative.

Q: A 7-year-old, two weeks after a cold, has palpable purpura on the buttocks and legs, knee pain, colicky abdominal pain and hematuria. What deposits in the vessel walls?

Answer

IgA immune complexes — Henoch–Schönlein purpura (IgA vasculitis); leukocytoclastic small-vessel vasculitis.

Q: Purpura, arthralgia and neuropathy in a patient with chronic hepatitis C. Name the vasculitis and the unusual lab property of the culprit protein.

Answer

Cryoglobulinemic vasculitis — the immunoglobulins precipitate in the cold.

Topic 8

Hyperreactive arteries · veins · lymphatics

Raynaud, Veins & Obstruction Syndromes

AbbreviationsSMC = smooth muscle cell · SLE = systemic lupus erythematosus · PE = pulmonary embolism · DVT = deep vein thrombosis · SVC = superior vena cava

Raynaud phenomenon — vessel hyperreactivity

Exaggerated vasoconstriction of arteries and arterioles in response to cold or emotion; fingers blanch (then blue, then red Board add-on). Attacks can last hours; patients learn to avoid cold, wear gloves, massage the hand.

🧤

Primary Raynaud

3–5% of people (lecture also said ~1 in 20); young women; intrinsic hyperreactivity of medial SMCs; symmetric, stays stable → reassure.

🚩

Secondary Raynaud

Driven by SLE, scleroderma, Buerger disease, even atherosclerosis. Asymmetric and progressively worse. About 10% of diagnosed Raynaud turns out to have an autoimmune cause.

⚠️ Saddle embolus

A long leg-vein thrombus travels through the right heart and lodges across the pulmonary artery bifurcation, blocking both sides at once → rapid death.

Q: A 45-year-old woman's Raynaud attacks have become asymmetric and worse over a year, and her fingertips have small ulcers and tight skin. What does this pattern suggest?

Answer

Secondary Raynaud — look for an underlying autoimmune disease such as scleroderma (or SLE). Primary Raynaud is symmetric and non-progressive.

Q: Which two factors combine to produce thrombophlebitis, per the lecture?

Answer

Reduced flow (stasis) plus a hypercoagulable state (injury, sepsis, inherited thrombophilia).

A small corner of vascular pathology

Tumours of Blood Vessels

AbbreviationsHHV-8 = human herpesvirus 8 · HIV = human immunodeficiency virus · CD31 = endothelial marker
🍓

Benign — hemangiomas

  • Juvenile "strawberry" (capillary) hemangioma: present at birth, surprising-looking, regresses in the first decade, often leaving no trace
  • Cavernous hemangioma
  • Pyogenic granuloma: reactive granulation tissue that doesn't know when to stop
  • Bacillary angiomatosis: reactive, infection-induced (Bartonella in immunocompromised Board add-on)
⚠️

Malignant

  • Angiosarcoma: vanishingly rare; the classic occupational cause is vinyl chloride (liver angiosarcoma), now mostly historical. Locally aggressive, metastasizes.
  • Kaposi sarcoma: more indolent; HHV-8, typically in HIV; also a sporadic form in older Mediterranean men.

Q: A former PVC-plant worker develops a hemorrhagic liver mass with atypical, CD31-positive vascular channels. Tumour and exposure?

Answer

Hepatic angiosarcoma from vinyl chloride exposure.

Q: A man with advanced HIV has violaceous plaques on his legs and palate; biopsy shows spindle cells around slit-like vessels. Causative agent?

Answer

HHV-8 → Kaposi sarcoma.

Q: Parents worry about a raised red lesion that appeared on their newborn's forehead and grew for months. What is the expected course?

Answer

Juvenile (strawberry) capillary hemangioma — it regresses spontaneously during the first decade, usually without trace.

WALL · ARTERIOSCLEROSIS · ATHEROMA · BP

Endothelium

  • Intact = no clot; denuded → collagen exposed → platelets
  • Laminar flow = quiet; turbulent flow (branch points) = activated → plaques
  • Any injury → SMCs into intima → intimal thickening → stenosis
  • Anti-clot: prostacyclin, thrombomodulin, heparin-like, tPA · Pro-clot: vWF, tissue factor, PAI · Tone: endothelin vs NO

Arteriosclerosis subtypes

  • Hyaline: pink glassy arterioles — benign HTN, diabetes
  • Hyperplastic "onion-skin" ± fibrinoid necrosis — malignant HTN, pre-eclampsia
  • Mönckeberg: medial calcium, lumen spared, asymptomatic, X-ray visible
  • Fibromuscular intimal hyperplasia: after stents/balloons, graft failure (≠ FMD "string of beads")
  • Atherosclerosis = subset caused by atheroma; ~40% of global deaths

Atherosclerosis

  • Risk: hyperlipidemia (main), smoking, HTN, diabetes (glycation), age/sex/family
  • Injury → LDL in → monocytes → foam cells → platelets → SMC migration + ECM → necrotic core + cholesterol clefts + Ca²⁺ — all in the intima
  • Sites: abdominal aorta/iliacs > coronary > popliteal > ICA > circle of Willis; patchy, branch points
  • Thin cap → rupture/hemorrhage → thrombus (MI, SCD); thick cap → erosion, critical stenosis (angina)
  • Outcomes: thrombosis, plaque hemorrhage, embolism, aneurysm

Blood pressure & HTN

  • BP = CO × SVR; SVR at arterioles; volume via renal Na⁺
  • RAAS: ↓ afferent pressure → renin → AI → (ACE on endothelium) AII → tone + aldosterone → Na⁺ retention
  • Primary 90–95% (polygenic + salt); secondary: renal, endocrine, vascular, intracranial — pre-eclampsia commonest
  • ↑ systolic + normal diastolic + bradycardia = ↑ ICP (Cushing)
  • Chronic HTN → fixed-narrow arterioles → end-organ hypoxia; rapid BP drop can under-perfuse the brain
ANEURYSM · DISSECTION · LARGE/MEDIUM VASCULITIS

Aneurysm types & causes

  • True: saccular (one side), fusiform (circumferential) · False = contained extravascular hematoma · Dissection = blood in the media via intimal tear
  • Causes: weak CT (Marfan fibrillin-1, EDS IV collagen III, TGF-β), inflammation/MMPs, vasa vasorum ischemia, syphilis, mycotic
  • AAA most common — infrarenal, atherosclerosis, smokers · TAA — Marfan · berry — circle of Willis, congenital, SAH · ventricular — late post-MI

Aortic dissection

  • Tearing chest pain moving to the back and down
  • Kills by adventitial rupture, great-vessel or coronary occlusion, retrograde to valve (AR)
  • DeBakey I/II = Stanford A → surgery; III = B → often conservative; chronic → double-barreled aorta
  • Marfan: dissection risk ~30%, MVP >90% · vEDS: arterial rupture high risk

Large-vessel vasculitis

  • GCA: >50, temporal artery, headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, blindness, PMR; biopsy = granulomas, giant cells, broken IEL → steroids now
  • Takayasu: young Asian women, arch + branches, pulseless, arm claudication, bruits, HTN

Medium-vessel vasculitis

  • PAN: kidney, heart, GI, nerves (spares lung), HTN, abdominal pain, hepatitis B, ANCA-neg, aneurysms
  • Kawasaki: <5 y, fever + CRASH, coronary aneurysm
  • Buerger: heavy smoker, limb claudication, Raynaud, gangrene, no organs; cure = quit smoking
SMALL VASCULITIS · RAYNAUD · VEINS · TUMOURS

ANCA-associated

  • GPA (Wegener): granulomas; sinus + lung + kidney; c-ANCA / PR3
  • MPA: no granulomas; kidney + lung hemorrhage; p-ANCA / MPO; drugs
  • EGPA (Churg–Strauss): asthma + eosinophilia + neuropathy; p-ANCA or negative
  • ANCA bind primed neutrophils → complement + NETs → endothelial damage

Immune-complex (leukocytoclastic = nuclear dust)

  • HSP: child after URI, IgA; purpura + arthritis + abdominal pain + GN · adult IgA vasculitis → worse kidneys
  • Cryoglobulinemic: hepatitis C, Sjögren; cold-precipitating Ig
  • Anti-GBM (Goodpasture): GN + pulmonary hemorrhage
  • Behçet: orogenital ulcers + uveitis, HLA-B51, Turkey/Middle East · Cogan: keratitis + hearing loss

Raynaud

  • Primary: 3–5%, young women, medial SMC hyperreactivity, symmetric, stable
  • Secondary: SLE, scleroderma, Buerger; asymmetric + progressive; ~10% autoimmune

Veins

  • Varices: leg, esophageal, hemorrhoidal
  • Thrombophlebitis = stasis + hypercoagulability → DVT → PE / saddle embolus
  • Obstruction: SVC syndrome, lymphedema

Tumours

  • Strawberry hemangioma regresses · pyogenic granuloma = runaway granulation tissue · bacillary angiomatosis (infection)
  • Angiosarcoma: vinyl chloride (liver)
  • Kaposi: HHV-8, HIV; also older Mediterranean men
