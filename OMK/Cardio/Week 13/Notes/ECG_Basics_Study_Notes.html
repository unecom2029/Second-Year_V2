Orientation

"Look at it the same way every single time"

A System for Reading Any ECG

Dr. Qazi reads an ECG in half a second — after hundreds of thousands of them. Her point: you get there only by using the same step-by-step system every time so nothing is missed. The first ECG you read alone will take a long time. That's expected. Even veteran ER doctors call cardiology to ask, "can you look at this ECG?"

How to read the colours Red bold = lecturer emphasis / "this will be on the boards" Blue bold = key criterion Yellow = number to memorise Board add-on = crib sheets / Dubin / board sources, not said in lecture
AbbreviationsECG / EKG = electrocardiogram (same thing) · SA / AV = sinoatrial / atrioventricular · LBBB / RBBB = left / right bundle branch block · LAFB / LPFB = left anterior / posterior fascicular block · LVH / RVH = left / right ventricular hypertrophy · QTc = corrected QT · STEMI / NSTEMI = ST-elevation / non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction

The order Dr. Qazi uses

  1. RateCount QRS on the rhythm strip × 6. Tachy ≥100, brady <60.
  2. RhythmRegular? If yes — is it sinus (the 5 criteria)? If irregular — which of the 5 irregular rhythms?
  3. AxisQRS in lead I and aVF (lead II as back-up). Left or right? Then ask why.
  4. IntervalsPR (AV block?) → QRS (bundle branch block?) → QT/QTc (torsades risk?).
  5. Chamber enlargementP waves in II and V1 (atria); voltage + axis (LVH); R-wave progression + axis (RVH).
  6. Ischemia / injury / infarctionST elevation (transmural), ST depression (subendocardial), Q waves (dead, old) — in which territory?
  7. Put it into one sentencee.g. "Sinus bradycardia, rate 42, normal axis, 2:1 AV block."

Topic 1

Paper · waves · leads — the vocabulary

Before You Read: Paper, Waves & Leads

Abbreviationsmm = millimetre · ms = millisecond · mV = millivolt · aVR / aVL / aVF = augmented limb leads (right arm / left arm / foot) · V1–V6 = precordial (chest) leads

The ECG draws the heart's electricity. Each wave is one step of conduction: SA node → atria (P wave) → pause in the AV node (PR segment) → His → bundle branches → Purkinje → ventricles (QRS) → ventricular repolarisation (T wave). The SA node sets the pace simply because it is the fastest pacemaker.

The paper (standard speed 25 mm/s)

PR interval

Start of P → start of QRS

Normal ≤ 200 ms = 1 big box (crib sheet: 120–200 ms). Long = AV-node delay.

QRS duration

Start → end of QRS

Normal < 120 ms = 3 small boxes. Wide = bundle branch block (or ventricular origin).

QT / QTc

Start of QRS → end of T

QTc normal < 450 ms men / 460 ms women. Clinical danger > 500 ms.

🔑 Naming the QRS (you'll need it for every criterion)

Q = first negative deflection · R = first positive deflection · S = the negative deflection after the R. A second R is R′ ("R-prime"). Big letters = big waves, small letters = small waves. You won't always see a Q or an S.

Leads

12 views from 10 electrodes: 6 limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF — the frontal plane) and 6 chest leads (V1–V6 — the horizontal plane). The printout shows ~2.5 s per lead, and the bottom strip is the full 10-second rhythm strip, almost always lead II.

Q: How long is one small box, one big box, and a standard 12-lead tracing?

Answer

Small box 0.04 s (40 ms); big box 0.2 s (200 ms); standard 12-lead 10 seconds (5 big boxes = 1 s).

Topic 2

Step 1

Rate

Abbreviationsbpm = beats per minute · QRS = ventricular complex · EMS = emergency medical services
🔑 Dr. Qazi's method — works for any rhythm, regular or not

Count every QRS on the rhythm strip × 6 (a standard 12-lead is 10 s). A 6-s monitor strip → × 10. That's it.

  • Tachycardia ≥ 100 · normal 60–99 · bradycardia < 60. Sinus rhythm at 110 must be called sinus tachycardia.
  • The machine's rate is right ~90% of the time; artifact and movement fool it.
  • Board add-on For regular rhythms, 300 – 150 – 100 – 75 – 60 – 50 by big boxes between two R waves (or 1500 ÷ small boxes). Only works when the rhythm is regular.

Topic 3
⭐ Know the 5 irregular rhythms

Step 2 — regular or irregular?

Rhythm

AbbreviationsNSR = normal sinus rhythm · AF / Afib = atrial fibrillation · MAT = multifocal atrial tachycardia · WAP = wandering atrial pacemaker · RA / LA = right / left atrium

Regular → is it sinus? All five must be true

  1. R–R regularmarch the QRSs out with calipers.
  2. P–P regular(matters later for AV block).
  3. PR constantmeasure it beat to beat.
  4. A P for every QRS(proven by 1–3).
  5. P upright in lead II— the impulse started in the SA node. (In V1 the normal P is biphasic, like a sine wave.)

Irregular → five things

#RhythmWhat you see
1Atrial fibrillationNo P waves, irregularly irregular R–R (no pattern), undulating baseline. The arrhythmia you'll see most (~90% of rhythm problems in adult cardiology).
2Sinus arrhythmiaSinus P waves, rate varies with respiration; common in the young (normal).
32nd-degree AV blockDropped beats (a P with no QRS) make it look irregular — may have a pattern.
4Wandering atrial pacemakerImpulses from several atrial foci: ≥3 P-wave morphologies, rate <100.
5Multifocal atrial tachycardiaSame, but rate ≥100. Board add-on classic in COPD.

Atrial flutter — "highly tested"

⚠️ Sawtooth

Flutter waves in a "sawtooth" pattern (best in II, III, aVF), often with a fixed ratio of flutter waves to QRS (e.g. 4:1) or variable block (irregular). Origin: typical flutter circles in the right atrium around the cavotricuspid isthmus; atrial fibrillation starts around the pulmonary veins in the left atrium.

Q: An irregular narrow-complex rhythm at 118 bpm has three different P-wave shapes. Name it, and what would it be called at 82 bpm?

Answer

Multifocal atrial tachycardia; at <100 it's a wandering atrial pacemaker.

Q: What are the five criteria for calling a rhythm "sinus"?

Answer

Regular R–R, regular P–P, constant PR, a P for every QRS, and an upright P in lead II.

Step 3 — two seconds, then ask "why?"

Axis

AbbreviationsLAD = left axis deviation (here, not the artery) · RAD = right axis deviation · LAFB / LPFB = left anterior / posterior fascicular block · LVH / RVH = left / right ventricular hypertrophy

Dr. Qazi's take: axis matters mainly as a criterion for other diagnoses. Skip the quadrants — just look at the QRS in lead I and aVF.

AxisLead IaVFMemory hook
Normal++"Two thumbs up"
Left+−Peaks point away from each other
Right−+Peaks point toward each other

aVF isoelectric? Use lead II instead of aVF — same pattern (normal +, left −, right +). That gets you ~95%. Board add-on Normal ≈ −30° to +90°.

⬅️

Left axis — 4 causes

  1. LVH
  2. LBBB
  3. Left anterior fascicular block
  4. Old inferior MI (Q waves in II, III, aVF make aVF negative)
➡️

Right axis — 3 causes

  1. RVH
  2. Left posterior fascicular block
  3. Old lateral MI (Q waves in I, aVL)

Lung disease also shifts it right — but doesn't change the read.

⚠️ The classic wrong answer

RBBB has a normal axis. RBBB + right axis = RBBB + left posterior fascicular block.

Q: QRS is positive in lead I and negative in aVF, the QRS is narrow, there's no LVH and no Q waves in II, III, aVF. Diagnosis?

Answer

Left anterior fascicular block — a left axis with no other cause.

Topic 5
⭐ P–P, R–R, PR every time

Step 4a — PR interval

PR Interval & AV Block

AbbreviationsAVB = atrioventricular block · P–P / R–R = interval between consecutive P waves / R waves · PR = start of P to start of QRS · CHB = complete heart block

Normal PR ≤ 200 ms (one big box). Long PR = slowed conduction through the AV node → think AV block. Dr. Qazi's rule: for every AV block, measure the same three things.

BlockP–PR–RPRKey
1st degreeRegularRegularLong (>200 ms) but constantEvery P conducts — it's just slow
2nd degree, Mobitz I (Wenckebach)RegularIrregularProgressively lengthens → dropped QRSPR after the drop is shorter than the PR before it (easier to see than the gradual lengthening). Benign.
2nd degree, Mobitz IIRegularIrregularConstantA P suddenly doesn't conduct (often a pattern, e.g. every 3rd). High-grade → pacemaker.
3rd degree (complete)RegularRegularAll over the placeAV dissociation: Ps and QRSs each march out on their own; atrial rate usually faster than ventricular

In every AV block the P–P interval is constant — if the Ps don't march out, think of something else.

🧩 2:1 AV block — the trick ECG

Every other P is blocked, so there are never two conducted P waves in a row → you cannot tell Mobitz I from Mobitz II. Call it "2:1 AV block" and let the history decide: an asymptomatic 25-year-old → probably Mobitz I (benign); an 80-year-old with syncope or exhaustion → treat as Mobitz II (pacemaker); a young patient with syncope → think congenital AV block, ask about family history. Look for the hidden P inside the T wave — it looks identical to the other P waves.

🎯 AV-block drill

Read the description, name the block. (Shuffles each time you open the page.)

Q: P–P regular, R–R regular, but the PR changes from beat to beat; atrial rate 80, ventricular rate 36. Diagnosis?

Answer

Third-degree (complete) AV block — AV dissociation.

Q: Why does telling Mobitz I from Mobitz II matter?

Answer

Mobitz I is benign; Mobitz II is high-grade and needs a pacemaker.

Topic 6

Step 4b — QRS duration

Wide QRS: Bundle Branch & Fascicular Blocks

AbbreviationsBBB = bundle branch block · LBBB / RBBB = left / right BBB · LAFB / LPFB = left anterior / posterior fascicular block · WPW = Wolff–Parkinson–White

Normal QRS < 120 ms (3 small boxes). Wide QRS → first think bundle branch block: conduction delayed below the His bundle. Anatomy to picture: His → right bundle + left bundle; the left bundle splits into anterior and posterior fascicles.

⬅️

LBBB

  • QRS ≥ 120 ms
  • Left axis (required, per lecture)
  • V1–V3 negative — rS / mostly negative, 100% of the time
  • I, aVL, V5–V6 positive — broad R, sometimes notched at the peak
➡️

RBBB

  • QRS ≥ 120 ms
  • Normal axis
  • rSR′ ("rabbit ears") in V1–V2 — or at least mostly positive
  • Slurred S waves in I, aVL, V5–V6
  • Wide QRS + rSR′ in V1–V2 → call it
🧠 "WiLLiaM MaRRoW" Board add-on

LBBB = W in V1, M in V6 (WiLLiaM). RBBB = M in V1, W in V6 (MaRRoW).

Fascicular blocks — the QRS is normal

Left anterior fascicular block

Common on its own

Left axis with no other explanation — no LVH, no LBBB, normal QRS width, no inferior Q waves.

Left posterior fascicular block

Rare alone

Right axis with no other explanation — no RVH, no old lateral MI. Usually seen as RBBB + right axis = RBBB + LPFB.

Board add-on A short PR with a slurred QRS upstroke (delta wave) is pre-excitation (WPW) — covered with arrhythmias in Dr. Cecilberg's lecture.

Q: QRS 140 ms, rSR′ in V1–V2, slurred S in I and V6, and right axis deviation. Full read of the conduction?

Answer

RBBB + left posterior fascicular block (RBBB alone has a normal axis).

Q: QRS 150 ms, left axis, deep negative QRS in V1–V3 and broad notched R in I, aVL, V6. Diagnosis?

Answer

Left bundle branch block.

Topic 7
⭐ Long QT → torsades (boards)

Step 4c — the clinically dangerous interval

QT / QTc

AbbreviationsQTc = QT corrected for heart rate · RR = interval between R waves (in seconds) · VT = ventricular tachycardia · Mg / K = magnesium / potassium · HIV = human immunodeficiency virus
Bazett: QTc = QT ÷ √RR·Normal < 450 ms (men) / 460 ms (women)·Worry > 500 ms
  • Eyeball test: mark halfway between two R waves. If the T wave ends before the halfway point, the QT is ~90% likely normal; at or beyond it → calculate.
  • Why it matters: a long QT sets up polymorphic VT = torsades de pointes ("twisting" ribbon of changing amplitude) — tested on the boards.
  • Over 500 ms the torsades risk rises sharply (a wide QRS from a BBB lengthens the QT itself — account for it).

What lengthens it

CauseLecture points
Class III antiarrhythmicsDofetilide — the only drug she's actually seen cause torsades; sotalol with electrolyte problems; amiodarone prolongs QT but she has never seen torsades from it
ElectrolytesLow Mg, low K (high K prolongs everything — wide QRS, long PR)
Other drugsFluoroquinolones and other antibiotics, every antidepressant, antipsychotics, HIV drugs — an ever-growing list. Check the med list + electrolytes before prescribing (her story: a patient died of polymorphic VT after a new fluoroquinolone)
Congenital long QTYoung athlete who collapses — the 12-lead is the key first test (though the commonest cause of on-field sudden death is hypertrophic cardiomyopathy)

Q: A patient on dofetilide for AF is started on ondansetron; K⁺ is 3.1 and Mg²⁺ low. What rhythm are you worried about and why?

Answer

Torsades de pointes (polymorphic VT) — stacked QT-prolonging drugs + hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia.

Topic 8

Step 5 — P waves, voltage, R-wave progression

Chamber Enlargement & Hypertrophy

AbbreviationsLAE / RAE = left / right atrial enlargement · LVH / RVH = left / right ventricular hypertrophy · TWI = T-wave inversion · mm = millimetre (1 small box tall)

Atria — look at the P in II and V1

↔️

Left atrial enlargement

Lead II: P still upright but wider (Board add-on >120 ms, notched "P mitrale"). V1: mostly negative — big terminal negative part. Common with ageing (stiff LV), athletes.

↕️

Right atrial enlargement

Lead II: tall, peaked P (Board add-on >2.5 mm, "P pulmonale"). V1: mostly positive — big initial part. In children, think congenital heart disease.

LVH — why it matters

New heart failure + LVH on the ECG starts the work-up; LVH also brings TWI / ST depression ("strain") that can be mistaken for ischemia.

CriterionRuleNote
Left axisRequiredLeftward forces
R in aVL> 11 mmMost specific
S (V1 or V2) + R (V5 or V6)≥ 35 mmMost sensitive (Sokolow–Lyon)

Call LVH with the left axis + either voltage criterion (or both).

Normal R-wave progression → RVH

Normal: V1 = small r, deep S → R grows → transition (R = S) at V3–V4 → V5–V6 tall R, small s. RVH = right axis + the progression reverses (tall R in V1, getting smaller toward V6). Rare in adults; think congenital disease.

Board add-on A tall R in V1 also appears in RBBB, posterior MI and HCM; poor R-wave progression in prior anteroseptal MI, LVH, LBBB, COPD.

Q: Left axis; S in V2 = 22 mm, R in V5 = 18 mm; R in aVL = 13 mm. Diagnosis and which criterion is most specific?

Answer

LVH — both voltage criteria met (22 + 18 = 40 ≥ 35); R in aVL >11 mm is the most specific.

Step 6 — which artery, how acute?

Ischemia, Injury & Infarction

AbbreviationsRCA = right coronary artery · PDA = posterior descending artery · LMT = left main trunk · LCx = left circumflex · LAD = left anterior descending (the artery, here) · STE / STD = ST elevation / depression · MI = myocardial infarction · TWI = T-wave inversion

Coronary anatomy in one table

ArteryCourseSuppliesLeads
RCA → PDAAnterior AV groove → PDA in the inferior interventricular groove (right-dominant ~80%)Inferior wall + inferior ⅓ of septumII, III, aVF
LCx (from left main)Posterior AV groove, obtuse marginals (gives the PDA in ~20% — left-dominant)Inferolateral ("posterior") wallNo direct leads → reciprocal changes in V1–V3
LAD (from left main) — "widowmaker"Anterior interventricular groove; septal perforators + diagonals; ~40%+ of LVAnterior wall, anteroseptum (top ⅔), anterolateral wallV1–V3 anteroseptal · V4–V6 anterior · I, aVL anterolateral

The three findings — what each means

ST ELEVATION

Transmural injury = STEMI

Usually plaque rupture → 100% occlusion. ≥1 mm in ≥2 contiguous leads (an anatomic pattern). → open the artery now (cath lab, or thrombolytics then transfer). "Time is muscle."

ST DEPRESSION

Subendocardial injury = NSTEMI

Rupture → 70–90% narrowing, some flow continues. ≥1 mm below baseline; often diffuse, not one territory. Stabilise first — no emergent revascularisation.

Q WAVES

Dead myocardium = old MI

≥1 small box wide and deep, in an anatomic pattern. The infarct is completed (usually by 12–24 h, varies). Normal only in lead III (lecture).

T INVERSION

Non-specific

Ischemia, electrolytes, metabolic — "I don't know what TWI means" without context.

🧠 Reciprocal changes confirm a STEMI

ST elevation in one territory with ST depression in the opposite leads is an electrical mirror image and confirms the STEMI — e.g. inferior STE (II, III, aVF) with STD in aVL/anterior leads.

Board add-on (crib sheet) V2–V3 need more: ≥2 mm men ≥40 y, ≥2.5 mm men <40 y, ≥1.5 mm women. Evolution: hyperacute T waves → STE → Q waves + TWI as ST returns to baseline. Posterior MI: ST depression + tall R + upright T in V1–V3. Known LBBB or pacing makes ischemia hard to read.

📍 STEMI localizer

Tap the leads that show ST elevation. The tool names the territory and artery.

No leads selected.

Q: ST elevation in II, III, aVF with ST depression in I and aVL, and a heart rate of 38 with dissociated P waves. Which artery, and why the slow rate?

Answer

RCA (inferior STEMI with reciprocal changes); the RCA supplies the AV node in most people → complete heart block — exactly class ECG 3.

Q: Q waves in II, III, aVF but a flat ST segment. What does this mean?

Answer

An old (completed) inferior MI — dead myocardium, not acute injury (and a cause of left axis deviation).

🎯 ECG Basics — High-Yield One-Pager

Amina Qazi, DO FACC · OMK 2 Cardio · Rate → rhythm → axis → intervals → chambers → ischemia, every time

RATE · RHYTHM · AXIS

Paper

  • Small box 1 mm = 0.04 s, 0.1 mV · big box = 0.2 s · 12-lead = 10 s; bottom strip = lead II
  • Q = first −, R = first +, S = − after R

Rate

  • QRS on rhythm strip × 6 (6-s strip × 10)
  • Tachy ≥100 · brady <60 · (300-150-100-75-60-50 if regular)

Sinus = all 5

  • Regular R–R · regular P–P · constant PR · P for every QRS · P upright in II (biphasic in V1)

5 irregular rhythms

  • AF: no P, irregularly irregular · sinus arrhythmia (breathing, young) · 2° AVB · WAP ≥3 P shapes <100 · MAT ≥3 P shapes ≥100
  • Flutter: sawtooth (II, III, aVF), RA cavotricuspid isthmus; AF = pulmonary veins, LA

Axis (I, aVF; II if aVF ≈0)

  • ++ normal · +− left · −+ right
  • Left: LVH, LBBB, LAFB, old inferior MI
  • Right: RVH, LPFB, old lateral MI
  • RBBB = normal axis; RBBB + RAD = + LPFB
INTERVALS

PR ≤ 200 ms (1 big box)

  • 1°: long, constant PR
  • Mobitz I: PR lengthens → drop; PR after drop shorter; benign
  • Mobitz II: constant PR, sudden drop; pacemaker
  • 2:1: can't type it — history decides
  • 3°: P–P and R–R regular, PR random (AV dissociation)
  • All AV blocks: P–P constant

QRS < 120 ms (3 small boxes)

  • LBBB: wide, left axis, V1–V3 negative, I/aVL/V5–6 broad (notched) R
  • RBBB: wide, normal axis, rSR′ V1–V2, slurred S I/aVL/V5–6
  • LAFB = left axis, no other cause · LPFB = right axis, no other cause (QRS normal)
  • WiLLiaM MaRRoW · delta wave + short PR = WPW

QT

  • QTc = QT/√RR; normal <450 M / 460 F; >500 = danger
  • Eyeball: T ends before half the R–R → likely normal
  • Long QT → torsades. Causes: dofetilide (sotalol), ↓Mg, ↓K, fluoroquinolones, antidepressants, antipsychotics, HIV drugs, congenital
  • ↑K: peaked T → everything widens · ↓K: flat T, U wave
CHAMBERS · ISCHEMIA

Atria (II + V1)

  • LAE: wide P in II, mostly negative in V1
  • RAE: tall peaked P in II, mostly positive in V1

Ventricles

  • LVH = left axis + (R aVL >11 mm most specific, or S V1/2 + R V5/6 ≥35 most sensitive)
  • Normal R progression: transition V3–V4
  • RVH = right axis + reversed R progression (tall R V1)

Territories

  • II, III, aVF = inferior → RCA (80%) / LCx
  • V1–V3 anteroseptal · V4–V6 anterior · I, aVL anterolateral → LAD
  • All → proximal LAD ("widowmaker")
  • Posterior (LCx/RCA): STD + tall R in V1–V3

What each finding means

  • STE ≥1 mm, ≥2 contiguous leads = transmural, 100% occlusion → open artery now
  • STD = subendocardial (NSTEMI), often diffuse → stabilise
  • Q ≥1 box = dead, old MI (normal in III)
  • TWI non-specific · reciprocal STD confirms STEMI
  • Evolution: hyperacute T → STE → Q + TWI
