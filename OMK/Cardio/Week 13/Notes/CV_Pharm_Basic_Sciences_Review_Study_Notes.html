Topic 1
⭐ Fluency needed

Where each receptor is and what it does

Autonomic Nervous System Refresher

AbbreviationsNE = norepinephrine · Epi = epinephrine · ACh = acetylcholine · NN = neuronal nicotinic receptor · M2/M3 = muscarinic subtypes · α1/α2/β1/β2 = adrenergic receptor subtypes · JG = juxtaglomerular · GI = gastrointestinal

Sympathetic = fight or flight

  • Mediators: norepinephrine (neurocrine, from nerve endings) and epinephrine (endocrine, from the adrenal medulla).
  • β1 on the heart → ↑ heart rate, ↑ contractile force, ↑ conduction velocity → ↑ cardiac output.
  • β1 is also on the kidney's juxtaglomerular cells → ↑ renin → RAAS → more volume.
  • α1 on peripheral vascular smooth muscle → vasoconstriction → ↑ systemic vascular resistance. You don't need fine motor skills or skin perfusion to fight or run.
  • β2 in skeletal-muscle vascular beds → vasodilation (blood to the muscles); β2 in the lungs → bronchodilation (gas exchange). α1 also dilates the pupil.
  • α2 presynaptically and in the CNS → ↓ transmitter release / ↓ sympathetic outflow (the brake — see Topic 6).

🧠 The nicotinic receptor is shared — why nicotine looks "both"

Preganglionic neurons of both branches release ACh onto neuronal nicotinic (NN) receptors in the ganglia. So nicotine drives both: parasympathetic (the GI tract "wakes up") and sympathetic (arousal, tachycardia). That's also why nicotine toxicity is unpredictable — both branches fire. (Sweat glands use ACh too, but on muscarinic receptors — a common mix-up raised in class.)

⚠️ "Muscarinic = cholinergic"

Muscarinic receptors are cholinergic receptors — easy to forget under pressure. If a finding fits a muscarinic effect, it will be produced by cholinomimetics and blocked by antimuscarinics (atropine).

One more detail from the lecture: the peripheral vasculature has no parasympathetic innervation — its tone comes only from sympathetic α1 signalling, kept "a little on" like a dimmer switch so there is always room to dial it back and dilate.

Q: A patient receives a drug that selectively activates β1 receptors. Besides tachycardia, what happens to plasma renin, and why?

Answer

Renin rises — β1 receptors on juxtaglomerular cells stimulate renin release (and β-blockers lower renin for the same reason).

Q: A smoker trying to quit notices both a racing heart and increased bowel movements after a nicotine lozenge. Explain with one receptor.

Answer

Neuronal nicotinic (NN) receptors sit in both sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia, so nicotine activates both branches at once.

Topic 2

Gq · Gi · Gs — and what they do to Ca²⁺

G-Protein Signalling in Heart & Smooth Muscle

AbbreviationsGPCR = G-protein-coupled receptor · PLC = phospholipase C · PIP2 = phosphatidylinositol bisphosphate · IP3 = inositol trisphosphate · DAG = diacylglycerol · PKC / PKA / PKG = protein kinase C / A / G · AC = adenylyl cyclase · cAMP / cGMP = cyclic AMP / GMP · SR = sarcoplasmic reticulum · MLCK = myosin light-chain kinase · MLC = myosin light chain · sGC = soluble guanylyl cyclase · NO = nitric oxide · eNOS = endothelial NO synthase · ROCK = Rho-associated kinase
🧠 The rule and the exception

Odd = Gq, even = Gi for muscarinic and α receptors: M1, M3, α1 → Gq; M2, α2 → Gi. "Betas are BS" — they break the odd/even rule: every β (β1, β2, β3) → Gs.

PathwayReceptorsCascadeResult
GqM1, M3, α1PLC → PIP2 → IP3 (→ SR Ca²⁺ release) + DAG (→ PKC)Smooth muscle contraction (vasoconstriction, miosis via M3)
GiM2, α2α subunit inhibits AC → ↓cAMP; βγ subunit blocks presynaptic Ca²⁺ influxSlower heart (M2); ↓ transmitter release (α2)
Gsβ1, β2 (all β)Activates AC → ↑cAMP → PKAHeart: ↑ rate + force. Smooth muscle: relaxation (PKA inhibits MLCK)

In the heart: β1 and M2 pull on the same enzyme

β1 (Gs) and M2 (Gi) converge on adenylyl cyclase. Gs → ↑cAMP → PKA phosphorylates Ca²⁺-handling proteins (L-type channel, phospholamban) → faster, stronger beats. Gi inhibits the same enzyme, so the same proteins are not phosphorylated → slower rate, slower AV conduction. Net heart rate = the integrated cAMP signal.

In vascular smooth muscle: Ca²⁺ is the currency

Contraction = Ca²⁺ in (L-type channels, IP3-released SR stores) → Ca²⁺–calmodulin → MLCK phosphorylates myosin → cross-bridges. Tone changes are slow, summed signals — not twitches.

  • Contract: α1/Gq → IP3 → Ca²⁺ ↑ → MLCK on.
  • Relax via cAMP: β2/Gs → PKA → inhibits MLCK → dilation (skeletal-muscle beds, bronchioles).
  • Relax via cGMP: endothelial NO → soluble guanylyl cyclase → cGMP → PKG → MLC phosphatase → dilation.

Q: Which second-messenger pathway do phenylephrine (α1) and bethanechol acting on the pupillary constrictor (M3) share?

Answer

Gq → PLC → IP3 + DAG → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ → smooth muscle contraction (odd-numbered = Gq).

Q: How does activation of β2 receptors relax bronchial and skeletal-muscle vascular smooth muscle?

Answer

Gs → adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP → PKA inhibits myosin light-chain kinase → less myosin phosphorylation → relaxation.

Topic 3
⭐ Every drug moves one of these

Kidney volume control · MAP = CO × PVR

Blood Pressure: the Dials Drugs Turn

AbbreviationsMAP = mean arterial pressure · CO = cardiac output · PVR / SVR = peripheral / systemic vascular resistance · HR = heart rate · SV = stroke volume · AT-II = angiotensin II · ADH = antidiuretic hormone · RAAS = renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system · PDE3 = phosphodiesterase 3

Kidneys: the long-term volume regulator

The ANS is the fast responder; volume is the long-term answer. For cardiovascular purposes, the nephron's job is sodium (and water) handling. Angiotensin II acts on the adrenal cortex (aldosterone), proximal tubule (↑ NaCl reabsorption), efferent arterioles (constrict, maintain GFR), hypothalamus (thirst, ADH) and heart (remodelling).

💡 Striking fact from lecture

The kidney's role in BP is so large that hypertension can be transplanted: a kidney from a donor with renal-based hypertension can make the recipient hypertensive.

ACE has a second job worth remembering now: it is also kininase II, the enzyme that inactivates bradykinin — the basis of ACE-inhibitor cough and angioedema in the hypertension lecture.

MAP = CO × PVR

MAP = CO × PVR→CO = HR × SV→SV ← preload + contractility·PVR ← arteriolar diameter

Perfusion is what we are protecting; MAP is how we measure it.

Preload, afterload and heart failure

The circuit has a resistance side (arterioles) and a capacitance side (veins). Change afterload to change impedance; change preload to change how hard the heart contracts (Frank–Starling). In heart failure: lower afterload → less contractility needed to move blood forward → ↑ stroke volume. These same levers give the mortality benefits in heart failure later in the block.

🩺 Compensation in action — blood loss

Lose volume → ↓ preload → ↓ CO → BP falls. Acute: constrict peripheral vessels (α1) + ↑ heart rate (β1). Long-term: RAAS + thirst restore volume. In hypertension the body treats the high pressure as its set point and uses these same mechanisms to defend it — the theme of the next lecture.

Q: A drug dilates arterioles but not veins. Which determinant of blood pressure does it change, and what would the heart of a patient with systolic heart failure gain?

Answer

It lowers SVR / afterload. Lower impedance means the failing ventricle ejects a larger stroke volume for the same contractility.

The Heart as an Electrical Organ

AbbreviationsEKG/ECG = electrocardiogram · SA / AV = sinoatrial / atrioventricular · AP = action potential · If = "funny" current · ICa / INa / IK = calcium / sodium / potassium current

You'll be asked to read EKGs on the fly, so know where the current is generated, how it propagates, how it causes contraction, and how it maps to the trace:

Ventricles

Ventricles

Nodal vs non-nodal action potentials

⏱️

SA / AV nodal cell (pacemaker)

  • Phase 4: slow depolarisation — If (inward, mainly Na⁺)
  • Phase 0 upstroke: Ca²⁺ through voltage-gated channels
  • Phase 3: repolarisation by K⁺
  • No stable resting potential; small, slow upstroke
💪

Ventricular / atrial myocyte

  • Phase 4: stable resting potential (~−85 mV)
  • Phase 0 upstroke: Na⁺ (fast channels)
  • Phase 1: transient outward K⁺
  • Phase 2 plateau: inward Ca²⁺ balanced by outward K⁺
  • Phase 3: repolarisation by K⁺
🔑 The three questions asked in class

Myocyte upstroke = Na⁺. Nodal upstroke = Ca²⁺. Repolarisation in both = K⁺. These are exactly the currents antiarrhythmics target.

Q: A drug blocks L-type Ca²⁺ channels. Which phase of which action potential is slowed most, and what happens to AV conduction?

Answer

Phase 0 of nodal (SA/AV) cells, whose upstroke is carried by Ca²⁺ → slower SA rate and slower AV conduction (↑ PR interval). Myocyte phase 0 (Na⁺) is spared.

Topic 5

Why we care about the lipid panel

Vasculature: Lipids, Plaque & Clots

AbbreviationsLDL / HDL = low- / high-density lipoprotein · ox-LDL = oxidised LDL · TG = triglycerides · PCSK9 = proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin 9 · HMG-CoA = 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A
  1. Too much LDLenters the endothelium / intima and is oxidised.
  2. Macrophages take it up → foam cells→ fatty streak → plaque.
  3. Plaques rupture→ a site for thrombogenesis → clots that occlude or break off.
  4. Therefore:nutrition + lipid-lowering drugs to prevent disease, and antiplatelet drugs to prevent clotting on plaques in the different pathologies.

Atherosclerosis rarely travels alone — it co-occurs with hypertension, ischemic heart disease and heart failure. Ischemic heart disease and dyslipidemia are covered later; for assessments you're accountable for the mechanism of action of each lipid drug.

Q: What cell becomes a foam cell, and what event turns a stable plaque into an acute coronary syndrome?

Answer

Macrophages that ingest oxidised LDL. Plaque rupture exposes thrombogenic material → platelet aggregation and thrombus (why antiplatelets are used).

Topic 6
⭐ The in-class questions

Reflexes, receptor selectivity, converging pathways

Putting It Together: The Four Class Questions

AbbreviationsNE = norepinephrine · NET = norepinephrine transporter · MAO = monoamine oxidase · COMT = catechol-O-methyltransferase · PDE3 / PDE5 = phosphodiesterase 3 / 5 · HR = heart rate · BP = blood pressure

Q1 — Phenylephrine and reflex bradycardia

  1. Phenylephrine = α1 agonistGq → IP3 → Ca²⁺ → vasoconstriction → ↑ SVR → ↑ BP. (Oral phenylephrine as a decongestant doesn't really work — but IV it does this.)
  2. Baroreceptors sense the stretchin the carotid sinus and aortic arch → brainstem.
  3. Sympathetic dimmer down, parasympathetic up→ ACh on M2 in the SA node → reflex bradycardia.
  4. Block it with an M2 antagonist: atropine.

Q2 — What ends norepinephrine's signal?

  • Unlike ACh, NE is mainly recycled: NET reuptake removes ~70% — the primary termination.
  • What diffuses away is broken down by MAO (and COMT in the liver) — but high NE does not stimulate MAO.
  • Negative feedback: NE hits presynaptic α2 (Gi) → the βγ subunit blocks Ca²⁺ influx → fewer vesicles released. Same trick as μ-opioid and GABA-B receptors (glutamate, etc.). This is how clonidine / α-methyldopa lower BP.
  • High synaptic NE does not increase NE release (the body isolates signalling tracks) and has nothing to do with dopa decarboxylase.

Q3 — M2 antagonist, then IV norepinephrine

ReceptorDoes NE activate it?Consequence
α1YesVasoconstriction (the only driver of peripheral vascular tone)
α2YesPresynaptic negative feedback
β1YesTachycardia (directly)
β2NoNo β2 dilation to oppose α1 — you can't nebulise NE for asthma
💉

NE alone

Unopposed α1 → ↑↑ SVR → ↑ BP → baroreflex → M2 → HR ↓. BP ↑, HR ↓ (the β1 effect is overridden).

🚫

NE after an M2 antagonist

Reflex bradycardia is blocked, so β1 tachycardia shows: HR ↑ and BP ↑.

Q4 — Dobutamine and inamrinone

  • Dobutamine = β1 agonist → Gs → ↑cAMP.
  • Inamrinone (amrinone) / milrinone = PDE3 inhibitor → cAMP isn't broken down. It does not activate a Gs-coupled receptor.
  • Common ground: both increase cAMP → inotropy + chronotropy. Together → easy clinical overshoot.
  • Same pharmacodynamic shape as nitrates + PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil): one makes cGMP, the other stops its breakdown → dangerous hypotension.

Q: IV phenylephrine is given and the heart rate falls. What drug blocks this adverse effect?

Answer

Atropine — the reflex bradycardia is mediated by increased parasympathetic (M2) outflow; block it with an antimuscarinic.

Q: Increased norepinephrine concentration in an adrenergic synapse causes which of the following? (A) activation of dopa decarboxylase (B) increased NE release (C) activation of presynaptic Gi-coupled receptors (D) stimulation of MAO

Answer

C — presynaptic α2 (Gi) negative-feedback inhibition.

Q: A patient receives an M2 antagonist, then IV norepinephrine. (A) HR↑ BP↑ (B) HR↑ BP↓ (C) HR↓ BP↑ (D) HR↓ BP↓

Answer

A — the reflex bradycardia is blocked. C is what NE does on its own.

Q: Dobutamine and inamrinone both increase heart rate by: (A) activating Gs GPCRs (B) inhibiting Na⁺ channels (C) inhibiting Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (D) increasing cAMP

Answer

D — dobutamine is a β1 agonist but inamrinone is not; it inhibits PDE3 so cAMP accumulates.

AUTONOMICS & SIGNALLING

Receptors to know cold

  • α1 vascular SM → vasoconstriction (Gq)
  • α2 presynaptic/CNS → ↓ NE release, ↓ sympathetic outflow (Gi)
  • β1 heart ↑HR/force/conduction + JG cells ↑ renin (Gs)
  • β2 bronchodilation, skeletal-muscle vasodilation (Gs)
  • M2 SA ↓rate, AV ↓conduction (Gi) · M3 SM contraction (Gq)
  • NN in all ganglia → nicotine hits both branches
  • Peripheral vessels: no parasympathetic innervation; tone = α1 only

G proteins

  • Odd = Gq (M1, M3, α1): PLC → IP3 (Ca²⁺) + DAG (PKC)
  • Even = Gi (M2, α2): ⊣ AC; βγ blocks presynaptic Ca²⁺ entry
  • "Betas are BS": all β = Gs → AC → cAMP → PKA
  • Heart: β1 and M2 converge on AC → net cAMP
  • SM: MLCK = contraction; PKA ⊣ MLCK (β2 relax); NO → sGC → cGMP → PKG → MLC phosphatase (relax)
BP DIALS · KIDNEY · ELECTRICAL · LIPIDS

MAP = CO × PVR

  • CO = HR × SV; SV ← preload + contractility; PVR ← arteriolar diameter
  • ↑HR: antimuscarinic, β agonist, PDE3i · ↓HR: M agonist, β-blocker, CCB
  • Preload: venous tone (NO ↓), volume (diuretics, RAAS block ↓)
  • SVR ↑: α1, catecholamines, AT-II, Ca²⁺ · ↓: NO, α1 block, α2 agonists, RASi, CCB
  • HF: ↓ afterload → ↑ SV for same contractility
  • ANS = fast; kidney Na⁺/volume = long-term; HTN is transplantable
  • Hypertension = body defends a high set point

Action potentials

  • P = atrial depol · QRS = ventricular depol · T = ventricular repol
  • Myocyte phase 0 = Na⁺; plateau = Ca²⁺ vs K⁺
  • Nodal phase 0 = Ca²⁺; phase 4 = If
  • Repolarisation (both) = K⁺

Lipids

  • LDL → intima → ox-LDL → macrophage foam cells → plaque → rupture → thrombus (antiplatelets)
  • Know MOA of each lipid drug (statin, ezetimibe, PCSK9i, sequestrant, fibrate, niacin)
THE FOUR CLASS QUESTIONS

Phenylephrine

  • α1 → ↑SVR → ↑BP → baroreceptors (carotid sinus, aortic arch) → ↑ vagal M2 → reflex bradycardia
  • Block with atropine

Ending NE's signal

  • NET reuptake ~70% (main); MAO/COMT for the rest
  • ↑ synaptic NE → presynaptic α2 (Gi) negative feedback (answer C)
  • Same presynaptic brake as μ-opioid, GABA-B; clonidine/methyldopa exploit it

M2 antagonist + NE

  • NE: α1 ✓ α2 ✓ β1 ✓ β2 ✗
  • NE alone: BP↑ HR↓ (reflex)
  • After M2 block: BP↑ HR↑ (answer A)

Dobutamine + inamrinone

  • Dobutamine β1 → ↑cAMP; inamrinone PDE3i → cAMP not broken down
  • Shared: ↑cAMP (answer D) → overshoot together
  • Analogy: nitrates (make cGMP) + PDE5i (keep cGMP) → hypotension
