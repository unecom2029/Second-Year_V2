34-year-old male — labile emotions and erratic movements

Chief Complaint

A 34-year-old male with labile emotions1 and erratic movements

History of Chief Complaint

This is a 34-year-old, right-handed laborer in a paper and pulp company who was referred to the company physician by his floor supervisor. The company had employed him, in good standing, for 16 years; however, recently his work habits and personality had undergone a progressive change2. He had become extremely emotional, yelling at his fellow workers and making unusually rude and sexual comments to the office staff3. He had also begun arriving late and frequently got confused on the job, leaving tasks unfinished4. In addition, he had begun to move his hands and arms strangely. At first insidious, these random movements now interfered with his work5. He appeared clumsy, frequently dropping tools and occasionally stumbling when walking6. The supervisor suspected alcoholism7 and requested the physician's evaluation to determine if it was safe to have him remain at work around heavy industrial equipment.

Family History

At the time of first examination, he was living with his wife and two children, who were 15 and 16 years of age. His mother was alive and in good health. His father had died at age 38 in an accident at the paper company plant 18 years before but had been in good health until that time8. The maternal and paternal grandparents were dead. The paternal grandfather had died at 43 years of age; he had not seen a physician but was described by the family as having gone "daffy" and died of the "shakes," which they had attributed to his excessive drinking9.

Medical History

The patient had left public school at the age of 18 after completing the 10th grade and obtained a job at the paper company. He had not seen a physician previously, and the only records available were those of the grade school nurse, which were unremarkable. He admitted to a 25-pack-year history of smoking and to having consumed two to three (16 oz.) cans of beer per day10.

General Physical Examination

The patient was a well-nourished, well-hydrated, muscular adult male appearing the stated age, with poor hygiene11. Head was normocephalic. Funduscopic examination revealed no evidence of exudate, hemorrhage, or papilledema. There was no cervical, supraclavicular, or inguinal lymphadenopathy. The thyroid was positioned on the midline without masses or nodules. He had a regular heart rate and rhythm without gallops or murmurs and carotid pulsations were clear. The lungs were clear to auscultation and percussion. The abdomen was soft, without masses, tenderness, rigidity, or rebound. Peripheral pulses were intact (+2/4) at the radial, femoral, popliteal, dorsales, and tibialis. There was no peripheral edema. Genitalia showed a circumcised penis with testes descended bilaterally. A soft, reducible mass was present in the right inguinal ring. Rectal examination showed sphincter tone intact without fissures, tags, or stenosis. Prostate was grade II without nodules or tenderness, and the stool was guaiac negative.

Neurologic Examination
Mental Status

The patient was awake but seemed disoriented with respect to time and place12. He was irritable and responded inconsistently to questions. He was able to add and subtract single-digit numbers but could not divide or multiply. He could follow most two-step commands but not three-step commands13. Speech, comprehension, and memory were appropriate for his education.

Cranial Nerves

He had a full range of eye movements and complete visual fields. Pupils were equal and reactive to light, both direct and consensual. His hearing was normal in both ears. His facial expressions were full and symmetric. The corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes were intact. The palate elevated symmetrically and the tongue protruded on the midline. His shoulders elevated symmetrically.

Motor Systems

Strength was 5/5 in the upper and lower extremities; coordination appeared intact but was hard to assess because of the involuntary motion. Tone in the limbs appeared slack, when not in motion14. Deep tendon reflexes were +2/4 symmetric in all limbs; however, tendon taps were pendular15. A continuous writhing motion was present in his hands and arms16. This consisted of jerky, quick motions about the wrist and slower, wandering motions in the arm17. He could not stop these motions on command18 and frequently tried to hide the more obvious ones by combining the motion with other, more purposeful arm movements19. He also had jerky movement in his feet and legs that interfered with his gait, causing him to lose balance occasionally and contributing to his drunken appearance. He denied the existence of the involuntary movements20, claiming he was nervous about being in a doctor's office. His wife was not sure when the movements began, but claimed that he had been making them for at least 9 months. She also stated that he did not have any involuntary motion when asleep21.

Sensation

Cutaneous sensory functions were intact throughout the body; proprioception was intact in all limbs22.

Case Questions — Answered

The manual's nine standard questions, answered directly from this patient's exam. Q6–Q9 are expanded further in the Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough below.

Q1Changes in consciousness/cognition, language, spatial recognition, personality/emotional behavior, or memory?

Yes to most. He's disoriented to time and place, can't handle multi-step commands or calculations beyond single-digit add/subtract, and has undergone a progressive personality change — emotional lability, irritability, and disinhibition (yelling, inappropriate sexual comments). Language and memory are grossly appropriate for his education; no aphasia or amnesia is described. Spatial recognition is not specifically tested and no neglect is noted.

Q2Any visual field loss? Any visual hallucinations?

No. Visual fields are complete to confrontation, pupils are equal and reactive, and the fundi are normal. No hallucinations of any kind are reported.

Q3Cranial nerve changes — suprasegmental or segmental?

None. Every cranial nerve tested — extraocular movements, pupils, hearing, facial symmetry, corneal/jaw-jerk/gag reflexes, palate, tongue, shoulder shrug — is normal. There is no suprasegmental or segmental cranial nerve deficit in this case.

Q4Changes in motor function (reflexes, tone, movement, coordination) — suprasegmental or segmental?

Yes, and they're suprasegmental. Strength is full (5/5) and reflexes are only mildly altered in character (pendular, not pathologically brisk or absent) — there's no weakness, spasticity, or asymmetry to suggest a segmental (spinal/peripheral) or corticospinal lesion. What's abnormal is extrapyramidal: slack resting tone, pendular tendon taps, and continuous, involuntary choreic movements of the hands, arms, feet, and legs that he cannot suppress — a basal ganglia (suprasegmental, extrapyramidal) pattern.

Q5Changes in sensory function — what level of the body?

None. Cutaneous sensation and proprioception are intact throughout the entire body. There is no sensory level to localize — this keeps the lesion confined to a motor/behavioral circuit rather than a sensory pathway or spinal cord level.

Q6Temporal profile — acute or chronic, progressive or stable?

Chronic and progressive. The personality change and movements both developed insidiously and have been worsening over many months (movements documented for at least 9 months; work performance decline implied over a longer stretch) — not the sudden onset expected of a vascular event.

Q7Distribution — focal, multifocal, or diffuse?

Diffuse and bilateral. Chorea involves both arms and both legs, and it's paired with diffuse behavioral/cognitive change — a pattern that fits bilateral, symmetric striatal degeneration rather than a single focal lesion.

Q8Differential diagnosis?

Huntington's disease is the leading diagnosis — see the full differential table below. Other considerations (alcohol-related movement disorder, Wilson's disease, tardive dyskinesia, other inherited choreas) are each undercut by some part of this history, most decisively the family history of chorea plus dementia in the paternal line.

Q9If vascular, what arterial territory?

This isn't a vascular presentation — the chronic, progressive, bilateral, hereditary picture argues against a single vascular event. If it were vascular, bilateral basal ganglia involvement would implicate the medial and lateral striate vessels (branches of the anterior and middle cerebral arteries) bilaterally, but that mechanism doesn't fit this patient's story.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

Localization

Suprasegmental — striatum (basal ganglia)

There is no weakness, spasticity, sensory loss, or cranial nerve deficit to suggest a corticospinal, spinal, or peripheral (segmental) lesion. Instead, the findings — slack resting tone, pendular reflexes, and involuntary, unsuppressible chorea — are classic extrapyramidal signs pointing to the neostriatum (caudate and putamen), with the behavioral/cognitive change implicating the caudate's association circuit specifically.

Distribution

Diffuse, bilateral

Chorea is present in both upper and lower extremities on both sides, and the behavioral/cognitive changes are global rather than lateralized. This bilateral, non-focal pattern fits a diffuse neurodegenerative process affecting the striatum symmetrically, not a single focal lesion.

Time Course

Chronic, progressive

The personality change and movement disorder both emerged insidiously and have steadily worsened — movements for at least 9 months, work performance declining over a longer span, all in a man previously in "good standing" for 16 years. This gradually-progressive course is the signature of a neurodegenerative disease, not a stroke or acute toxic/metabolic insult.

Diagnostic Workup

Confirming the diagnosis

  1. Genetic testing for the HTT CAG repeat expansion.The definitive test — confirms Huntington's disease and can quantify repeat length, which correlates loosely with age of onset.
  2. MRI brain.Look for bilateral caudate atrophy with secondary enlargement of the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles — the classic structural correlate of striatal cell loss.
  3. Screening labs to exclude mimics.TSH (thyrotoxicosis), ceruloplasmin/serum copper (Wilson's disease), peripheral smear (acanthocytes), and a toxicology screen.
  4. Formal neuropsychological testing.Characterizes the subcortical dementia pattern (slowed processing, executive dysfunction) and establishes a baseline for tracking progression.
  5. Pedigree and genetic counseling.Given the paternal-line history, map the family tree and discuss implications for his two teenage children before any predictive testing is pursued.
Prognosis

Progressive, currently incurable

Huntington's disease follows a relentlessly progressive course: chorea and cognitive/behavioral decline worsen over years to decades, sometimes evolving into a rigid-akinetic ("Westphal") variant resembling parkinsonism, with eventual severe dementia and loss of independence. No disease-modifying therapy currently exists; average survival is roughly 15–20 years from symptom onset.

Management

Supportive and symptomatic

  • Immediate workplace safety assessment — remove from duties around heavy industrial equipment given chorea plus impaired judgment
  • Genetic counseling for the patient and his two children
  • VMAT2 inhibitor (e.g., tetrabenazine/deutetrabenazine) for chorea
  • Psychiatric support for disinhibition, irritability, and mood lability
  • Multidisciplinary supportive care as the disease progresses (PT/OT, speech/swallow, social work)
  • Counseling on smoking and alcohol use as a general health measure

Concept Deep-Dive

The underlying basal ganglia anatomy and circuitry this case is testing

Term

Corpus Striatum — Naming Conventions

Caudate + putamen = striatum; putamen + globus pallidus = lentiform nucleus; all of it together = corpus striatum. This patient's chorea (putamen circuit) plus behavioral change (caudate circuit) means both halves of the striatum are involved.

Localization

Direct vs. Indirect Pathway

The direct pathway inhibits GPi (disinhibiting thalamus); the indirect pathway crosses GPe → STN before exciting GPi (net effect: more inhibition of thalamus). This patient's chorea comes from the balance between these two tipping toward the direct pathway.

Pattern

Why Striatal Degeneration Causes Chorea

In this patient, loss of the striatal neurons that normally drive the indirect pathway tips the direct/indirect balance so far that GPi can no longer adequately inhibit the thalamus — the thalamocortical circuit runs hot, and the result is the continuous, unsuppressible chorea seen on his exam.

Localization

Caudate vs. Putamen — Behavior vs. Motor

This patient shows both columns — caudate-type disinhibition and executive dysfunction at work, plus putamen-type chorea in the limbs — because Huntington's disease degenerates the caudate and putamen together, not one in isolation.

Term

Chorea, Athetosis, Dystonia — A Spectrum

This patient's exam actually spans two points on the spectrum at once — "jerky, quick motions about the wrist" (chorea) and "slower, wandering motions in the arm" (athetosis) — a common real-world overlap sometimes called choreoathetosis, both driven by the same underlying striatal disease.

Genetics

Autosomal Dominant, With Anticipation

Huntington's disease is caused by a CAG-repeat expansion in the HTT gene (autosomal dominant, 50% risk per child). The repeat can lengthen further when passed through the father, producing anticipation — which fits a grandfather symptomatic around 43 and this patient already symptomatic at 34, and raises the same question for his own two teenagers.
