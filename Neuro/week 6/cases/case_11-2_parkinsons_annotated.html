Case Study 11-2

67-year-old man — reduced motion and a tremor

Working Dx: Idiopathic Parkinson's Disease

Chief Complaint

A 67-year-old complaining of reduced motion and a tremor

History of Chief Complaint

A 67-year-old, right-handed, retired city worker was brought to you by his wife because of "shaking" and "weakness."1 His wife reported that not only did his hands shake, but she complained of changes in his personality2. She also complained that he had become very slow or "weak" in his movements and often sat motionless with an expressionless face3. He had difficulty getting up and moving about the house4. She admitted that this had been going on for over a year and was getting worse5, but she had resisted seeking treatment since she felt that he had just grown lazy after retirement6. He had recently suffered several falls, one of which had resulted in a skin laceration on his forehead7. She was seeking a physician at this time because he had started to make "funny noises with his mouth."8

Medical History

At the time of examination, the patient had been retired for 2 years; until now he had been in good health except for an appendectomy at age 18. He had three children, who were living independently; none had attended college.

General Physical Examination

This was an alert, cooperative, well hydrated, and well-nourished individual, oriented for place and time and appearing his stated age. He was seated quietly and did not offer much information during the examination, letting his wife provide most of the history9. Optic discs were clear with sharp borders. Chest was clear to auscultation and percussion. Blood pressure was normal; peripheral pulses were intact; respirations and temperature were normal. Abdomen was soft to palpation, with no masses or tenderness present. Skin was of normal texture and turgor; a recent skin laceration, 2 cm in length, was present on his forehead.

Neurologic Examination
Mental Status

The patient was alert, oriented for time and place, and cooperative. Memory and knowledge were appropriate for his age. Speech was clear and meaningful, but soft and low in volume10; his comprehension of language was good. He was capable of writing, but his letters were noticeably reduced in size when compared with a previous sample of 10 years ago provided by his wife11.

Cranial Nerves

His range of movement for the extraocular eye muscles was full, and visual fields were complete to confrontation. The corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes were intact; palate and uvula elevated symmetrically; tongue protruded midline; and shoulder shrug was symmetric. A three-per-second resting tremor was present in the orofacial musculature that diminished on speaking and swallowing or when he opened his mouth12. There was a detectable high-frequency hearing loss, more in the right ear than in the left13.

Motor Systems

His strength was intact and deep tendon reflexes were normal in all extremities. He had a three-per-second tremor in both upper and lower extremities that was ameliorated with movement and returned upon resting14. There was cogwheel rigidity upon passive movement of the limbs15. He had no dysmetria or past-pointing present in any extremity16. With his arms extended, the tremor diminished and there was no pronator drift17. The tremor returned when his arms were relaxed. His gait was slow, with many shuffling steps18. Postural reflexes were compromised; if given an abrupt push, he retropulsed, with many short steps and was at risk for falling19. The patient could not stand from a seated position in a low, soft-padded chair, but he could, after one or two attempts, rise from a higher chair with a stiffer seat20.

Sensation

Discriminative touch and proprioception were intact throughout the body and face21.

Case Questions — Answered

The manual's nine standard questions, answered directly from this patient's exam. Q6–Q9 are expanded further in the Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough below.

Q1Changes in consciousness/cognition, language, spatial recognition, personality/emotional behavior, or memory?

Formal mental status is largely intact — alert, oriented, memory and knowledge appropriate for age, comprehension good. The "personality change" and reduced conversational output his wife describes are better explained by hypomimia (masked facies), hypophonia, and apathy — recognized non-motor/motor features of parkinsonism — than by a true cognitive or personality disorder. No language deficit beyond reduced volume; no spatial recognition problem noted.

Q2Any visual field loss? Any visual hallucinations?

No. Optic discs are clear, visual fields are full to confrontation, and no hallucinations are reported.

Q3Cranial nerve changes — suprasegmental or segmental?

No true cranial nerve palsy — extraocular movements, facial symmetry, and the corneal/jaw-jerk/gag reflexes are all intact. The one abnormal cranial finding, a resting orofacial tremor that quiets with active mouth movement, is a suprasegmental (extrapyramidal) movement-disorder sign layered on an otherwise normal cranial nerve exam, not a segmental CN lesion. The high-frequency hearing loss is an incidental, age-related peripheral finding.

Q4Changes in motor function (reflexes, tone, movement, coordination) — suprasegmental or segmental?

Suprasegmental/extrapyramidal, and it's the core of the case: a 3 Hz rest tremor (limbs and orofacial region) that improves with movement, cogwheel rigidity, bradykinesia (slow gait, difficulty rising from a low chair), and impaired postural reflexes (retropulsion). Strength and deep tendon reflexes are normal, and there's no dysmetria or pronator drift — so there's no segmental (LMN/UMN) or cerebellar component here.

Q5Changes in sensory function — what level of the body?

None. Discriminative touch and proprioception are intact throughout the body and face.

Q6Temporal profile — acute or chronic, progressive or stable?

Chronic and progressive — symptoms have been present for over a year and are described as steadily worsening, initially mistaken for age-related slowing or post-retirement laziness.

Q7Distribution — focal, multifocal, or diffuse?

Diffuse and essentially bilateral — tremor is present in the face and in both upper and lower extremities, and bradykinesia/gait involvement is generalized rather than confined to one limb or side.

Q8Differential diagnosis?

Idiopathic Parkinson's disease is the leading diagnosis — see the full differential table below. Early falls and a bilateral-at-presentation tremor are worth flagging as possible clues toward an atypical parkinsonian syndrome, but they don't outweigh the otherwise classic tetrad seen here.

Q9If vascular, what arterial territory?

This is not a vascular presentation — it's a chronic, progressive degenerative process (loss of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons), not an acute event. Vascular (lower-body) parkinsonism is a consideration in this age group, but it would implicate bilateral small penetrating (lenticulostriate) vessels causing subcortical white-matter disease, and it doesn't typically produce a prominent rest tremor like this patient's.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

Localization

Suprasegmental — basal ganglia (nigrostriatal)

Normal strength, reflexes, and sensation, plus the absence of dysmetria and pronator drift, rule out a segmental (LMN/UMN) or cerebellar process. What's left is a pure extrapyramidal picture — rest tremor, cogwheel rigidity, bradykinesia, and impaired postural reflexes — the classic signature of substantia nigra / basal ganglia dysfunction.

Distribution

Diffuse, essentially bilateral

Tremor involves the face and all four limbs, and bradykinesia/gait changes are generalized. Bilateral involvement at presentation (rather than the classic asymmetric/unilateral onset of early idiopathic Parkinson's disease) is a subtle atypical note worth carrying into the differential.

Time Course

Chronic, progressive

Over a year of gradual worsening, initially attributed to aging or post-retirement laziness — the slow, insidious trajectory expected of a neurodegenerative disease rather than a stroke or acute toxic/metabolic insult.

Diagnostic Workup

Confirming the diagnosis, screening for red flags

  1. Detailed medication history.Rule out antipsychotics, metoclopramide, or other dopamine-blocking agents before labeling this idiopathic.
  2. Targeted exam for atypical-parkinsonism red flags.Vertical (especially downward) gaze, orthostatic blood pressure, cerebellar signs — screening for features that would point away from idiopathic PD.
  3. Trial of levodopa.A robust, sustained motor response supports idiopathic PD; a poor or absent response raises suspicion for an atypical syndrome.
  4. Neuroimaging.MRI brain to exclude structural or vascular contributors; a DaTscan can confirm a dopaminergic deficit if the diagnosis remains unclear.
  5. Fall-risk and home-safety evaluation.Given his recent falls and forehead laceration, refer for a formal gait/balance assessment and home safety review.
Prognosis

Chronic, slowly progressive

Idiopathic Parkinson's disease typically responds well to dopaminergic therapy for years (sometimes called the "levodopa honeymoon"), though bradykinesia, gait, and postural instability tend to become less responsive over time, and non-motor features (cognitive change, autonomic dysfunction, mood symptoms) often emerge later. If this instead proves to be an atypical parkinsonian syndrome, the expected course is faster and less responsive to dopaminergic treatment.

Management

Symptomatic and supportive

  • Dopaminergic therapy (levodopa/carbidopa or a dopamine agonist) as first-line treatment
  • Fall-prevention plan — home safety review, gait aids as needed
  • Physical/occupational therapy for gait, balance, and transfers
  • Speech therapy for hypophonia (e.g., LSVT LOUD)
  • Ongoing monitoring for emerging non-motor and cognitive symptoms
  • Caregiver support and education for his wife

Concept Deep-Dive

The underlying basal ganglia anatomy and circuitry this case is testing

Localization

Motor Circuit of the Basal Ganglia

The putamen-based motor loop scales the intensity of voluntary movement. In this patient, basal ganglia output through this exact loop is what's driving his bradykinesia, rigidity, and slow, shuffling gait.

Term

Nigrostriatal Dopamine & the Direct/Indirect Balance

Dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta normally excites the direct pathway and inhibits the indirect pathway — tilting the balance toward movement. In this patient, degeneration of these SNc neurons removes that tilt.

Pattern

Why Dopamine Loss Causes Bradykinesia

This is the mirror image of Huntington's disease: instead of too little inhibition of thalamus, this patient has too much. His slowness in rising from a chair and his shuffling gait are the direct behavioral output of an over-inhibited thalamocortical circuit.
