Case Study 11-3

54-year-old woman — post-stroke movement disorder, 9 months out

Working Dx: Posthemiplegic Hemiballism-Hemichorea

Chief Complaint

A 54-year-old woman status post-stroke 9 months ago for which she has a movement disorder and who is now presenting for evaluation

History of Chief Complaint

This was a 54-year-old, right-handed housewife who developed hemiparesis and hemiparesthesia of rapid onset in the right arm and leg 9 months earlier1. Subsequently, the paresis and sensory deficit resolved over a 3-month interval2; however, an involuntary, flinging motion of the right arm and a writhing, jerky motion of the right leg slowly developed during this time3. She is in considerable distress, since the involuntary motions of her extremity disrupted her gait and postural station and thus incapacitated her in her daily routines4. She admitted to severe social embarrassment because of the involuntary motion5.

Medical History

Her past medical history was positive for hypertension, smoking, and alcohol consumption6. Nine months previously, she had suffered a cerebrovascular event that left her with hemiparesis and hemibody sensory loss on the right side7. Language and cognition were not noticeably affected in this event8.

General Physical Examination

The patient was an alert, well-hydrated, but underweight female, appearing older than the stated age. Signs of exhaustion and distress were evident, and she was of anxious demeanor9. Her optic discs were clear and had sharp borders; visual acuity was normal. Her neck was supple, with no bruits. Her blood pressure, heart rate, and respirations were slightly elevated. Her chest was clear to percussion, and the abdomen was soft, with no masses or tenderness. The remainder of the examination was precluded because of the excessive involuntary limb motion10.

Neurologic Examination
Mental Status

This is an alert, oriented, and cooperative female in considerable emotional distress. Language, comprehension, reading, and memory were appropriate11.

Cranial Nerve

Testing was complicated by the violence of her involuntary motion in the upper extremity12. She had a full range of eye movements, pupils were equal and reactive to light, and accommodation was intact. The corneal and gag reflexes were intact; jaw-jerk was normotensive. Her hearing was equal in both ears. Her tongue protruded on the midline.

Motor Systems

Muscle strength and reflexes were normal in both extremities on the left but were difficult to test definitively on the right because of the continuous and violent involuntary motion13. The movement in the upper extremity consisted of violent flinging motions superimposed on a continuous writhing jerky movement14. The lower extremity demonstrated the continuous writhing motion with only brief jerks15. Occasionally, the jerky motion in the lower extremity became violent. Attempts to reduce the motion in either extremity by physical restraint were unsuccessful16. She could move the right extremities on command in between the involuntary motions17. Gait was severely compromised by the flinging of the upper extremity18. Although she did not experience an embarrassment of postural reflexes19, the upper extremity motion was continually pulling her off station. The involuntary movement of the right extremities was ameliorated with sleep but returned upon waking20.

Sensation

Vibratory sensation and pinprick were intact on the left; to the extent that it could be tested, both modalities were equivocal on the right21.

Case Questions — Answered

The manual's nine standard questions, answered directly from this patient's exam. Q6–Q9 are expanded further in the Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough below.

Q1Changes in consciousness/cognition, language, spatial recognition, personality/emotional behavior, or memory?

Cognition, language, comprehension, reading, and memory are all explicitly documented as appropriate — both at the time of her original stroke and on this exam. She is, understandably, in considerable emotional distress with an anxious demeanor and signs of exhaustion, but that's a reasonable reaction to nine months of a disabling, embarrassing movement disorder, not a primary personality or psychiatric change.

Q2Any visual field loss? Any visual hallucinations?

No. Visual acuity is normal, optic discs are clear, and no hallucinations are reported.

Q3Cranial nerve changes — suprasegmental or segmental?

None. Extraocular movements, pupils, corneal/gag reflexes, jaw-jerk, hearing, and tongue position are all normal — testing was simply made difficult by the violent arm movement, not by any true cranial nerve deficit.

Q4Changes in motor function (reflexes, tone, movement, coordination) — suprasegmental or segmental?

Yes, and it's the core of the case — but note the history has two motor components. Nine months ago there was a segmental/corticospinal sign (hemiparesis, i.e., true weakness) that has since resolved. What remains now is purely suprasegmental/extrapyramidal: violent flinging (ballism) of the right arm and writhing, jerky (choreoathetosis) movements of the right arm and leg, with normal strength and reflexes on the left and preserved voluntary movement of the right limbs between episodes.

Q5Changes in sensory function — what level of the body?

There was a transient right hemibody sensory loss at the time of the stroke that resolved along with the weakness. Currently, sensation is fully intact on the left; on the right it's "equivocal," but that's most likely a testing artifact from the violent involuntary motion rather than a definite residual deficit.

Q6Temporal profile — acute or chronic, progressive or stable?

Biphasic. An acute stroke 9 months ago produced sudden hemiparesis and hemiparesthesia; that deficit resolved over about 3 months, after which a movement disorder gradually emerged and has now been persistent and disabling for months. The initial event was acute; the current complaint is chronic and essentially stable (not actively acute or progressive).

Q7Distribution — focal, multifocal, or diffuse?

Focal and strictly unilateral — right arm and leg only, with the entire left side completely spared. This is a sharp contrast to Cases 11-1 and 11-2, where the process was diffuse and bilateral.

Q8Differential diagnosis?

Posthemiplegic hemiballism-hemichorea from a focal contralateral (left) subthalamic/lenticular infarct is the leading diagnosis — see the full differential table below. A focal motor seizure and a structural mass lesion are reasonable alternatives to actively rule out, but the classic biphasic stroke story argues strongly for the vascular explanation.

Q9If vascular, what arterial territory?

Yes — this is fundamentally a vascular case. The lesion is on the left (contralateral to her right-sided findings), most likely a small penetrating-vessel (lacunar) infarct involving the subthalamic nucleus and/or adjacent lenticular nucleus (putamen)/internal capsule. That territory is supplied by lenticulostriate branches of the middle cerebral artery and/or thalamic-subthalamic perforators, and a lacunar mechanism fits well with her vascular risk factors (hypertension, smoking).

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

Localization

Suprasegmental — left subthalamic/lenticular region

The combination of ballism (subthalamic nucleus) and choreoathetosis (striatum/lenticular nucleus), a preceding contralateral hemiparesis/hemiparesthesia (internal capsule), and completely spared language and cognition together point to a discrete, deep, contralateral (left) subcortical lesion rather than a cortical stroke or a diffuse basal ganglia disease.

Distribution

Focal, strictly unilateral

Only the right arm and leg are affected; the left side is entirely normal. This focal, one-sided pattern is the key distribution clue that separates a discrete vascular lesion from the diffuse, bilateral basal ganglia diseases seen in Cases 11-1 (Huntington's) and 11-2 (Parkinson's).

Time Course

Biphasic — acute event, then a delayed, persistent movement disorder

An abrupt stroke 9 months ago, a 3-month resolution of the original weakness/numbness, and then a slowly emerging movement disorder that has now been stable and disabling for several months. This staged pattern — acute vascular insult followed by a delayed hyperkinesia — is itself a diagnostic clue (a posthemiplegic movement disorder).

Diagnostic Workup

Confirming the lesion, preventing the next stroke

  1. MRI brain.Look for a chronic infarct involving the left subthalamic nucleus and/or lenticular nucleus (putamen)/internal capsule, confirming the localization suggested by the exam.
  2. Vascular risk-factor workup.Blood pressure, lipid panel, glucose/HbA1c — she already carries hypertension and a smoking history, the classic small-vessel (lacunar) risk profile.
  3. Cardiac and carotid evaluation.Echocardiogram and carotid imaging to exclude an embolic source, since a cardioembolic mechanism can occasionally mimic a lacunar presentation.
  4. Movement-disorder characterization.Formally grade the severity of the ballism/chorea to guide and monitor treatment response.
  5. Psychosocial assessment.Given her considerable distress, exhaustion, and embarrassment, screen for a co-existing mood disorder and connect her with support resources.
Prognosis

Often persistent, but can gradually ease

Posthemiplegic movement disorders can improve gradually over the first year or two after the causative stroke, but many patients — like this one, already nine months in — are left with a significant, persistent disability. Her ongoing vascular risk factors also carry a real risk of a second cerebrovascular event, making secondary prevention just as important as treating the movement disorder itself.

Management

Symptomatic treatment plus secondary stroke prevention

  • Pharmacologic treatment of hemiballism — a dopamine-depleting agent (e.g., tetrabenazine) or a low-dose antipsychotic; an anticonvulsant such as valproate is another option
  • Aggressive secondary stroke prevention — antihypertensive therapy, antiplatelet therapy, smoking and alcohol cessation counseling
  • Physical/occupational therapy for gait and fall safety, given how much the arm movement disrupts her balance
  • Consider referral for stereotactic surgical options (e.g., pallidotomy) if medication-refractory
  • Psychosocial support and counseling for the distress and embarrassment she's experiencing

Concept Deep-Dive

The underlying basal ganglia anatomy and circuitry this case is testing

Localization

The Subthalamic Nucleus and Hemiballism

The subthalamic nucleus normally provides excitatory drive that keeps GPi active and thalamus appropriately inhibited. Knock out the STN with a small vascular lesion, and GPi output collapses — thalamus runs unchecked, and the result is the violent, large-amplitude flinging seen in this patient's right arm.

Term

Ballism, Chorea, Athetosis, Dystonia — One Spectrum

Ballism is essentially chorea's larger, more violent cousin — same basal ganglia disinhibition mechanism, bigger amplitude, usually proximal. This patient shows the full range at once: ballism in the arm, choreoathetosis in the leg, all from a single small lesion.

Pattern

Posthemiplegic Movement Disorders — the Timeline

The initial hemiplegia effectively masks a separate, adjacent basal ganglia injury until the corticospinal deficit resolves enough to unmask it — which is exactly why this patient's movement disorder wasn't the presenting problem nine months ago, but is now.
