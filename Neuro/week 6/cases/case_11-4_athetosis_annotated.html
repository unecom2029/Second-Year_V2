Case Study 11-4

75-year-old man — postural movement disorder, 2 years post-stroke

Working Dx: Posthemiplegic Athetosis (Right Internal Capsule Infarct)

Chief Complaint

A 75-year-old man with a history of a postural movement disorder subsequent to a cerebrovascular accident who presents for evaluation

History of Chief Complaint

This was a 75-year-old, right-handed male with tonic posturing motions of the left upper extremity that developed subsequent to a resolving spastic paralysis1. He presented for an annual checkup, 2 years following a cerebrovascular event2.

Medical History

He was a Jewish scholar who emigrated from eastern Poland to the United States after World War II. He had been in good health until 2 years before, when he suffered a cerebrovascular accident of rapid onset that left him with spastic paralysis and sensory paresthesia in the left extremities3. At that time his strength in the left limbs was 2/5 and his deep tendon reflexes were elevated at +4/44. No language or cognitive deficit was recorded at the time of the first presentation5.

General Physical Examination

This was an alert, oriented, and cooperative man. He was well nourished and well hydrated and appeared his stated age. He had male-pattern baldness. His optic discs were clear with sharp borders. Visual acuity was normal with his glasses. His neck was supple; a slight bruit was present over the right carotid6. His chest was clear to percussion and abdomen was soft to palpation, with no tenderness or masses. His heart rate, blood pressure, and respirations were physiologic. His peripheral pulses were intact at the wrists and ankles. His skin was warm and moist, with good turgor.

Neurologic Examination
Mental Status

He was alert, oriented for time and place, and cooperative. He gave a precise history. Memory was appropriate; speech, writing, and reading (English, Polish, and Hebrew) were intact. He could list all the presidents in order and recite passages from the Torah verbatim7.

Cranial Nerves

He had a full range of eye movements and visual fields were intact. His hearing was significantly diminished, especially for the high frequencies, more so in the right ear than in the left8. The corneal and jaw-jerk reflexes were intact; facial expression was symmetric and appropriate to the situation. The gag reflex was intact; palate, uvula, and tongue were symmetric in position. He had slightly diminished sensation to vibratory sense on the left side of his face9.

Motor Systems

Strength was 5/5, and deep tendon reflexes were +2/4 for both extremities on the right. Strength was mildly reduced (4/5) for the upper limb on the left, and deep tendon reflexes were slightly elevated (+3/4)10. The lower limb on the left had reduced strength (3/5) and increased deep tendon reflexes (+3/4)11. An involuntary, posturing movement was present in the left limbs that had not been detected in his first presentation, poststroke12. In the upper limb the movement consisted of slow, writhing postural changes, including pronounced flexion of the wrist, and phalangeal-metacarpal joints13. With the upper limbs held horizontally extended, the left limb wandered in position continuously14. Occasional sudden, jerky movements of the upper limb occurred15. The lower left limb displayed slow postural movements that interfered with his gait. The movement disorder was somewhat masked by the more pronounced residual paralysis in the lower left limb16. The involuntary motion ceased when the patient slept, returning when he awoke17. Past-pointing and dysmetria were not present on either side18. Pronator drift did not appear to be present, but this was difficult to evaluate on the left side because of the wandering motion in the upper limb19. With the upper limbs extended anteriorly and held in a fixed position, a 10-Hz tremor was present in the right arm, but not in the left. This movement in the right limb was ameliorated when the arm was relaxed to the adducted portion20. The tremor could be seen in his writing, particularly if he held his hand off of the paper's surface as he wrote21.

Sensation

Response to pinprick, vibratory stimuli, and position sense was normal on the right side of his body and only slightly reduced on the left side22.

Case Questions — Answered

The manual's nine standard questions, answered directly from this patient's exam. Q6–Q9 are expanded further in the Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough below.

Q1Changes in consciousness/cognition, language, spatial recognition, personality/emotional behavior, or memory?

None — and impressively so. He's alert, oriented, gives a precise history, and demonstrates fully intact memory, trilingual speech/reading/writing, and even verbatim recall. There's no personality change described. This is about as complete a picture of preserved higher cortical function as an exam can show.

Q2Any visual field loss? Any visual hallucinations?

No. Visual fields are intact and extraocular movements are full; no hallucinations are reported.

Q3Cranial nerve changes — suprasegmental or segmental?

Essentially normal — full eye movements, symmetric face, intact corneal/jaw-jerk/gag reflexes, midline palate/tongue. Two minor incidental notes: asymmetric high-frequency hearing loss (age-related, unrelated) and mildly diminished facial vibratory sensation on the left, which is more a subtle extension of his sensory findings than a true cranial nerve deficit.

Q4Changes in motor function (reflexes, tone, movement, coordination) — suprasegmental or segmental?

Suprasegmental, and there are two separate stories layered together. First, a persistent (partially resolved) upper motor neuron deficit on the left — mild weakness with hyperreflexia in both the arm and leg, worse in the leg. Second, and new since his original presentation, athetosis of the left limbs — slow writhing postures, wrist/finger flexion, continuous wandering with the arms extended, and occasional jerky movements. A third, separate finding — a 10 Hz action/postural tremor in the right arm — is on the unaffected side and is very likely an unrelated, incidental essential tremor rather than part of the stroke syndrome.

Q5Changes in sensory function — what level of the body?

Yes — mildly reduced pinprick, vibratory, and position sense on the left side of the body (plus mildly diminished facial vibratory sense on the left), consistent with residual sensory involvement from the original right-hemisphere stroke. The right side is fully normal.

Q6Temporal profile — acute or chronic, progressive or stable?

Chronic. It's been 2 years since an acute stroke; the initial spastic paralysis has only partially resolved, and the athetosis emerged gradually afterward, absent at the very first presentation. At this point the picture appears to be a stable, chronic residual deficit rather than an actively evolving one.

Q7Distribution — focal, multifocal, or diffuse?

Focal and unilateral — every stroke-related finding (weakness, hyperreflexia, athetosis, sensory change) is confined to the left side of the body. The right-arm tremor is a separate, isolated finding on the opposite side, unrelated to the stroke's distribution.

Q8Differential diagnosis?

Posthemiplegic athetosis from a right internal capsule infarct is the leading diagnosis — see the full table below. The right-arm tremor is best regarded as a separate, incidental essential tremor rather than a second neurologic event tied to the stroke.

Q9If vascular, what arterial territory?

Yes, vascular — most likely a small penetrating-vessel infarct of the right posterior limb of the internal capsule, where corticospinal and pallidothalamic fibers travel together (lenticulostriate or anterior choroidal artery territory). The bruit over the right carotid raises right carotid atherosclerosis as a plausible embolic source feeding that territory.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

Localization

Suprasegmental — right internal capsule

Persistent hyperreflexic weakness (corticospinal tract) plus athetosis (pallidothalamic fibers) in the same left-sided limbs, with cortical functions and cerebellar coordination completely spared, points to a single discrete lesion of the right posterior limb of the internal capsule — the one place these two fiber systems travel side by side.

Distribution

Focal, unilateral

Every stroke-related abnormality is confined to the left side of the body. The right-arm action tremor is a separate, isolated finding on the opposite side — worth noting, but not part of this distribution.

Time Course

Chronic — 2 years out, now stable

An acute stroke 2 years ago produced severe spastic paralysis; that has only partially resolved, and a separate movement disorder (athetosis) emerged afterward and has apparently plateaued into a stable, chronic picture by this annual checkup.

Diagnostic Workup

Confirming the lesion, addressing the carotid finding

  1. Carotid duplex ultrasound.Given the right carotid bruit, evaluate for stenosis as a possible embolic source and to guide secondary prevention.
  2. MRI brain.Confirm a chronic right internal capsule infarct and exclude any new or additional lesion.
  3. Cardiac evaluation.Echocardiogram and rhythm monitoring to further assess embolic risk.
  4. Vascular risk factor optimization.Blood pressure, lipids, and glucose control given his age and prior stroke.
  5. Separate work-up of the right-arm tremor.Apply standard essential tremor criteria (family history, alcohol responsiveness) independently of the stroke evaluation.
Prognosis

Chronic, likely at a plateau

Posthemiplegic athetosis, once established, tends to be a stable, chronic deficit; at 2 years, his residual weakness and movement disorder are likely close to their long-term baseline. His main ongoing risk is a second cerebrovascular event, given the carotid finding and vascular risk profile. The right-arm tremor, if essential tremor, typically has its own separate, slowly progressive but benign course.

Management

Secondary prevention plus symptomatic care

  • Secondary stroke prevention — antiplatelet therapy, carotid intervention if significant stenosis is confirmed, risk factor control
  • Spasticity management — physical therapy, positioning/splinting, antispasmodic medication if functionally limiting
  • Symptomatic treatment of athetosis if disabling, with the same general pharmacologic approach used for other basal ganglia hyperkinesias
  • Independent evaluation and treatment of the right-arm tremor (e.g., propranolol or primidone) if it bothers him functionally — understanding it as unrelated to the stroke
  • Routine follow-up given his age and vascular risk profile

Concept Deep-Dive

The underlying basal ganglia anatomy and circuitry this case is testing

Localization

The Internal Capsule: Two Fiber Systems, One Lesion

Because the corticospinal and pallidothalamic fiber systems run side by side through this narrow bottleneck, one small lesion here can injure both at once — explaining why this patient has persistent spastic weakness (corticospinal) and athetosis (pallidothalamic) in exactly the same limbs.

Term

Ballism, Chorea, Athetosis, Dystonia — Across the Chapter

Four cases, four points on the same basal ganglia hyperkinesia spectrum. This patient's slow, sustained wrist and finger posturing sits squarely at the athetosis end — slower and more sustained than the chorea in Case 11-1 or the ballism in Case 11-3.

Pattern

One Lesion, Two Outputs

A single small infarct produces two clinically distinct signs at once simply because two functionally unrelated fiber systems happen to travel through the same narrow anatomical bottleneck.
