Background

History & Clinical Overview

James Parkinson first described this disease in 1817 in "An Essay on the Shaking Palsy" — he called it paralysis agitans. His case series gives modern-day context: many now suspect environmental/industrial chemical exposures accumulating since that era play a role, though this remains theoretical.

The original 1817 essay

Dr. James Parkinson (1755–1824)

Parkinson disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, with Lewy bodies in the surviving neurons — often the only way to confirm the diagnosis is postmortem, at autopsy.

Lewy bodies — eosinophilic, intracytoplasmic inclusions in a surviving substantia nigra neuron

1

Resting Tremor

Involuntary trembling when the limb is completely at rest — improves with intentional movement.

2

Rigidity

Inability to properly initiate movements — increased resistance throughout range of motion.

3

Bradykinesia

Slowness of movement — the feature most disabling for activities of daily living.

🧠 Memory Hook — The Triad (and the 4th you'll hear elsewhere)

This lecture drills 3 hallmark features: resting Tremor, Rigidity, Bradykinesia. Elsewhere you'll see the classic 4-part mnemonic TRAP — Tremor, Rigidity, Akinesia/bradykinesia, Postural instability. Know both, but for this course's testing purposes, the 3 listed here are the ones explicitly called out.

🩺 Non-Motor Symptoms — Don't Forget These
  • Cognitive dysfunction and dementia; psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions)
  • Mood disorders — depression, anxiety, apathy/abulia
  • Sleep disturbances, fatigue
  • Autonomic dysfunction — orthostatic hypotension occurs in up to 60% of PD patients ⭐ HY
  • Olfactory dysfunction (often an early, pre-motor clue), GI dysfunction, pain/sensory disturbances, dermatologic findings
🔑 The Threshold for Symptoms

Clinical symptoms typically emerge only after ~50% loss of dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatum — which corresponds to an even larger 70–80% loss of actual striatal dopamine. The brain has substantial reserve capacity before symptoms surface. ⭐ HY

Why Does This Happen?

Etiology & Drug-Induced Parkinsonism

The true etiology of idiopathic PD (IPD) remains unknown. Leading theory: oxidative stress — oxidation of dopamine itself generates highly reactive free radicals that are selectively toxic to the dopaminergic neurons producing it (a kind of metabolic self-destruction). Other proposed contributors: infectious agents, environmental toxins, and genetic predisposition.

📰 In the Headlines (FYI — not exam material, but shows where the field is looking)
  • Trichloroethylene (an industrial solvent) proposed as an "invisible" environmental cause
  • Braak's hypothesis: GI syndromes may precede PD diagnosis — a gut-brain axis clue
  • Desulfovibrio gut bacteria shown to enhance alpha-synuclein aggregation in a C. elegans model

Drug-Induced Parkinsonism

You don't need idiopathic disease to get parkinsonian features — any drug that blocks striatal D2 receptors can mimic the loss of dopamine signaling and produce a clinically identical picture.

🤢

Antiemetics

  • Metoclopramide
  • Prochlorperazine
💊

Antipsychotics

  • Haloperidol, olanzapine, risperidone
  • Phenothiazines
🧠 Memory Hook

Traditional antipsychotics work specifically by blocking D2 receptors — the exact same receptor whose loss of stimulation causes idiopathic PD. Anything that blocks D2 can produce parkinsonism. This is also why levodopa and antipsychotics are pharmacologic enemies (see Drug Interactions below).

The Foundation for Everything Else

Direct & Indirect Pathways

You don't need to memorize every nucleus in this circuit — but understanding why D2 receptors are the central target explains almost every drug in this lecture. The cortex needs excitatory glutamate input (from itself and from thalamic VA/VL nuclei) to drive the motor cortex and initiate movement. Whether that thalamic drive is allowed to happen is controlled by two competing basal ganglia loops.

Normal circuitry (top) vs. Parkinsonism (bottom) — note the loss of substantia nigra dopamine output on both pathways

✅

Direct Pathway — D1 Receptors

Substantia nigra dopamine → stimulates D1 (coupled to Gs) on the striatum → ↑GABA release onto GPi/SNr → inhibits the inhibitor → thalamus is freed to excite the cortex → movement proceeds.

🛑

Indirect Pathway — D2 Receptors

Substantia nigra dopamine → stimulates D2 (coupled to Gi) on the striatum → this normally suppresses a chain (external GP → subthalamic nucleus → GPi/SNr) that would otherwise drive GABA onto the thalamus and block movement.

🧠 Memory Hook — The "Double Negative"

Both pathways rely on inhibiting an inhibitor. Direct = D1 = "Drive" (dopamine directly enables movement via a double-negative). Indirect = D2 = "Interrupt the interrupter" — dopamine's job here is to keep the movement-blocking chain suppressed. In PD, you lose dopamine on both pathways: you lose some ability to actively drive movement (direct), but far more importantly, you lose the brake on the indirect pathway, and it becomes chronically overactive — flooding the thalamus with inhibitory GABA and locking movement down. ⭐ HY

💡 One More Player: Cholinergic Interneurons

The striatum also contains acetylcholine interneurons that help modulate motor output. Dopamine loss without a corresponding loss of these cholinergic neurons creates an imbalance that contributes to motor fluctuations and dyskinesias — this is the rationale for using anticholinergic drugs as adjunctive PD therapy.

Q: In Parkinson disease, is the indirect pathway over- or under-active — and why does that produce bradykinesia?

Answer

Overactive. Loss of dopaminergic D2 stimulation removes the normal brake on the indirect pathway. The chain (↓D2 → ↓GABA on external GP → disinhibited subthalamic nucleus → ↑glutamate drive on GPi/SNr → ↑GABA onto thalamus) runs unchecked, chronically inhibiting the thalamus and starving the motor cortex of its excitatory drive.

Framing Every Drug That Follows

Goals of Treatment

What current therapy CAN do:

  • Improve motor and non-motor symptoms
  • Preserve functional mobility (ADLs) for several years — not forever
  • Improve quality of life and increase life expectancy
  • Minimize adverse effects, depression, fatigue, and sleep disturbance
🚫 What It Cannot Do

Current drug therapy does NOT reverse the disease or halt its progression. ⭐ HY Every drug below is symptomatic management, full stop — this single fact underlies nearly every "goals of care" question you'll see on this topic.

🧠 Memory Hook — Where Each Class Works

Carbidopa and COMT inhibitors work in the periphery (protect levodopa on the way in). MAO-B inhibitors work inside the brain (protect dopamine once it's there). Dopamine agonists skip the conversion step entirely and hit the receptor directly. Keep this "where does it act" framework running as you go through each class below.

The Cornerstone Drug

Levodopa/Carbidopa

⭐ HY All patients with PD will eventually require levodopa — the question is only ever when to start it.

Mechanism

  • Levodopa is the immediate precursor to dopamine, converted by L-aromatic amino acid decarboxylase (LAAD) — also called DOPA decarboxylase, same enzyme, different name
  • Dopamine itself cannot cross the blood-brain barrier — levodopa can
  • Carbidopa is a peripheral LAAD inhibitor given alongside levodopa to block its conversion to dopamine before it reaches the brain, sparing more levodopa for CNS entry
  • A trial of levodopa with clinical improvement can help confirm a suspected PD diagnosis

The master MOA diagram — refer back to this for COMT-I, MAO-B-I, and dopamine agonists below (Brenner & Stevens, Pharmacology)

🧠 Memory Hook — Carbidopa's One Job

The single most important fact about carbidopa: it never enters the brain. If it did, it would block LAAD centrally too — and dopamine synthesis requires LAAD activity in the brain. Carbidopa's entire value is staying outside and guarding the periphery so levodopa isn't wasted before it crosses the BBB. ⭐ HY

Pharmacokinetics

  • Absorbed from the proximal duodenum via amino acid transporters — dietary amino acids compete for uptake, so a protein-restricted diet or separating high-protein meals from dosing may help
  • Enormous first-pass effect: ~95% metabolized in gut wall/liver; only ~1% reaches the brain when given alone ⭐ HY
  • t½ ~1.5h (immediate release); controlled-release and orally-disintegrating forms exist
  • Inhaled levodopa (Inbrija) is a fast-acting rescue formulation for acute intermittent "off" periods

Adverse Effects

Common / Non-selective

  • Nausea/vomiting — tolerance develops over time
  • Orthostatic hypotension (peripheral dopamine → β-receptor vasodilation)
  • Sedation; agitation/delirium/psychosis
  • Nightmares / vivid dreams
  • Cardiac arrhythmia — enhanced in cardiac disease (α/β effects)

The One to Never Miss

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome can be precipitated by abrupt withdrawal of levodopa.

☐ Never Withdraw Abruptly

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome presents with very high fever, irregular/rapid pulse, tachypnea, muscle rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic dysfunction (BP swings), and profuse diaphoresis. Always taper levodopa gradually — never stop it abruptly. Note: NMS can also be precipitated by dopamine receptor blockade (antipsychotics), not only by levodopa withdrawal.

Drug Interactions

  • Anticholinergics — delay gastric emptying, ↓levodopa absorption
  • MAO-A inhibitors (phenelzine) — block dopamine breakdown in the periphery → risk of hypertensive crisis. This is the same mechanism as the tyramine "cheese effect": phenelzine also blocks tyramine metabolism, and tyramine is what actually triggers the crisis with dietary cheese.
  • Antipsychotics — block dopamine receptors centrally, blunting levodopa's effect

Q: Why is phenelzine dangerous in a patient on levodopa, and is this the same mechanism as the classic tyramine "cheese effect"?

Answer

Yes — phenelzine is a non-selective MAO inhibitor. It blocks breakdown of dopamine (from levodopa) in the periphery, risking a hypertensive crisis, via the identical enzyme-inhibition logic that makes it dangerous with dietary tyramine (found in aged cheese): unmetabolized pressor amine accumulates and triggers a hypertensive crisis.

Motor Complications With Long-Term Treatment

Carbidopa/levodopa (Dhivy, Duopa, Rytary, Sinemet, Crexont) is usually effective for the first few years, but motor fluctuations increase over the following 2–5 years. When to start it must be individualized (age, disease severity).

ComplicationWhat It Looks Like
End-of-dose "wearing off"Motor fluctuations timed to the end of each dose interval
"On-off" phenomenonRandom, severe motor fluctuations — unpredictable, not tied to dose timing
Peak-dose dyskinesiaOral/facial writhing, flinging limb movements at peak plasma levels

🔑 High-Yield Recap

⭐ HY Start low, go slow. ⭐ HY Levodopa benefit is typically robust for 2–5 years before motor complications accumulate. ⭐ HY All patients eventually need it regardless of what they start on first.

Extending Levodopa's Reach

COMT Inhibitors

Catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) metabolizes levodopa to 3-O-methyldopa in the gut and liver. Blocking it — like blocking LAAD with carbidopa — increases levodopa's bioavailability (~2×) and half-life, extending its clinical effect.

⚠️ No Effect Alone

COMT inhibitors have zero antiparkinsonian effect on their own. They exist purely to be given with levodopa. Indicated specifically for the "wearing off" phenomenon.

COMT inhibition happens mainly in the periphery — blocking methylation of L-DOPA so more reaches the brain intact

Entacapone (Comtan)

Taken with every dose of levodopa

Also available combined with carbidopa/levodopa as Stalevo. Does not cross the BBB.

Opicapone (Ongentys)

Once-daily add-on

The newest agent (FDA approved 2020). Avoid food within 1 hour of dosing. Does not cross the BBB.

Tolcapone (Tasmar)

Rarely used today

The only one that crosses the BBB (minimal added clinical benefit). Requires LFT monitoring — see boxed warning.

☐ Fatal Hepatotoxicity — Tolcapone Only

Tolcapone carries a risk of fatal hepatotoxicity and requires serial liver function test monitoring. It has been withdrawn from the Canadian market and is rarely used in the U.S. except in patients who fail entacapone.

Class-wide adverse effects: diarrhea, nausea (non-selective), and — for tolcapone and entacapone specifically (not opicapone) — benign brown/orange discoloration of the urine.

🧠 Memory Hook

"3 -capones, 1 bad liver": Tolcapone = Toxic to the liver. It's also the odd one out for BBB crossing and for lacking the harmless orange-urine side effect shared by entacapone/tolcapone (wait — it DOES share that one; it's the liver toxicity and BBB-penetration that set it apart). Rarely first-choice among the three.

Protecting Dopamine Where It's Already Working

Monoamine Oxidase-B Inhibitors

MAO-A

Metabolizes norepinephrine, serotonin, AND dopamine. Non-selective blockade here is what causes the tyramine "cheese effect" and hypertensive crisis with levodopa.

MAO-B

Selectively metabolizes dopamine. Selective MAO-B blockade leaves MAO-A free to still clear tyramine/tryptamine peripherally — no cheese effect.

🧠 Memory Hook — Location, Location, Location

Unlike carbidopa or COMT inhibitors (which act in the periphery to protect levodopa on its way in), MAO-B inhibitors act inside the brain to slow the breakdown of dopamine that's already there. Different job, different location. ⭐ HY

A secondary proposed benefit: MAO metabolism of dopamine generates hydrogen peroxide, a source of oxidative stress. MAO-B inhibitors may have antiapoptotic properties in vitro — but whether this translates into true disease modification in patients remains unproven.

Selegiline (Eldepryl)

Early PD monotherapy or add-on later for "wearing off"

Uniquely metabolized to desmethylselegiline, amphetamine, and methamphetamine — may help patients with levodopa-related drowsiness/fatigue.

Rasagiline (Azilect)

Early and advanced PD

Monotherapy in early PD → less functional decline, better long-term outcomes. Metabolized via CYP1A2 (ciprofloxacin ↑ levels). High-fat meals ↓ absorption. May delay need for levodopa by 9–12 months.

Safinamide (Xadago)

Newer, not first-line

Similar indications/AEs/DIs to selegiline. No proven benefit over the older agents, and costs more.

Class AEs: confusion, hallucinations, dyskinesias, hypotension. Class DIs: serotonergic drugs → risk of serotonin syndrome. ⭐ HY No cheese effect with any of these (selective MAO-B).

Skipping the Conversion Step

Dopamine Receptor Agonists

These drugs activate D2 receptors in the striatum directly — no enzymatic conversion required. Compared with levodopa, they have a longer duration of action with fewer on/off fluctuations. Many experts favor them as first-line in younger patients, delaying the need for levodopa by roughly 3 years. In advanced disease, they're also used as an adjunct to lower the levodopa dose and reduce levodopa-related motor complications. All three non-ergot agonists are also FDA-indicated for restless legs syndrome.

Pramipexole (Mirapex)

D2 & D3 selective

Renally excreted — dose-adjust in renal insufficiency. t½ ~8–12h.

Ropinirole (Requip)

D2 selective

Metabolized by CYP1A2 — fluoroquinolones ↑ levels, omeprazole ↓ levels. t½ ~6h.

Rotigotine (Neupro)

D3 > D2 > D1

Transdermal patch only — no dose adjustment needed for hepatic or renal insufficiency.

Apomorphine (Apokyn — subcutaneous; Kynmobi — sublingual)

Chemically related to morphine but does NOT bind opioid receptors. Profile: D4 > D2 = D3 = D5 > D1, plus α-adrenergic activity. Extensive first-pass metabolism is why it's given SC or sublingually instead of orally.

  • Rapid onset (~10 min), short duration (up to 2h) — a true "rescue therapy" for acute, intermittent freezing/akinesia in advanced PD
  • N/V are common and can be dose-limiting — premedicate with the antiemetic trimethobenzamide, starting 3 days before the first dose and continuing throughout apomorphine use
  • Contraindicated with 5-HT3 receptor blockers (dolasetron, granisetron, ondansetron) — combination risks severe hypotension and syncope
🧠 Memory Hook

Apomorphine sounds like morphine but isn't an opioid — it's a rapid-onset dopamine agonist "rescue shot" for sudden freezing episodes. And remember: you can't rescue nausea with ondansetron here — the 5-HT3 blocker interaction can cause severe hypotension/syncope. Use trimethobenzamide instead.

Bromocriptine (ergot-derived, rarely used)

⚠️ The Ergot Problem

Bromocriptine is an ergot alkaloid — the same chemical class as LSD. It's a non-selective D2 agonist, formerly used for wearing-off and on-off fluctuations. Now rarely used for PD due to potentially life-threatening fibrosis, seizures, CVA, arrhythmias, and MI. Non-ergot agonists are preferred. (Still commonly used for acromegaly, just not PD.)

Class-Wide Adverse Effects & Contraindications

  • Nausea (~50%, by far the most common), confusion, dyskinesias, sedation, vivid dreams, hallucinations, orthostatic hypotension, dry mouth, and sleep attacks
  • CIs: history of psychotic illness, recent MI, active peptic ulcer disease (bromocriptine only)
☐ Impulse Control Disorders

Pramipexole and ropinirole in particular have been linked to intense urges — pathological gambling, compulsive shopping, and hypersexuality. Risk is substantially higher when a dopamine agonist is combined with levodopa. ⭐ HY Counsel every patient started on these agents.

🧠 Memory Hook — Ergot vs. Non-Ergot

Ergot agonists (bromocriptine) = old and risky — think vasospasm, fibrosis, valve damage (same family as LSD). Non-ergot agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine) = modern, D2/D3-selective, and the ones you'll actually reach for.

The Newest Class

Adenosine Receptor Antagonist — Istradefylline

Istradefylline (Nourianz) selectively antagonizes the adenosine A2A receptor. A2A receptors co-localize with D2 receptors in the indirect pathway. Normally, adenosine activation of A2A decreases D2's affinity for dopamine — inhibiting D2 function. By blocking A2A, istradefylline effectively boosts D2 signaling.

  • Given orally, once daily
  • Indicated as an adjunct to levodopa/carbidopa for "off" episodes
  • Better tolerated than COMT inhibitors or dopamine agonists
  • AEs: dyskinesia, dizziness, insomnia. DIs: strong CYP3A4 inhibitors
🧠 Memory Hook

Istradefylline is a caffeine analog. Caffeine is a non-selective adenosine receptor antagonist — this is also the mechanistic thread connecting to the neuroprotection data on caffeine later in this lecture.

⭐ HY Modest effect size: reduces "off" time by less than 1 hour/day versus placebo. No head-to-head trial exists against COMT inhibitors or dopamine agonists, though dyskinesias appear less frequent than with COMT-I.

The Swiss Army Knife

Amantadine (Symmetrel)

Amantadine — an adamantane derivative

Livedo reticularis — a unique amantadine AE

Amantadine has an unusually broad mechanism for a single drug — three separate actions feeding into a modest symptomatic benefit:

📤

↑ Dopamine release

May increase release from surviving neurons

🔄

↓ Dopamine reuptake

May inhibit reuptake, prolonging synaptic action

🧱

NMDA blockade

↓ Excitotoxic glutamate effects

🚫

Anticholinergic

Blocks muscarinic receptors too

🩺 Its Special Job

Amantadine is the go-to adjunct for treating dyskinesias (in addition to motor fluctuations) — a distinct niche most other adjuncts don't fill as directly.

AEs: sedation, confusion, vivid dreams, nausea, dry mouth, hypotension. Unique AE: livedo reticularis (diffuse mottling of the skin) with peripheral edema. Reduce dose in renal dysfunction. Bonus fact: also FDA-indicated for influenza treatment.

🧠 Memory Hook

Amantadine = the Swiss Army knife of PD drugs: boosts dopamine release, blocks reuptake, blocks NMDA/glutamate, and has anticholinergic activity — and it moonlights as an antiviral. One drug, four jobs.

Old, Selective for Tremor

Acetylcholine Receptor Antagonists

Benztropine and trihexyphenidyl are muscarinic antagonists with antihistamine activity. Recall the pathophysiologic rationale from the Circuitry section: loss of dopamine without a matching loss of cholinergic interneurons creates an imbalance — blocking acetylcholine helps restore it.

🔑 Selective for Tremor

⭐ HY Generally less effective than dopaminergic drugs overall, but more effective at reducing tremor than rigidity or bradykinesia. Used as adjunctive therapy; may prolong dopamine's action.

AEs — the classic anticholinergic toxidrome: agitation, confusion, blurred vision, dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, sedation.

  • Contraindicated in glaucoma
  • Poorly tolerated in the elderly — not first-line in that population; better tolerated by younger patients
  • Taper off to discontinue — don't withdraw abruptly

💡 Reading This Diagram

Putting It Into Practice

Treatment Algorithm & Managing Complications

These are approaches, not rigid guidelines — but the pattern below is testable.

🧑

Younger patients (< ~65)

Consider a dopamine agonist or MAO-B inhibitor first — tolerate agonists well, and delaying levodopa is more valuable given a longer disease course ahead. Anticholinergics can help tremor specifically in this group.

🧓

Older / elderly patients (> ~65)

Levodopa/carbidopa is the better initial choice — the most effective drug for symptoms, and the elderly tend to tolerate dopamine agonists poorly (more confusion/hallucinations/hypotension).

A general management approach — mild disease at top, escalating for suboptimal response

Managing Specific Complications

ProblemManagement Approach
Motor fluctuations ("wearing off")↑ dosing frequency of levodopa; add MAO-B-I or COMT-I; add a dopamine agonist
Peak-dose dyskinesia↓ dopaminergic drug dose first; add amantadine
Hallucinations / psychosisEvaluate for another cause first (electrolytes, hypoxemia, infection); D/C anticholinergics → taper amantadine → MAO-B-I → dopamine agonist → COMT-I (in that order); reduce L-DOPA; consider low-dose clozapine or quetiapine (low D2 affinity) if needed
🧠 Memory Hook — Peeling Back the Onion

When psychosis emerges, you remove drugs in reverse order of how "non-essential" they are: anticholinergics first, then amantadine, then MAO-B-I, then dopamine agonist, then COMT-I — and only reduce levodopa last, since it's the most essential drug for motor function. If an antipsychotic becomes necessary, pick one with low D2 affinity (clozapine/quetiapine) so you don't undo everything else.

Q: A 70-year-old newly diagnosed with PD asks about starting a dopamine agonist versus levodopa. What do you recommend, and why?

Answer

Levodopa/carbidopa. At ~70, this patient is past the typical age cutoff (~65) where dopamine agonists are well tolerated — elderly patients have higher rates of confusion, hallucinations, and hypotension on agonists. Levodopa remains the most effective agent for symptom control and is the preferred initial choice in older patients.

Interesting, Not Actionable

Neuroprotection — Caffeine & Nicotine

☕ Epidemiology ≠ Recommendation
  • Caffeine consumption is inversely related to PD risk in epidemiologic studies. Mechanistically, it's an adenosine receptor antagonist that enhances locomotor activity in animal parkinsonism models — the same target as istradefylline.
  • Nicotine shows a consistent inverse association with PD risk in epidemiologic studies; in animals it stimulates striatal dopamine release and may preserve nigral neurons/striatal dopamine in lesioned models.

Neither finding means you should recommend caffeine or, obviously, cigarettes — these are epidemiologic associations, not evidence-based interventions.

Before the Exam

Conclusion & Rapid Review

Parkinson disease is currently incurable. The goal of all pharmacotherapy is to preserve function and quality of life for as long as possible — avoiding or delaying nursing home placement — not to reverse or halt the disease.

🧠 Full Memory Hook Recap
  • Circuitry: Direct (D1) = drive movement. Indirect (D2) = interrupt the interrupter. PD → indirect pathway overactive → thalamus chronically inhibited.
  • Carbidopa/COMT-I: act in the periphery, protect levodopa on the way in. Carbidopa itself never crosses the BBB.
  • MAO-B-I: act in the brain, protect dopamine once it's there. No cheese effect (selective).
  • Apomorphine: sounds like morphine, isn't an opioid — a rescue shot, not maintenance therapy.
  • Ergot vs. non-ergot agonists: ergot (bromocriptine) = old/risky (fibrosis, valve disease); non-ergot = modern first choice.
  • Amantadine: the Swiss Army knife — 4 mechanisms in one drug, and the go-to for dyskinesias.
  • Age >65: start levodopa. Age <65: consider a dopamine agonist or MAO-B-I first.

Q: Which two drug classes have zero antiparkinsonian effect when given without levodopa?

Answer

COMT inhibitors and — indirectly — carbidopa itself (as a standalone, it has no dopaminergic activity of its own; its entire job is protecting levodopa). Both exist purely as levodopa "helpers."

🩺 Non-Motor Symptoms — Don't Forget These
  • Cognitive dysfunction/dementia; psychosis (hallucinations, delusions)
  • Mood — depression, anxiety, apathy
  • Sleep disturbance, fatigue; olfactory dysfunction (often an early, pre-motor clue)
  • Autonomic dysfunction — orthostatic hypotension in up to 60% of PD patients
🚫 Goals of Treatment — What Therapy Can't Do

Every drug in this lecture is symptomatic management only — none reverse the disease or halt its progression. Goals: improve motor/non-motor symptoms, preserve function (ADLs) for a period of years, improve quality of life, minimize AEs.

🧠 Memory Hook — Where Each Class Works

Carbidopa & COMT-I act in the periphery (protect levodopa on the way in). MAO-B-I act in the brain (protect dopamine once it's there). Dopamine agonists skip the conversion step and hit the receptor directly. Carbidopa's one job: it never enters the brain — if it did, it would block the LAAD activity the brain needs for dopamine synthesis.

☕ FYI — Neuroprotection (Not Actionable)

Caffeine and nicotine both show an inverse epidemiologic association with PD risk (caffeine is an A2A antagonist — same target as istradefylline). These are associations from observational studies, not evidence-based recommendations — don't prescribe coffee or cigarettes.

🧠 Memory Hook — Wearing-Off vs. On-Off

Wearing-off = predictable, timed to the end of the dose interval (fix: shorten intervals or add an adjunct). On-off = random and severe, not tied to timing (needs apomorphine rescue for acute episodes). Same toolkit treats both — the difference is predictability, not severity.

  • All patients eventually need levodopa — the question is only ever when to start it. Benefit is typically robust for 2–5 years before motor complications accumulate. Start low, go slow.
  • Never withdraw levodopa abruptly — risk of neuroleptic malignant syndrome (also triggerable by dopamine-receptor-blocking antipsychotics).
  • Impulse control disorders (gambling, compulsive shopping, hypersexuality) — counsel every patient started on a dopamine agonist, especially combined with levodopa.
  • Apomorphine ≠ opioid despite the name resemblance to morphine — it's a rescue-therapy dopamine agonist.
  • Non-ergot agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine) are preferred; ergot agonists (bromocriptine) carry fibrosis/valvulopathy risk and are rarely used for PD today.
