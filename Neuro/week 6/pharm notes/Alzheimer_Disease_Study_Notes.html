Background

Alzheimer Disease — The Basics

Alzheimer disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia — a progressive, ultimately fatal neurodegenerative disorder. ⭐ HY

🚫 No Cure Exists

Keep this in mind through every section below — nothing in this lecture cures, halts, or reverses AD. All of it is symptom management.

Why Does This Happen?

Risk Factors

🧬

Genetic Susceptibility

  • Early onset — chromosomes 1, 14, 21
  • Late onset — apolipoprotein E gene (APOE*4)
🌍

Other Contributors

  • Increasing age — the single biggest risk factor
  • Reduced brain reserve capacity (small brain size, low educational attainment, reduced mental/physical activity)
  • Head injury, Down syndrome, depression, vascular disease
🔑 APOE*4 & Amyloid

The APOE*4 mutation leads to accelerated deposition of amyloid — directly linking this risk factor to the pathophysiology you're about to see. ⭐ HY

The Foundation for Every Drug Target

Pathophysiology

Two microscopic hallmarks define the AD brain:

🧩

Amyloid Plaques

Form between neurons — composed of β-amyloid polypeptides.

🕸️

Neurofibrillary Tangles

Form within neurons — made of hyperphosphorylated tau protein. Filament density correlates directly with disease severity.

The amyloid cascade — from APP cleavage to tau tangles, inflammation, and eventual neuron death (N Engl J Med 2004)

💡 Reading the Cascade

Amyloid precursor protein (APP) can be cleaved by α-, β-, or γ-secretase. The amyloidogenic pathway (β- then γ-secretase) generates Aβ, which aggregates into plaques and triggers a cascade: tau hyperphosphorylation → neurofibrillary tangles, plus inflammation, excitotoxicity, and oxidation — all converging on neuronal cell death, neurotransmitter deficits, and the cognitive/behavioral abnormalities you see clinically.

🧠 Memory Hook — 3 Hypotheses, 3 Drug Classes

Every drug class in this lecture maps directly onto one of these three hypotheses. If you know the hypothesis, you already know why the drug class exists: Amyloid → monoclonal antibodies. Cholinergic loss → cholinesterase inhibitors. Glutamate excitotoxicity → memantine. ⭐ HY

🆚 Contrast With Parkinson Disease

AD is not primarily a dopaminergic disease. The key neurotransmitter losses are acetylcholine, serotonin, and norepinephrine — a very different profile from Parkinson disease's relatively selective loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra.

Other contributing processes: inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and brain vascular disease all play supporting roles in the neurodegenerative process.

What You're Treating

Clinical Presentation

🧠

Cognitive Symptoms

  • Memory loss (amnesia)
  • Aphasia — language
  • Apraxia — movement
  • Agnosia — recognition
  • Disorientation
  • Impaired executive function
😔

Noncognitive Symptoms

  • Depression
  • Psychotic symptoms — hallucinations, delusions
  • Behavioral disturbances — physical/verbal aggression, wandering
  • Inability to care for self / perform ADLs (dressing, bathing, toileting, eating)
🧠 Memory Hook — The 4 A's

The classic cognitive-symptom mnemonic: Amnesia, Aphasia, Apraxia, Agnosia — all four are named in this lecture's cognitive symptom list (plus disorientation and executive dysfunction on top).

Recognize This on a Vignette

Staging Severity — The Mini-Mental State Exam

⭐ HY The MMSE is scored 0–30 and is the tool used throughout this lecture to determine which drug is appropriate. You should be able to look at a score in a clinical vignette and instantly classify severity — the treatment algorithm downstream depends on it.

StageMMSE ScoreClinical Picture
Mild21–26Difficulty remembering recent events; declining ability to manage finances, prepare food, and carry out other household activities; may get lost while driving; begins withdrawing from difficult tasks/hobbies; may deny memory problems.
Moderate10–20Requires assistance with ADLs; frequently disoriented to time; recall of recent events severely impaired; may forget details of past events and names of family/friends; function fluctuates day to day; generally denies problems; may become suspicious or tearful; loses ability to drive safely; agitation, paranoia, delusions common.
Severe0–9Loses ability to speak, walk, and feed self; incontinent of urine and feces; requires 24/7 care.
🔑 Why Staging Matters Clinically

The MMSE score determines drug selection: cholinesterase inhibitors are used for mild-to-moderate AD; memantine is reserved for moderate-to-severe AD. Get comfortable converting a vignette's described deficits into a rough MMSE band.

Q: A patient can no longer manage her finances or prepare meals independently but still recognizes her family and denies any memory problems. What stage of AD is this, and what MMSE range would you expect?

Answer

Mild AD, MMSE ~21–26. Difficulty with finances/food prep plus denial of memory problems are classic mild-stage features — family recognition and preserved recent-event recall (relatively) are still intact.

Before Reaching for a Prescription

Nonpharmacologic Interventions

These become especially important as the disease progresses — and given how modest the pharmacologic benefits turn out to be (see Evidence of Effectiveness below), they carry real clinical weight.

  • Identify and minimize causes of problematic behaviors
  • Maintain a stable, low-stress environment; avoid noise and glare
  • Provide security objects
  • Promote daytime activity to improve nighttime sleep
  • Individualized exercise programs may slow the progression of dementia — genuinely evidence-supported, unlike some of the pharmacologic "other therapies" you'll see later
  • Caregiver support groups and stress management are important

Framing Every Drug That Follows

Goals of Pharmacotherapy

Desired Outcomes

  • Preserve patient function for as long as possible — avoid/delay nursing home placement
  • Treat psychiatric and behavioral sequelae
🚫 What Current Drugs Do NOT Do

⭐ HY Current treatments do not prolong life, cure AD, or reverse the underlying pathophysiologic process. Even "halt" is questionable — the newest anti-amyloid antibodies are designed with halting in mind, but the clinical data don't clearly support it (more on this in the Anti-Amyloid section).

Targeting the Cholinergic Hypothesis

Cholinesterase Inhibitors

Donepezil (Aricept, Adlarity), rivastigmine (Exelon), and galantamine (Razadyne) all inhibit the degradation of acetylcholine within the synapse, prolonging cholinergic receptor activation. Indicated for mild-to-moderate AD. All three are reversible inhibitors — a distinction worth remembering.

🧠 Memory Hook — Reversible vs. Irreversible

All three cholinesterase inhibitors used for AD are reversible. The classic irreversible cholinesterase inhibitors are organophosphates and nerve gases — those are poisons, not therapeutics. If a question describes an irreversible AChE inhibitor, it's not talking about an AD drug.

Donepezil (Aricept)

Piperidine derivative

Selective, reversible AChE inhibitor binding the peripheral anionic site. Metabolized by CYP2D6 & 3A4. t½ ~70h.

Rivastigmine (Exelon)

Carbamate, non-selective

Slow-reversible; inhibits both AChE and BuChE, binding the esteratic site. Hydrolyzed in plasma (not CYP-dependent). t½ ~1.5h (~10h in CNS).

Galantamine (Razadyne)

Selective, competitive, rapidly-reversible

Interacts with the anionic (choline-binding) subsite. May also positively modulate nicotinic receptors allosterically. Metabolized by CYP2D6 & 3A4. t½ ~7h.

⚠️ Class-Wide Adverse Effects

Nausea, anorexia, vomiting, diarrhea — start low, go slow. (Tacrine caused hepatotoxicity and is no longer actively marketed.) Also watch for bradycardia and syncope — a cholinergic class effect that's easy to forget.

Drug Interactions

Anticholinergic agents work directly against the mechanism you're trying to achieve — they may decrease the clinical benefit of cholinesterase inhibitors:

  • Diphenhydramine
  • Tolterodine, oxybutynin (for urinary incontinence — common in this population, and the gold standard for that indication, so watch for this combination)
  • Tricyclic antidepressants (imipramine, amitriptyline — often prescribed for comorbid depression or diabetic peripheral neuropathy in this population)
🧠 Memory Hook

These interacting drugs aren't obscure — they're things AD patients are commonly already taking (urinary incontinence meds, TCAs for depression/neuropathy). Always screen the med list for anticholinergic burden before starting a cholinesterase inhibitor.

Targeting the Glutamate Hypothesis

NMDA Receptor Blocker — Memantine (Namenda)

MOA: N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist — an uncompetitive channel blocker that binds within the channel pore rather than at a distinct allosteric site. Blocks the excitotoxic effects of glutamate. Indicated for moderate-to-severe AD.

  • PK: 100% bioavailable, low protein binding, excreted unchanged in urine, t½ ~60–80h
  • AEs: dizziness, confusion, hallucinations, delusions (ironic, given the drug is meant to help cognition)
  • Reduce dose if CrCl < 30 mL/min
  • No clinically significant drug interactions — a nice contrast to the cholinesterase inhibitors' anticholinergic-interaction problem
🔑 Mild-to-Moderate vs. Moderate-to-Severe

⭐ HY Cholinesterase inhibitors → mild-to-moderate AD. Memantine → moderate-to-severe AD. There's deliberate overlap at "moderate" — combination therapy is an option once a patient progresses to moderate-to-severe while already on a cholinesterase inhibitor.

The Uncomfortable Truth

Evidence of Effectiveness

🔑 The Theme of This Entire Section

⭐ HY Statistically significant ≠ clinically meaningful. Hold onto this idea — it's the thread running through every number below.

Cholinesterase Inhibitors

  • Average improvement on the 70-point ADAS-Cog scale is only −2.7 points (95% CI −3.0 to −2.3, p<0.00001) — statistically rock-solid, but a tiny fraction of the scale
  • More patients discontinue ChEI treatment due to adverse events (29%) than discontinue placebo (18%)

NMDA Receptor Blocker (Memantine)

Benefit demonstrated after six months:

  • Cognition: 2.97 points on the 100-point SIB scale (95% CI 1.68–4.26, P<0.00001)
  • ADLs: 1.27 points on the 54-point ADCS-ADLsev scale (95% CI 0.44–2.09, P=0.003)
  • Behavior: 2.76 points on the 144-point NPI scale (95% CI 0.88–4.63, P=0.004)

Combination therapy (ChEI + memantine) is statistically better still — but the incremental clinical benefit is marginal and it's very costly.

🩺 A Useful Clinical Benchmark

The average untreated patient declines 2–4 MMSE points per year. A treatment response that keeps decline under 2 points/year is considered clinically meaningful. ⭐ HY This gives you a concrete way to judge "is this drug actually helping" in a given patient, beyond trial statistics.

Practical Recommendations

Mild-to-moderate ADCholinesterase inhibitor

💬 Setting Expectations

Patients — and especially their family, friends, and caregivers — need realistic expectations. These drugs are not a miraculous turnaround. The goal is modestly slowing cognitive decline and delaying nursing home placement, not curing or reversing the disease.

Reasonable Theories That Didn't Pan Out

Other Therapies

Vitamin E

MOA: antioxidant, scavenges toxic free radicals. Three double-blind RCTs found no convincing benefit in AD or MCI.

Why Not Just Try It?

Doses ≥400 IU/day for a year or more may increase risk of death in both healthy people and those with chronic disease, and may increase bleeding/hemorrhagic stroke risk.

⚠️ NSAIDs — Also No Benefit

The inflammation seen in AD pathophysiology made NSAIDs a reasonable theoretical target — but prospective clinical trials showed no benefit, while carrying real risk of gastritis and GI bleeding. Not recommended.

🧠 Memory Hook

Both vitamin E and NSAIDs are cautionary tales: a biologically plausible mechanism (antioxidant / anti-inflammatory) does not guarantee clinical benefit — and both carry real bleeding risk that can outweigh a theoretical upside that never materialized in trials.

Targeting the Cascade Directly

Anti-Amyloid Monoclonal Antibodies

Donanemab (Kisunla) and lecanemab (Leqembi) are IgG monoclonal antibodies against soluble and insoluble amyloid beta — they reduce amyloid plaque burden.

☐ Adverse Effects to Know

Hemosiderosis (iron deposit accumulation), microhemorrhage, brain edema, headache, and increased risk of falling.

⚠️ A Controversial Approval History

Aducanumab — the original anti-amyloid antibody — had a controversial path to approval: the Peripheral and Central Nervous System Drugs Advisory Committee voted 8 to 1 against approving it, citing insufficient evidence of efficacy. The FDA approved it anyway. It has since been removed from the U.S. market. Donanemab and lecanemab followed with more scrutiny, but questions about clinically meaningful benefit persist for the class.

🔑 What These Drugs Do NOT Do

⭐ HY Despite targeting the disease mechanism directly, there is no direct head-to-head comparison between donanemab and lecanemab, and neither drug stops or reverses cognitive decline. Donanemab may carry more serious AEs but is dosed less frequently and can be discontinued once amyloid PET imaging shows minimal plaque burden.

Often the Real Reason for Nursing Home Placement

Treatment of Noncognitive Symptoms

Psychosis, disruptive/inappropriate behavior, and depression increase caregiver burden more than cognitive loss or self-care decline themselves — and are the leading cause of nursing home placement. ⭐ HY Always try environmental interventions before reaching for a prescription.

🧠 Memory Hook

Avoid tricyclic antidepressants for depression in this population — their anticholinergic effects can be counterproductive, especially in a patient already on a cholinesterase inhibitor (the exact drug interaction covered earlier). SSRIs are the safer antidepressant choice here.

☐ Boxed Warning — Increased Mortality

Elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis treated with antipsychotic drugs are at increased risk of death. This applies to the entire antipsychotic class — atypical and typical. Use must be judicious, weighing risk against benefit, and treatment should be for the shortest duration necessary — not continuous.

Brexpiprazole (Rexulti)

The only drug FDA-approved specifically for agitation in Alzheimer dementia. MOA: serotonin 5-HT1A and dopamine D2/D3 receptor partial agonist — more antagonist-leaning activity than aripiprazole at comparable receptors. Also FDA-approved for schizophrenia and as an adjunct for major depression.

  • AEs: weight gain, akathisia, sedation
  • No clear evidence of superior efficacy or safety versus aripiprazole — yet costs $1,400/month versus aripiprazole's $15/month ⭐ HY
  • Still carries the boxed warning for increased mortality in older dementia patients
🩺 The Off-Label Alternative

Other atypical antipsychotics — aripiprazole, risperidone, olanzapine — are used off-label for AD agitation and have shown efficacy, in some cases comparable to brexpiprazole, at a fraction of the cost. Know that brexpiprazole is the only on-label choice, even though it may not be the only reasonable choice.

Q: Why might a clinician choose aripiprazole over brexpiprazole for agitation in an AD patient, despite brexpiprazole being the FDA-approved option?

Answer

Cost and comparable evidence. Aripiprazole costs roughly $15/month versus brexpiprazole's ~$1,400/month, and there's no clear evidence that brexpiprazole is more effective or safer. Both still carry the class boxed warning for increased mortality in elderly dementia-related psychosis.

Before the Exam

Conclusion & Rapid Review

Alzheimer disease is progressive and incurable. Every drug in this lecture manages symptoms or comorbid behaviors — none of them prolong life, cure the disease, or reverse its underlying pathology.

🧠 Full Memory Hook Recap
  • 3 hypotheses, 3 targets: amyloid → mAbs; cholinergic loss → ChEIs; glutamate excitotoxicity → memantine.
  • 4 A's: Amnesia, Aphasia, Apraxia, Agnosia.
  • MMSE thirds: mild 21–26, moderate 10–20, severe 0–9 — ChEIs for mild-moderate, memantine for moderate-severe.
  • Statistically significant ≠ clinically meaningful — the theme of the entire evidence base for these drugs.
  • <2 MMSE points/year decline = a clinically meaningful treatment response (versus an untreated 2–4 pt/year decline).
  • Boxed warning: any antipsychotic in an elderly dementia patient with psychosis carries increased mortality risk — brexpiprazole is on-label, but not proven superior to cheaper off-label options.

Q: A patient with moderate-to-severe AD is already on donepezil. Her MMSE has declined 3 points this year. What would you consider adding, and what benchmark tells you whether it's working?

Answer

Add memantine (combination therapy, appropriate now that she's moderate-to-severe). Judge response by MMSE decline rate going forward — keeping decline under 2 points/year would be considered a clinically meaningful benefit, versus the ~2–4 point/year decline expected without treatment.
