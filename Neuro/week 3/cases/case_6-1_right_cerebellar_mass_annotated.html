Case 6-1 — Right Cerebellar Hemisphere Mass

36-year-old attorney · gait imbalance, headaches, vomiting · working differential: right cerebellar hemisphere neoplasm with obstructive hydrocephalus

Chief Complaint

A 36-year-old attorney was referred with a chief complaint of gait imbalance, headaches and vomiting1.

History of Chief Complaint

The patient had had frontal and biparietal headaches for approximately one month. He stated that the headaches began in the occipital region2 approximately two months ago and about a month ago became frontal as well. Coughing, sneezing, or bending precipitated or worsened the headaches3. On at least one occasion the patient had been awakened by a headache4. He experienced nausea and vomiting independent of the headaches. At times, vomiting occurred suddenly without preceding nausea5. For several weeks prior to admission he had been unsteady on his feet with a tendency to fall to the right6. He admitted to occasional clumsiness of his right hand7. During the week prior to admission he had been excessively drowsy and constantly tired8. The headaches had become more frequent, so that they were now occurring three to four times per day9.

Medical History

His medical history was unremarkable except for an episode of rheumatic fever at age 610.

Family History

He was unmarried, lived alone, and was employed by a large law firm. His parents were alive and in good health; he had no siblings.

Social History

He professed to be heterosexual and was involved in a monogamous relationship that had lasted for several years. He had a 10-pack-year history of smoking, but had quit 6 years previously. He had been active in outdoor sports since age 31 and had been running up to 4 miles daily until 4 weeks before, when he had to quit because of an unsteady gait and worsening headaches.

General Physical Examination

This is a stable male who is seated upright on an examination table. He is awake, oriented, well-nourished, well-hydrated, and distressed with considerable head pain. He appears his stated age and is in otherwise good physical condition. His eyes have no cotton wool patches, but papilledema is evident11. His chest is clear to auscultation and percussion; his abdomen is soft without lumps or masses. Blood pressure is 135/87 and pulse rate is 69. Temperature and respirations are normal. Peripheral pulses are intact; no extremity edema is present; no cervical, axillary, or inguinal lymphadenopathy is detectable.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

He is awake and oriented with respect to person, place, and time. His memory and knowledge base are appropriate for his training. Speech is fluent and meaningful. He is a coherent historian.

Cranial Nerves

A full range of eye movements are present. His pupils are 3 to 4 mm in diameter and reactive to light, both direct and consensual. On funduscopic examination there is evidence of bilateral papilledema. Horizontal nystagmus in the direction of gaze is present on attempted lateral gaze to either side12. He denies any double vision13. Hearing is normal and equal in both ears. Corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes are intact. Facial expressions are full and symmetric. Uvula and tongue protrude on the midline. Shoulder shrug is symmetric.

Motor Exam

His strength is intact in all extremities and his deep tendon reflexes are symmetric and physiologic. His plantar responses are flexor20. When walking, he maintains a slightly widened base and there is a mild tendency to veer to the right14. He is unable to stand on his right leg comfortably15. Closing his eyes does not markedly alter the instability on his right leg16. He can stand comfortably on his left leg with his eyes open or shut. A very mild end-point tremor is present in the right upper extremity on finger-to-nose testing, and in the right lower extremity on heel-to-shin testing17. Alternating movements of the right hand are slightly slower, and with prolonged periods become disorganized18.

Sensory Exam

Pinprick, two-point discrimination, vibratory sense, and proprioception are intact throughout body and face19.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Condensed localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

1 · Localization

Right cerebellar hemisphere (lateral zone)

All of the localizing signs are unilateral and appendicular: right-sided fall/veer, right hand clumsiness, right finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin dysmetria, and right-hand dysdiadochokinesia, with a negative Romberg. Strength, reflexes, plantar responses, and full sensory testing are normal, arguing against corticospinal or dorsal-column involvement. Mental status, language, and visual fields are intact, arguing against a suprasegmental (cortical/subcortical) process. This is a segmental, infratentorial picture centered on the right cerebellar hemisphere (dentate/lateral zone), not a hemispheric or spinal cord lesion.

2 · Distribution

Focal, with secondary generalized effects

The neurologic deficit itself is focal — confined to the right cerebellar hemisphere. The bilateral papilledema, progressively frequent and positional headaches, and drowsiness are not separate lesions; they reflect generalized elevation of intracranial pressure, most likely from the mass obstructing CSF outflow at or near the fourth ventricle (obstructive hydrocephalus) rather than a second, independent process.

3 · Time Course

Subacute, chronically progressive

Symptoms evolved over roughly two months — occipital headache first, then frontal spread, then gait change, then limb clumsiness, then drowsiness — with escalating headache frequency in the final week. This step-wise worsening over weeks is a chronic-progressive course, not the abrupt-onset pattern expected of a vascular event, and not a relapsing-remitting (fluctuant) course.

4 · Differential Diagnosis
DiagnosisFitsArgues Against
Right cerebellar hemisphere glioma/astrocytoma Most Likely Subacute progressive unilateral hemisphere signs; slowly rising ICP; age compatible with a low- to intermediate-grade glial tumor Histology can't be confirmed without tissue — imaging and biopsy still needed
Hemangioblastoma Classic cerebellar hemisphere location; can produce mass effect and hydrocephalus Usually seen with von Hippel-Lindau stigmata or family history, neither reported here
Metastatic tumor Can present as a new focal cerebellar lesion with raised ICP No known primary malignancy or systemic symptoms reported
Medulloblastoma Posterior fossa mass with obstructive hydrocephalus Classically a midline/vermian pediatric tumor causing truncal, not lateralized limb, ataxia — poor fit for a 36-year-old with unilateral appendicular signs
Cerebellar abscess Can cause a focal cerebellar mass effect No fever, no infectious source, no systemic signs of infection
Cerebellar infarct/hemorrhage Can produce unilateral cerebellar signs Two-month step-wise progression is atypical for a vascular event, which is classically abrupt in onset
5 · Diagnostic Workup
  1. MRI brain, with and without contrast.Characterizes the posterior fossa mass, its enhancement pattern, and confirms/quantifies any hydrocephalus.
  2. Non-contrast CT headif MRI is not immediately available — quick assessment of ventricular size and mass effect before anything else is done.
  3. Avoid lumbar puncture.A posterior fossa mass with obstructive hydrocephalus carries real risk of tonsillar or upward herniation if CSF pressure is released from below.
  4. Neurosurgical consultation.For biopsy/resection planning and, if needed, CSF diversion (external ventricular drain, endoscopic third ventriculostomy, or shunt).
  5. Histopathology of resected/biopsied tissue.Establishes the definitive diagnosis and grade, which drives any adjuvant treatment decisions.
6 · Prognosis

Histology-dependent

Well-circumscribed, lower-grade lesions (e.g., pilocytic astrocytoma, hemangioblastoma) carry an excellent prognosis with gross total resection. Higher-grade gliomas or metastatic disease carry a more guarded prognosis. The hydrocephalus and its symptoms are expected to improve once the obstructing mass is addressed and/or CSF diversion is established.

7 · Management
  • Close neurologic monitoring for signs of decompensation while awaiting definitive care
  • Head-of-bed elevation; avoid straining/Valsalva maneuvers that further raise ICP
  • Corticosteroids (e.g., dexamethasone) if significant peritumoral edema is present
  • Urgent neurosurgical evaluation for resection and/or CSF diversion
  • Post-operative physical/occupational therapy for gait and coordination
  • Long-term surveillance imaging and oncologic follow-up guided by final histopathology

Concept Deep-Dives

The underlying neuroanatomy this case is really testing

Localization

Cerebellar Zones & the Two Motor Systems

This patient: right-sided appendicular ataxia and dysmetria localize to the right lateral zone → dentate nucleus → lateral motor system pathway, which governs distal limb coordination — not the median zone that would instead produce truncal/gait-only signs.

Localization

Why Ipsilateral? The Double-Cross Circuit

This patient: a lesion in the right cerebellar hemisphere disrupts output that ultimately reaches the right limbs — the superior cerebellar peduncle crosses once, and the descending rubrospinal/corticospinal tracts cross back a second time, so cerebellar signs are always ipsilateral to the lesion.

This patient: instability on the right leg was unchanged by closing the eyes, and vibratory/proprioceptive sense was fully intact — both point away from a dorsal-column problem and confirm the ataxia is cerebellar.

Exam Finding

Raised ICP from a Posterior Fossa Mass

This patient: the mass narrows the fourth ventricle's CSF outflow, producing obstructive hydrocephalus — which explains the Valsalva-worsened and nocturnal headaches, the sudden vomiting without nausea, and the bilateral papilledema, all as one mechanism rather than three separate problems.

Terminology

Dysmetria & Dysdiadochokinesia

This patient: the end-point tremor on finger-to-nose/heel-to-shin testing is dysmetria, and the slowed, progressively disorganized rapid alternating hand movements are dysdiadochokinesia — both isolated to the right side, both classic cerebellar hemisphere signs.

Cerebellar Surface Anatomy

This patient: the vermis (highlighted) runs down the midline between the two hemispheres — it's the structure that stays intact here, while the lateral hemisphere off to one side is where this patient's lesion sits. Keeping the vermis/hemisphere boundary visually clear is what makes the hemisphere-vs-vermis distinction in Card 3 click.

This patient: this is the reference table this case was built to match — ataxia predominantly involving the limbs rather than the trunk, localizing to the neocerebellar (lateral) hemisphere, arising here from a suspected neoplasm rather than infarction or abscess.
