Case 6-2 — Pediatric Midline Cerebellar Mass

8-year-old girl · truncal ataxia, nausea/vomiting, irritability · working differential: medulloblastoma (vermis) with obstructive hydrocephalus

Chief Complaint

An 8-year-old girl was brought to her family physician by her mother because the child has become "unsteady" when walking.

History of Chief Complaint

Seven months earlier, she had begun complaining of nausea frequently1. She is still complaining of nausea, and is also vomiting frequently2. In the past 3 months, the child had begun walking unsteadily with a broad-based gait3. When seated, she swayed from side to side and occasionally fell over4. Her head rotated from side to side as her body swayed5. The mother also noted that the child had recently become extremely irritable6.

Developmental and Medical History

The mother had had an uneventful pregnancy and delivery. At birth the child weighed 3.25 kg; by 5.5 months she could maintain a seated, upright posture, and she could roll over at 6 months of age. She could rise to a standing position and take several steps by 15 months, and could climb stairs by 20 months. By 3 years of age she could stand on one foot, unassisted. For the next 4 years she was active in outdoor play7. All of her immunizations were current20.

Family History

The mother was a social worker for the state and a single parent; the father was remarried and living out of state. The child's maternal and paternal grandparents were in good health. The mother had a 12-pack-year history of smoking and smoked through the pregnancy8; she denied any use of alcohol either at this time or during the pregnancy. No one else in her family had similar or related symptoms9.

General Physical Examination

This was an awake, oriented child who was complaining of headaches and dizziness. Face and personality appeared the stated age, but she was underweight and lacked appropriate muscle mass10. Edges of optic discs were blurred, but lacked cotton wool patches or papilledema11. Nystagmus was evident on lateral gaze to either side12. Her chest and abdomen were normal; blood pressure, pulse rate, temperature, and respirations were physiologic. Peripheral pulses were intact, with normal tissue turgor, and no cervical, axillary, or inguinal lymphadenopathy was detected.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

The child was awake and oriented with respect to time and place. Memory and knowledge were appropriate for her age. However, response time to questions was protracted and she appeared preoccupied with her headache pain13.

Cranial Nerves

She had a full range of eye and facial movements. However, nystagmus was present on horizontal gaze to either side19. Hearing was normal in both ears. Corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes were normal. The facial expressions were complete, the eyes were closed tightly, and the forehead was wrinkled symmetrically when frowning. Uvula and tongue protruded on the midline.

Motor Exam

Strength was normal in all extremities; deep tendon reflexes were physiologic in all extremities14. In the seated position, the child swayed from side to side and could not maintain her torso in a vertical position15. Her gait was broad-based and reeling16. Finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin testing were grossly abnormal unless her torso was supported in a vertical position17.

Sensory Exam

Pinprick, thermal, vibratory, two-point discrimination, and proprioceptive senses were normal throughout the body and face18.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Condensed localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

1 · Localization

Midline cerebellum (vermis / flocculonodular lobe)

The dominant signs here are axial: swaying while simply seated, titubation, and a broad-based, non-lateralized gait. Critically, limb testing (finger-to-nose, heel-to-shin) only looks abnormal when the trunk is unsupported — once the torso is stabilized, appendicular coordination is preserved. That combination localizes to the median zone/vermis and flocculonodular lobe, not a cerebellar hemisphere. Normal strength, reflexes, and sensory exam rule out corticospinal or dorsal-column involvement; preserved orientation and memory argue against a suprasegmental process. This is segmental/infratentorial, just like Case 6-1 — but midline rather than lateral.

2 · Distribution

Focal, with secondary generalized effects

The primary lesion is focal to the midline cerebellum. Nausea, vomiting, blurred disc margins, and irritability are not separate disease processes — they reflect early, evolving increased intracranial pressure from CSF outflow obstruction at the fourth ventricle, the same mechanism as Case 6-1, just caught at an earlier stage (blurred margins rather than frank papilledema).

3 · Time Course

Chronic, progressive — slower than Case 6-1

Nausea began seven months before presentation, with truncal ataxia only emerging in the last three months and irritability most recently. This step-wise, several-month evolution is a chronic-progressive course, consistent with a slow-growing midline tumor — not an acute event and not a fluctuating one.

4 · Differential Diagnosis
DiagnosisFitsArgues Against
Medulloblastoma Most Likely Classic midline/vermian pediatric tumor arising near the roof of the fourth ventricle; peak incidence 5–9 years; truncal ataxia plus early hydrocephalus fits well Histology and molecular subtype can't be confirmed without tissue — imaging and biopsy still needed
Pilocytic astrocytoma Most common pediatric posterior fossa tumor overall; can arise in or near the vermis; typically indolent Usually an even slower, more indolent course than described here
Ependymoma Arises from the fourth ventricle floor; can cause truncal ataxia with obstructive hydrocephalus in young children Often produces more prominent lower cranial nerve signs from floor infiltration than seen here
Brainstem glioma Posterior fossa tumor of childhood Would typically show more cranial nerve palsies and long-tract (motor/sensory) signs rather than an isolated truncal-ataxia picture
Acute/post-infectious cerebellitis A recognized cause of pediatric truncal ataxia Classic course is acute (days), self-limited; a 7-month progressive prodrome with signs of rising ICP is a poor fit
5 · Diagnostic Workup
  1. MRI brain with contrast.Characterizes the posterior fossa/fourth-ventricle mass and quantifies hydrocephalus.
  2. MRI of the entire spine, with contrast.Medulloblastoma can seed the CSF (drop metastases), so the whole neuraxis needs to be staged, ideally before surgery.
  3. Non-contrast CT headif MRI is not immediately available — quick assessment of ventricular size before anything else is done.
  4. Avoid lumbar puncture before imaging.Same herniation risk as Case 6-1 with an obstructive posterior fossa mass; CSF cytology for staging is deferred until it's safe to obtain, typically post-operatively.
  5. Neurosurgical consultation.For maximal safe resection and, if needed, CSF diversion (ETV or shunt) for the hydrocephalus.
  6. Histopathology and molecular subtyping.Modern medulloblastoma classification (WNT, SHH, Group 3, Group 4) drives risk-adapted therapy decisions.
6 · Prognosis

Subtype- and stage-dependent

Medulloblastoma prognosis is risk-stratified by age, extent of resection, presence of metastatic spread, and molecular subgroup — WNT-subgroup tumors carry the best prognosis, Group 3 tumors the worst, with modern multimodal therapy substantially improving overall survival compared to surgery alone. Pilocytic astrocytoma, if that's the eventual diagnosis instead, carries an excellent prognosis with resection.

7 · Management
  • Nutritional support given documented weight loss and reduced muscle mass
  • CSF diversion (pre- or intra-operative) if hydrocephalus is significant
  • Maximal safe neurosurgical resection
  • Risk-adapted adjuvant craniospinal radiation and/or chemotherapy (radiation is limited or avoided in very young children to protect neurocognitive development)
  • Physical/occupational therapy for truncal control and gait rehabilitation
  • Long-term surveillance imaging, and neurocognitive/endocrine follow-up given the child's age

This patient: truncal swaying, titubation, and a non-lateralized gait — with limb testing that normalizes once the trunk is supported — is the mirror image of Case 6-1's right-sided limb ataxia, and it's how you tell median-zone from lateral-zone disease at the bedside.

Pattern Recognition

Why Trunk Support "Fixes" the Limb Exam

This patient: the vermis/median-zone circuit (left) governs axial posture, not limb precision — so when this circuit fails, the trunk wobbles and drags the limb exam down with it. Supporting the torso removes that confound and reveals that the lateral-zone/limb circuit (right) is comparatively intact.

Terminology

Titubation & the Vestibulocerebellum

This patient: the flocculonodular lobe (vestibulocerebellum) links directly to the vestibular nuclei, which stabilize head and trunk position — disruption here produces the rhythmic head/trunk oscillation described as titubation, alongside the truncal instability and gaze-evoked nystagmus also seen on this exam.

Exam Finding

Early vs. Late Papilledema

This patient: blurred disc margins without hemorrhages or cotton-wool spots represent early papilledema — an earlier point on the same continuum as the fully developed bilateral papilledema in Case 6-1 — still evidence of rising ICP, just caught sooner.

Localization

Fastigial Nucleus & Axial Control

This patient: a vermian mass presses on median-zone output to the fastigial nucleus, which normally drives the vestibulospinal and reticulospinal tracts controlling truncal and neck posture — disrupt this circuit and you get exactly what's on this exam: seated swaying, titubation, and a wide-based, reeling gait.

This patient: this is the reference table this case was built to match — truncal (not limb) ataxia from midline vermis involvement, plus nystagmus and titubation-like head rotation from flocculonodular lobe involvement, arising from a midline tumor (medulloblastoma) growing into the caudal vermis — exactly the leading diagnosis reached in the differential above.

This patient: the highlighted region is exactly the structure a caudal-vermis medulloblastoma compresses or invades — producing the bilateral, non-lateralized truncal ataxia syndrome seen throughout this case, in contrast to Case 6-1's one-sided lateral hemisphere pattern.
