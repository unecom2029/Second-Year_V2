Case 6-3 — Left Superior Cerebellar Artery Infarct

49-year-old man · sudden-onset gait imbalance, crossed brainstem signs · working diagnosis: left SCA territory ischemic stroke

Chief Complaint

A 49-year-old man was admitted to the hospital after experiencing the rapid onset of acute gait imbalance 3 days ago1.

History of Chief Complaint

He had spent Sunday doing light work in the yard and that evening suddenly developed difficulty walking2. The gait imbalance has persisted unchanged since that time3. He noted that he tended to lean to the left on standing or walking4. He currently was experiencing a mild headache, but denied any vertigo5. He denied any history of neck trauma6. Vomiting occurred once or twice late Sunday night, but had not reoccurred since that time7.

Family History

He was married, with two children, both of whom were in college. His mother was alive and in good health; his father had died from a myocardial infarct at the age of 60.

Past Medical History

He had had diabetes since childhood; control was maintained daily with insulin8. He had recently experienced visual loss, and numbness in his toes9. He had a history of mild hypertension that had been controlled with pharmaceuticals for the past 3 years10.

General Physical Examination

He was an awake, oriented, and afebrile male, appearing his stated age and of appropriate weight. He was well nourished; his skin had good color, texture, and temperature. He had several small bruises on his left foot11. Optic discs had numerous microaneurysms, with several surrounding deep hemorrhages and scattered hard exudates12. A bruit was noted over the left carotid artery13. Chest was clear to auscultation and percussion; abdomen was soft with no masses or lumps. Blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respirations were normal on the date of admission.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

He was awake and oriented to time and place. Memory and knowledge were appropriate. He was mildly dysarthric.

Cranial Nerves

Eye movements were full, with an up-beating nystagmus that worsened on down gaze and remitted on up gaze14. The left pupil was smaller than the right; both pupils reacted normally to light. The left palpebral fissure was reduced compared to that of the right15. Normal hearing to finger rub was present in both ears, and he denied tinnitus. He had diminished sensation to pinprick on the right side of his face16. Pinprick sensation and corneal reflex on the left were intact. His gag and jaw-jerk reflexes were intact. He had a mild paralysis of the facial muscles around the corner of his mouth on the right17. His palate elevated on the midline and his tongue protruded on the midline.

Motor Exam

Limb strength and reflexes were physiologic in all extremities. Past-pointing was present on finger-to-nose testing in the left upper extremity and on heel-to-shin testing in the left lower extremity18. He demonstrated a mild endpoint intention tremor on purposeful movements in the left upper extremity. On attempted synchronous rapid alternating movements of the hands, the left hand moved more slowly. Left pronator drift was evident, and he deviated to the left on walking19.

Sensory Exam

Vibratory, two-point discrimination, and proprioceptive senses were intact throughout the body. Pinprick sensation was intact on the left side of his body but diminished in the upper and lower extremities on the right20.

Follow-Up (Six Months)

Follow-up examination at six months after discharge finds the patient slightly clumsy with movements involving the left extremity. He complains of occasional stumbling movements involving the left leg and has some difficulty picking up delicate objects with his left hand. Motor exam finds a slight suggestion of past-pointing with both heel-to-shin and finger-to-nose testing; otherwise coordination and strength are normal. The right-sided hemianalgesia has greatly diminished, however a very slight asymmetry is still detectable. Eye movements are full and symmetrical and facial symmetry is equal. He has sensation to pinprick throughout his face and his corneal reflexes are present bilaterally. He complains of a vague, but slightly different feeling on the right side of his face when compared to the left side.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Condensed localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

1 · Localization

Left superior cerebellar artery (SCA) territory

Ipsilateral (left) limb ataxia, dysmetria, and gait deviation localize to the left cerebellar hemisphere/peduncles. Ipsilateral (left) Horner's syndrome localizes to the left lateral brainstem tegmentum, where descending sympathetic fibers travel. Contralateral (right) supranuclear facial weakness and contralateral (right) combined face-and-body pain/temperature loss both localize to a rostral brainstem level — high enough that the ascending trigeminothalamic pathway has already crossed, unlike in a lower medullary lesion. This full combination — ipsilateral cerebellar and Horner's signs with contralateral facial and combined sensory loss — is the textbook signature of an SCA territory infarct, not PICA (Wallenberg) or AICA syndrome.

2 · Distribution

Focal, unilateral, single arterial territory

Every finding is explainable by a single left-sided posterior fossa/rostral brainstem lesion in one vascular distribution — there's no need to invoke a second, separate process, unlike the secondary generalized ICP effects that accompanied the mass lesions in Cases 6-1 and 6-2.

3 · Time Course

Acute onset, then plateau

Symptoms appeared over a single evening and have remained unchanged for three days — the classic temporal signature of an ischemic event: maximal deficit at onset, followed by stability (and, as the six-month follow-up shows, gradual improvement) rather than the progressive worsening expected from an enlarging mass.

4 · Differential Diagnosis
DiagnosisFitsArgues Against
Left SCA territory ischemic infarct Most Likely Sudden onset; ipsilateral cerebellar signs + Horner's; contralateral supranuclear facial palsy + contralateral combined face/body sensory loss — matches the SCA syndrome exactly; strong atherosclerotic risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, carotid bruit) Awaiting imaging confirmation of the exact territory
AICA territory infarct Posterior fossa/brainstem-cerebellar vascular event AICA syndrome classically causes ipsilateral (not contralateral) facial paralysis of the whole face, plus ipsilateral hearing loss/tinnitus — none of which are present here
PICA territory infarct (lateral medullary/Wallenberg) Brainstem vascular event with ataxia, Horner's, and sensory change Wallenberg classically produces ipsilateral face + contralateral body loss (alternating), plus dysphagia/dysphonia/hiccup — this patient's face and body loss are on the same (contralateral) side, with no bulbar symptoms
Vertebral artery dissection Can cause posterior-circulation stroke, sometimes in younger patients No neck trauma or neck pain reported, and this patient's strong atherosclerotic risk profile makes thromboembolism more likely than dissection
Cerebellar hemorrhage Can present with sudden ataxia No severe headache or decline in consciousness described — though this must still be excluded with initial imaging
5 · Diagnostic Workup
  1. Urgent non-contrast CT head.First-line to exclude hemorrhage before any antithrombotic therapy is considered.
  2. MRI brain with DWI/ADC.Most sensitive study for confirming an acute posterior fossa/brainstem ischemic infarct and delineating the SCA territory.
  3. CTA/MRA head and neck.Evaluates the vertebrobasilar system for stenosis/occlusion and further characterizes the carotid bruit.
  4. Carotid duplex ultrasound.Dedicated workup of the carotid bruit noted on exam.
  5. Cardiac evaluation — ECG, transthoracic (± transesophageal) echocardiogram.Screens for a cardioembolic source as part of standard stroke-etiology classification.
  6. Labs.HbA1c, fasting glucose, lipid panel, coagulation studies — standard secondary-prevention stroke workup.
6 · Prognosis

Favorable, with likely residual mild deficit

Cerebellar/brainstem infarcts often show substantial improvement over weeks to months through central compensation — exactly what this patient's six-month follow-up shows: near-resolution of the facial and sensory findings, with only mild residual left-sided dysmetria. Long-term recurrence risk depends heavily on how well the underlying diabetes, hypertension, and atherosclerosis are controlled going forward.

7 · Management
  • Confirm no hemorrhage (CT) before starting antithrombotic therapy; note that this presentation is beyond the acute reperfusion (thrombolysis/thrombectomy) window given the 3-day delay
  • Antiplatelet therapy for secondary stroke prevention
  • Statin therapy
  • Aggressive risk-factor control: blood pressure, glycemic management/insulin regimen review, diabetes and ophthalmology follow-up for retinopathy
  • Follow through on carotid findings (endarterectomy/stenting if indicated by workup)
  • Protective foot care given diabetic peripheral neuropathy and documented unnoticed trauma
  • Physical/occupational therapy for gait and coordination rehabilitation

This patient: facial weakness and both sensory losses all land on the side opposite the cerebellar/Horner's signs — the one-vessel pattern that separates SCA from its two neighboring cerebellar arteries.

Terminology

Partial Horner's Syndrome

This patient: the descending oculosympathetic pathway runs through the lateral brainstem tegmentum on its way to the eye. A lesion anywhere along that path — including a left SCA-territory infarct — interrupts it ipsilaterally, producing left miosis and left ptosis together: a partial Horner's syndrome.

Pattern Recognition

Why This Sensory Pattern Localizes Above the Medulla

This patient: by the time the ascending sensory pathways reach the rostral pons/midbrain, the trigeminothalamic tract has already crossed too — so a lesion here loses face and body pain/temperature sensation together on the same (contralateral) side, unlike the alternating pattern seen lower down in the medulla.

Localization

Supranuclear vs. Nuclear Facial Palsy

This patient: the forehead's upper facial muscles get bilateral cortical input, so a one-sided (supranuclear) lesion never weakens them — only the lower face, and on the side opposite the lesion, exactly as described here. A nuclear/nerve (LMN) lesion instead weakens the whole face, ipsilaterally.

This patient: a slow-rising curve over months (Cases 6-1, 6-2) says mass lesion; a sharp step that flattens out in a day (Case 6-3) says vascular event. Reading the shape of the timeline is often as diagnostic as the exam itself.

Genetics / Workup / Trajectory

Cerebellar Arterial Distribution (Gross Specimen)

This patient: seeing the three vessels on an actual specimen reinforces Card 5's table — the SCA is the last branch off the basilar before it splits into the posterior cerebral arteries, AICA arises mid-basilar, and PICA comes off the vertebral artery itself. That rostral-to-caudal order is exactly why each vessel produces a different cranial-nerve/tract signature.

Localization

Brainstem Arterial Supply & Cranial Nerve Relationships

This patient: the SCA sits at the ponto-mesencephalic junction near CN III/IV, AICA travels with CN VII/VIII at the cerebellopontine angle, and PICA winds around the medulla near CN IX–XII. That's why AICA infarcts classically add ipsilateral deafness/tinnitus and PICA infarcts add dysphagia/dysphonia/hiccup — and why this patient, with an SCA-level lesion, had none of those lower cranial nerve findings.
