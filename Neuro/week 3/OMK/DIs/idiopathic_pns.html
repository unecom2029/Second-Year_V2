🔥 Complex Regional Pain Syndrome I & II

🎯 How Boards Would Word It
  • A vignette with a patient — often a woman in her 40s — who had a seemingly minor extremity injury (classically a distal radius/Colles fracture, but also sprain, surgery, or crush injury) that should have healed, but weeks later has burning pain out of proportion to the inciting event, plus allodynia, swelling, and skin/temperature/color changes.
  • The single biggest distinguishing phrase to catch: pain is regional, not dermatomal — it doesn't respect a specific nerve territory, which is what separates CRPS from a compressive or entrapment neuropathy in a "which nerve is this" trap question.
  • Imaging distractor: a foot or hand X-ray showing patchy periarticular osteopenia (Sudeck atrophy) with joint spaces preserved — supportive, but boards will still expect you to know the diagnosis is clinical (Budapest criteria), not imaging-based.
  • Type I vs. Type II is almost always tested as: no demonstrable nerve injury → Type I (formerly RSD) vs. documented/confirmed nerve injury → Type II (formerly causalgia). Everything else about the presentation is identical between the two.
  • Classic wrong-answer trap: opioids as a treatment choice — boards want early corticosteroids/bisphosphonates + PT/OT, not opioids, which can worsen pain via opioid-induced hyperalgesia.
Overview — background, signs & symptoms, diagnosis, and treatment at a glance (Osmosis)
1 Natural History
Epidemiology & Etiology
  • Overall prevalence estimated at 5–25 per 100,000; incidence varies heavily by injury type.
  • Causalgia (CRPS II) develops in 1–5% of peripheral nerve injuries; RSD (CRPS I) develops in 1–2% of fractures generally, but in 7–35% of Colles (distal radius) fractures specifically.
  • Risk is higher with distal-extremity injuries or when the sciatic nerve is involved.
  • Peak age is the early 40s; female-to-male ratio is 3:1, with CRPS I most frequent in the 4th–5th decade in women.
  • Typically involves a single limb. Up to 77% of cases follow a known traumatic event — sprain/strain (29%), postsurgical (24%), crush/contusion (11%).
Pathophysiology

Mechanism is not fully established and is likely multifactorial. The defining difference between the two types is whether a nerve injury can be demonstrated:

  • CRPS I (formerly RSD): no evidence of nerve damage on exam or testing.
  • CRPS II (formerly causalgia): preceded by a documented peripheral nerve injury.

Proposed contributing mechanisms include an exaggerated/neurogenic inflammatory response, maladaptive healing with protective disuse, ephaptic transmission between damaged fibers (CRPS II), sympathetic nervous system dysfunction (catecholamine hypersecretion, alpha-adrenoceptor hypersensitivity), autoimmunity against peripheral autonomic tissue, central sensitization at the spinal dorsal horn, and cortical reorganization that alters sensory perception. Anxiety and depression are common comorbidities, though their causal role is unclear.

Peripheral injury ± nerve damage→ Exaggerated inflammatory + sympathetic response→ Peripheral & central sensitization→ Disproportionate regional pain + autonomic/trophic change→ Self-perpetuating pain–swelling cycle
Pathophysiologic cycle — the injury-triggered sympathetic/inflammatory feedback loop that perpetuates pain and swelling in CRPS

Symptoms typically emerge 4–6 weeks after the inciting event. With early multidisciplinary treatment, most patients improve within 18 months; roughly 15% go on to develop chronic symptoms.

2 Clinical Presentation
Signs & Symptoms

Findings are most prominent distally and span four domains — the hallmark is pain that is regional (not dermatomal) and disproportionate to the inciting injury:

  • Sensory: burning/stinging/electrical pain (93%), hyperpathy (79%), hyperesthesia (76%), hypoesthesia in a stocking/glove pattern (69%), allodynia.
  • Motor: limited range of motion (88%), decreased grip/strength, muscle atrophy (55%), incoordination (54%), tremor (49%).
  • Autonomic/vasomotor: altered skin temperature and color (92%), hyperhidrosis (47%).
  • Trophic/sudomotor: edema (69%), altered nail or hair growth (60%), skin atrophy (40%).
~1–3 months post-injury

Stage 1 — Acute

Aching, burning pain aggravated by contact or emotional upset; allodynia; early bony changes may appear.

~3–6 months post-injury

Stage 2 — Dystrophic

Spontaneous, radiating, burning pain with hyperpathy; trophic changes to skin and hair in the affected region.

~6–12 months post-injury

Stage 3 — Atrophic

Pain begins to diminish; skin cools; subcutaneous atrophy and tissue wasting develop.

This staging may reflect different patterns of presentation rather than an obligate sequential progression — not every patient moves through all three stages.

⚠ Must Exclude Before Diagnosing

Because CRPS is a diagnosis of exclusion, rule out mimics before committing to the diagnosis: infection (tenosynovitis, osteomyelitis), compartment syndrome, peripheral vascular disease, DVT, peripheral neuropathy, stress fracture, thoracic outlet syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, Raynaud phenomenon, gout, Charcot foot, and conversion/factitious disorder.

4 Treatment
Multidisciplinary Management

Goals are functional restoration and pain control; management works best when physical/occupational therapy, medical pain management, and psychological support are combined rather than any one modality alone.

Functional / Physical

  • PT/OT-driven graded sensorimotor stimulation, mirror visual feedback, graded motor imagery, desensitization
  • Contrast baths, gradual weight-bearing/ROM progression, aquatic therapy
  • Limb edema: elevation, compression garments, exercise

Early Disease (≤6 mo)

  • Short oral corticosteroid taper (e.g., prednisone 2–4 weeks)
  • Bisphosphonates
  • Transdermal lidocaine or capsaicin

Adjuncts (limited evidence)

  • NSAIDs, gabapentin/amitriptyline, calcitonin, clonidine
  • Avoid opioids — may worsen pain via opioid-induced hyperalgesia

Refractory / Interventional

  • Sympathetic nerve blocks
  • Spinal cord stimulation / dorsal column stimulator, TENS
  • Surgical sympathectomy or ablation

Psychological Support

Patient education and reassurance that pain does not reflect ongoing tissue damage, plus counseling for the anxiety/depression frequently seen alongside CRPS.

5 Monitoring & Complications
Follow-Up & Long-Term Complications
  • Track functional progress through PT/OT — range of motion, strength, and pain scores over time.
  • Periodically reassess against the Budapest criteria to gauge symptom resolution.
  • If opioids were used, monitor for opioid-induced hyperalgesia.
  • Long-term complications include chronic pain (~15% of cases), progressive muscle and skin atrophy, joint contractures, and disuse-related bone loss.
6 Prevention & Health Promotion
Risk Reduction
  • Vitamin C supplementation after fractures — the strongest evidence is following wrist/distal radius fractures, where it reduces CRPS incidence.
  • Early mobilization after fracture or surgery rather than prolonged immobilization.
  • Early recognition and referral — starting multidisciplinary treatment promptly improves the odds of resolution.
💡 Pearl

CRPS pain is regional, not dermatomal or nerve-territory-restricted — that single distribution clue is what separates it from a compressive neuropathy or radiculopathy on exam.

⚡ Trigeminal Neuralgia

🎯 How Boards Would Word It
  • An older patient (50s–70s) describes brief, electric shock-like or stabbing unilateral facial pain lasting seconds (never hours), sometimes triggered by chewing, talking, or brushing their teeth — an innocuous stimulus is the giveaway.
  • Pain respects the trigeminal nerve distribution (V1/V2/V3) and stays on one side during a given attack — a question describing pain crossing the midline at once, or occurring bilaterally in an older patient, is testing that this would be atypical for classic TN.
  • Bilateral trigeminal neuralgia in a young patient is a classic board pairing for multiple sclerosis (secondary TN) — this exact phrase shows up as a diagnosis clue far more than as a description of typical disease.
  • MRI is used to classify/rule out a cause (neurovascular compression, MS plaques, a cerebellopontine angle tumor) — but the diagnosis itself is clinical, not imaging-based.
  • First-line therapy is an anticonvulsant — carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine — not opioids (explicitly ineffective here) and not a standard analgesic.
1 Natural History
Epidemiology & Risk Factors
  • Primarily a disease of older adults, though it can occur less often in younger patients; peak incidence is 50–70 years old.
  • US prevalence ≈ 1.5 per 10,000; incidence ≈ 15,000 new cases/year (about 4–13 per 100,000).
  • More common in women, with a female-to-male ratio of roughly 1.5:1 to 2:1.
  • Risk factors include hypertension and a history of migraine.
Etiology, Classification & Pathogenesis

Most cases result from mechanical (neurovascular) compression of the trigeminal nerve root — typically by an aberrant loop of the superior cerebellar artery — producing a regional pain pattern tied to whichever portion of the root is compressed. Central sensitization of the spinal trigeminal complex in the medullary brainstem, and ephaptic transmission between peripheral sensory neurons in the trigeminal ganglion, likely contribute to the final pain syndrome. A "dental theory" of pain origin has little supporting evidence.

Neurovascular compression of CN V root (or demyelination/mass)→ Ephaptic transmission + root-level nerve injury→ Central sensitization of spinal trigeminal nucleus→ Paroxysmal shock-like pain in the affected division(s)
Neurovascular compression of the trigeminal nerve root by the superior cerebellar artery at the pons, with trigeminal ganglion (V1/V2/V3) and facial trigger-zone illustration (AMBOSS)

Classical TN (CTN)

~75–90% of cases

Neurovascular compression of the root, most often by the superior cerebellar artery.

Secondary TN (STN)

~15% of cases

Due to an underlying disease — most often multiple sclerosis, a cerebellopontine angle tumor, or an arteriovenous malformation.

Idiopathic TN (ITN)

~10% of cases

No identifiable cause; MRI and electrophysiological testing are unremarkable.

2 Clinical Presentation
Signs & Symptoms
  • Abrupt-onset, abrupt-terminating unilateral facial pain — sharp, shooting, stabbing, or electric shock-like — typically shooting from the mouth toward the angle of the jaw, sometimes followed by a burning ache.
  • Attacks last seconds (rarely up to ~2 minutes) and can recur up to 100 times per day; pain is confined to one or more trigeminal divisions (V1/V2/V3) and does not cross the midline within a single attack (though 3% of patients can switch sides between attacks).
  • Pain-free interictal periods are typical, though a background dull ache can persist in some patients; symptom-free intervals between attack clusters can range from weeks to years, and the overall course is usually progressive.
  • Triggered by innocuous stimuli — chewing, talking, brushing teeth, washing the face, or light touch — and triggers are sometimes found outside the painful area itself (occasionally even the cervical region).
  • Autonomic changes can accompany attacks: lacrimation, rhinorrhea, conjunctival injection.
  • Significant psychological burden is common, ranging from dysphoria to severe depression with suicidal ideation.
Cutaneous innervation of the trigeminal nerve — ophthalmic (V1), maxillary (V2), and mandibular (V3) divisions and their skull foramina of exit
⚠ Red Flag

True bilateral, simultaneous facial pain is very unusual for classic/idiopathic TN. Bilateral trigeminal neuralgia in a young patient should raise concern for multiple sclerosis (secondary TN) and warrants MRI. Sensory deficits or an abnormal neurologic exam between attacks likewise point toward a secondary cause rather than classical or idiopathic disease.

Differential Diagnoses to Exclude

Migraine, cluster headache, otitis media/externa, herpes zoster (shingles), dental infection, sinusitis, glaucoma, and temporomandibular joint dysfunction can all mimic trigeminal neuralgia and should be considered.

4 Treatment
General Approach

Manage acute pain crises first, start regular anticonvulsant therapy, involve neurology, and reserve neurosurgical options for symptoms that remain poorly controlled or medication side effects that are intolerable.

Acute Exacerbation

  • IV lidocaine infusion under specialist supervision with continuous cardiac monitoring
  • Alternatives: phenytoin/fosphenytoin infusion, oral or nasal lidocaine, local anesthetic trigger-point injection, sumatriptan
  • Avoid opioids — ineffective for trigeminal neuralgia pain

Chronic / First-Line

  • Carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine — pick one
  • Alternatives on an individual basis: lamotrigine, baclofen, phenytoin, gabapentin
  • Should be initiated and supervised by a neurology specialist

Surgical (insufficient response or intolerable side effects)

  • Microvascular decompression (MVD): suboccipital craniotomy to identify and separate the compressing vessel from the nerve root, often with a pad placed between them — achieves the most sustained pain relief of the invasive options, but carries surgical risk (aseptic meningitis, ipsilateral hearing loss, sensory loss, CSF leak, stroke, hematoma).
  • Surgical neurectomy or gangliolysis
  • Radiosurgery (gamma knife) and other percutaneous ablative procedures targeting the trigeminal root/ganglion
💡 Pearl

Bilateral trigeminal neuralgia in a young patient = think multiple sclerosis until proven otherwise. In an older patient with unilateral, trigger-provoked, shock-like pain, treat it as classical TN and start an anticonvulsant — not an opioid.
