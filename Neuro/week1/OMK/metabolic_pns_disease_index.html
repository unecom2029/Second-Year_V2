LO 1–6 Endocrine Sensorimotor + Autonomic Most Common Neuropathy Worldwide

🩸 Diabetic Neuropathy

A peripheral neuropathy affecting sensory, motor, and autonomic fibers in diabetic patients. "Diabetic neuropathy" most often refers to a distal, symmetric, sensorimotor polyneuropathy with a distal-to-proximal ("stocking-glove") distribution — the most common presentation — though autonomic, focal/multifocal, and small-fiber variants also occur.

🎯 How Boards Would Word It

"A 58-year-old man with a 15-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus presents with a burning, tingling sensation in both feet that is worse at night. He first noticed it in his toes about two years ago, and it has since spread toward his ankles. On examination, he has decreased sensation to light touch, vibration, and pinprick in a stocking-glove distribution bilaterally, along with absent ankle reflexes. Strength is preserved. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

This stem is built from the exact features boards test every time: long-standing T2DM + slowly ascending, bilateral, distal sensory loss + worse at night + depressed ankle reflexes + preserved strength → distal symmetric polyneuropathy, the most common pattern of diabetic peripheral neuropathy. Everything below unpacks that answer in full.

LO1 Natural History

Epidemiology & Risk Factors

  • Most common form of peripheral neuropathy worldwide; U.S. prevalence ≈ 6.5%
  • ≈ 50% of patients with diabetes develop some form of peripheral neuropathy; reported range 10–65%, rising to 50–80% by 25 years post-diagnosis
  • Up to 8% of patients already have evidence of neuropathy at the time diabetes is diagnosed
  • Autonomic neuropathy: Willard's notes cite ≈ 20–40% of diabetics; some sources (AMBOSS) report autonomic involvement in up to 90%, depending on how broadly it's defined and tested for
  • Risk factors: duration of diabetes, patient age, HbA1c level, presence of diabetic retinopathy

Pathogenesis

Chronic hyperglycemia
→
↑Polyol flux · AGE formation · PKC activation · ↑hexosamine flux
→
Vasa nervorum ischemia + oxidative stress + inflammation
→
Axonal glycation injury + microvascular damage

Pathology: prominent axonopathy with some segmental demyelination; disproportionate loss of small fibers relative to large fibers; vascular abnormalities are also seen within the nerve itself.

Prognosis if untreated: There is no treatment that reverses existing nerve damage. Tight glycemic control can prevent onset and slow progression, but cannot undo damage already present — untreated disease progresses from distal sensory loss toward proximal involvement, motor weakness, and disabling complications such as foot ulceration and Charcot arthropathy.

LO2 Clinical Presentation

Distal Symmetric Polyneuropathy (~75% of cases — most common pattern)

  • Numbness and painless tingling, or painful dysesthesia ("burning feet") that characteristically worsens at night
  • Stocking-glove distribution: begins in the toes and ascends to the calves before the hands/fingers become involved
  • Small pain fibers are affected early and severely; large fibers are also involved, but to a lesser degree
  • Mild distal muscle weakness and atrophy, ataxia, and balance problems/falls in long-standing disease
  • Depressed ankle reflexes; reduced vibration, pinprick, light touch, proprioception, and temperature sensation on exam
  • ≈ 50% of patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy are asymptomatic
Mononeuropathy

Focal neuropathy of a single nerve (e.g., cranial mononeuropathy, mononeuropathy multiplex). Affects <10% of patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy.

Radiculoplexus Neuropathy

Most commonly diabetic lumbosacral plexopathy — presents with thigh pain and proximal muscle weakness.

Treatment-Induced ("Insulin Neuritis")

Rare small-fiber neuropathy triggered by a rapid improvement in glucose control after a period of chronic hyperglycemia.

Red Flags / Referral Triggers

  • Painless foot wound or ulcer (malum perforans) — sensory loss means real tissue injury can go unnoticed and progress silently
  • Resting tachycardia (>100 bpm), orthostatic symptoms, or unexplained syncope — suggests cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy and raises risk of silent MI, arrhythmia, and death
  • Recurrent hypoglycemia the patient doesn't feel coming (hypoglycemia unawareness)
  • Atypical presentation or diagnostic uncertainty → refer to neurology for electrophysiologic testing
Malum perforans — a well-demarcated, characteristically painless plantar/interdigital ulcer from repetitive unfelt trauma on a foot with dense sensory loss. Classic complication of diabetic peripheral neuropathy. (Source: AMBOSS)

LO4 Treatment

Management is organ-system- and symptom-directed — there is no disease-modifying drug

Medical
  • Optimize glycemic control — the only measure shown to slow progression (cannot reverse existing damage)
  • Treat comorbid dyslipidemia and hypertension
  • Discontinue contributing medications where possible
Pharmaceutical (pain)
  • First-line: pregabalin, duloxetine, venlafaxine, amitriptyline
  • Also used: tricyclic antidepressants, tramadol, anticonvulsants, narcotic agents
  • Reassess and adjust if pain remains uncontrolled after 12 weeks
Autonomic / Organ-Directed
  • Orthostatic hypotension: exercise, fludrocortisone, midodrine
  • Genitourinary dysfunction: bladder/erectile-dysfunction management
  • Gastroparesis: dietary + prokinetic management

Refer to a pain management specialist for severe/refractory symptoms; refer to neurology whenever the diagnosis is uncertain.

LO5 Monitoring & Complications

Screening Protocol

  • Perform annually — starting at the time of diagnosis for T2DM, and 5 years after diagnosis for T1DM (earlier if symptoms develop)
  • Ask about peripheral sensory symptoms, GU symptoms, recurrent UTIs, dry/cracked skin, and hypotension/syncope
  • Exam: ankle reflexes, focused sensory testing, resting heart rate (>100 bpm suggests cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy), orthostatic vitals, ECG heart-rate-variability assessment

Long-Term Complications

  • Diabetic foot ulcers
  • Diabetic neuropathic (Charcot) arthropathy
  • Silent myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, and increased mortality (cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy)
  • Falls and ataxia from proprioceptive loss
LO6 Prevention & Health Promotion
  • Tight glycemic control — the primary evidence-based measure to prevent onset and slow progression
  • Daily foot self-inspection and foot hygiene to catch painless injury early
  • Smoking cessation
  • ASCVD risk-factor modification
  • Patient/caregiver education on recognizing early neuropathy symptoms and preventive foot care

💡 Pearl

≈50% of patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy are completely asymptomatic — this is exactly why annual screening exams (not waiting for symptoms) are the standard of care from the time of diagnosis.

LO 1–2 Toxic Dose-Dependent Most Common Chemo Neurotoxicity

💉 Chemotherapy-induced Neuropathy

Peripheral neuropathy is the most common neuropathologic complication of chemotherapeutic agents. It is a dose-dependent adverse effect whose fiber involvement and clinical pattern vary by drug class — platinum agents and taxanes tend to be sensory-predominant, while vincristine produces a sensorimotor and prominently autonomic picture.

🎯 How Boards Would Word It

"A 62-year-old man being treated with cisplatin for a germ cell tumor reports new numbness and tingling in his hands and feet, along with difficulty telling where his feet are when walking in the dark. On examination, vibratory sense and proprioception are diminished in a stocking-glove distribution, while pinprick sensation is preserved. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

The stem is built from the features that make platinum-agent neuropathy recognizable: large-fiber sensory loss (vibration, proprioception, two-point discrimination) in a stocking-glove pattern, with pain sensation classically spared → cisplatin-induced peripheral neuropathy. Everything below unpacks that answer, plus how the picture changes with other chemotherapy classes.

LO1 Natural History

Epidemiology & Risk Factors

  • Dose-dependent — represents an adverse effect of the chemotherapeutic agent itself, not a separate disease process
  • ≈60% of patients receiving chemotherapy develop peripheral neuropathy within the first month of treatment
  • Risk factors for worsening chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN): older age, higher BMI, depression, hypertension, physical inactivity, anemia, statin use
  • Worsened by coexisting neuropathy-inducing conditions: vitamin B12 deficiency, diabetes mellitus, alcohol use disorder

LO2 Clinical Presentation
Cisplatin & Oxaliplatin
  • Sensory loss — proprioception, vibratory sense, two-point discrimination — in a stocking-glove pattern
  • Paresthesias in the extremities; pain sensation is preserved
  • Hearing loss (cisplatin — death of cochlear hair cells)
  • Motor loss may occur with diminished reflexes
  • Autonomic dysfunction; neuropathic pain
Vincristine
  • Autonomic neuropathy is the most common neurologic presentation — constipation, urinary retention, orthostatic hypotension
  • Sensory loss, distal-to-proximal; neuropathic pain
  • Jaw pain and hoarseness
  • Weakness with difficulty walking and loss of reflexes

Classifying Toxic Neuropathies by Fiber Involvement (AMBOSS)

  • Sensory-predominant: platinum-based agents, taxanes, bortezomib, thalidomide
  • Sensorimotor: vincristine
  • For comparison, other toxic causes: organic mercury poisoning is sensory-predominant; thallium poisoning produces a distal sensorimotor pattern (see Heavy Metal Neuropathy)

Coasting

Worsening of neuropathic symptoms after the chemotherapeutic agent is discontinued — symptoms can continue to progress for a period even though the causative drug has been stopped.

💡 Pearl

Long-term prognosis is generally favorable: most patients experience remission of signs and symptoms within months of treatment termination — a useful reassurance point, despite the "coasting" phenomenon in the interim.

LO 1–4 Toxic Sensory-Predominant Remits with Abstinence

🍷 Alcoholic Neuropathy

A symmetrical, sensory-predominant polyneuropathy associated with chronic alcohol abuse. Autonomic dysfunction also occurs, though it is harder to evaluate and thought to be somewhat less prominent than the sensory involvement.

🎯 How Boards Would Word It

"A 52-year-old man with a 20-year history of heavy alcohol use presents with burning pain and numbness in both feet that has slowly progressed over the past year. He also reports occasional erectile dysfunction. On examination, vibration and pinprick sensation are diminished in a stocking-glove distribution, legs more than arms, with reduced ankle reflexes and mild distal weakness. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

The stem is built from the classic features of this disease: chronic heavy alcohol use + painful, sensory-predominant stocking-glove neuropathy that is worse in the legs than the arms + autonomic findings (here, erectile dysfunction) → alcoholic neuropathy. Everything below unpacks that answer, including how to distinguish it from thiamine-deficiency neuropathy.

LO1 Natural History

Epidemiology & Risk Factors

  • Sensory neuropathy is one of the most common neurologic effects of chronic alcohol abuse
  • Affects ≈44% of chronic alcohol users; accounts for ≈10% of all polyneuropathies
  • Women are affected more than men; family history is a strong risk factor
  • Autonomic dysfunction is harder to evaluate but is thought to occur somewhat less often than sensory dysfunction
  • Duration of use and lifetime total quantity are key risk factors — more than 100 g/day over many years is thought necessary to generate peripheral neuropathy
  • Likely multifactorial: possible nutritional deficiency (e.g., thiamine) plus direct neurotoxic effect of alcohol on the nerve; picture is often mixed with the peripheral neuropathy of chronic liver disease, and nutritional deficiencies can exacerbate it further

Pathophysiology & Pathology

  • Small-fiber-predominant axonopathy; axonal transport and cytoskeletal properties are impaired by direct ethanol exposure
  • Protein kinase A and protein kinase C may contribute to pathogenesis, particularly in painful symptoms
  • Slowly progressive, following chronic abuse; typically remits with abstinence
  • Involves both axonopathy and demyelination — axonopathy >> demyelination
  • Sensory-predominant, though autonomic dysfunction and motor loss can also be present
LO2 Clinical Presentation

Sensory Dysfunction

  • Aching discomfort and fatigue; painful cramps, paresthesias, and burning paresthesias ("burning feet")
  • Weakness and muscle wasting — legs more than arms
  • Ataxia — lower extremities much more than upper extremities
  • Impaired sensation of all modalities — legs more than arms; numbness, decreased proprioception and vibration sense
  • Stocking-glove pattern — if the arms are as involved as the legs, consider an alternative diagnosis
  • Reduced or absent reflexes; symptoms typically begin distally and progress slowly over months to years
Autonomic Dysfunction
  • Abnormal cardiovascular reflexes and reduced heart rate variability
  • Deranged sudomotor function — greater in the foot than the arm
  • Erectile dysfunction
  • Questionable/inconsistent gastrointestinal dysfunction
Alcoholic vs. Thiamine-Deficiency Neuropathy
  • Alcoholic neuropathy: primarily a painful, sensory-dominant polyneuropathy
  • Thiamine-deficiency neuropathy: primarily a motor-dominant neuropathy (see Nutritional Diseases)
LO3 Diagnostic Workup

Diagnosis is made primarily on history and physical exam. The following studies help differentiate alcoholic neuropathy from other causes of polyneuropathy:

LO4 Treatment

Management

  • Cessation of alcohol use — the primary and most effective intervention
  • Nutrient replacement (e.g., thiamine supplementation)
  • Symptom management follows general principles of polyneuropathy pain management

💡 Pearl

This is a slowly progressive neuropathy that typically remits with abstinence — cessation of alcohol use, not medication, is the primary treatment.

LO 1–2 Toxic Pb · Hg · Tl · As Encephalopathy vs. Neuropathy

⚗️ Heavy Metal Neuropathy

Several heavy metals form toxic interactions with nervous tissue. Encephalopathy is typically the dominant clinical picture, but peripheral neuropathy occurs as well. Toxin-induced ("toxic") neuropathy has been described with lead, mercury, thallium, arsenic, cobalt, copper, and cadmium — each with a distinct exposure profile and clinical fingerprint.

🎯 How Boards Would Word It

"A 45-year-old man who strips lead-based paint for a living presents with several months of diffuse, colicky abdominal pain and new weakness in his right hand — he is unable to extend his wrist or fingers. Sensory examination of the hand is normal. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

The combination of an occupational lead exposure, abdominal pain, and a predominantly motor deficit with wrist-drop and preserved sensation is the classic fingerprint of lead neuropathy — a motor >> sensory neuropathy in which sensory loss is usually absent. Everything below unpacks that answer and contrasts it with mercury, thallium, and arsenic.

LO1 Natural History

General Exposure Pattern

Exposure is typically by inhaled dust or vapor, or ingested as a contaminant of food or water.
Common sources of exposure by metal — arsenic (wood preservatives, pesticides), lead (battery manufacture, insecticides, phosphate fertilizers), cadmium (electroplating, paints, plastics, pigments), mercury (medical waste, pesticides, fish, dental amalgam).
MetalExposure Sources
LeadCurrent major source: lead-based paint in old houses and its dust. Traditionally: battery manufacturing, painting, and pottery glazing
MercuryIndustrial exposure, dietary exposure (fish), dental amalgams
ThalliumHistorically: pesticides and rodenticides. Also industrial and dietary exposure
ArsenicIndustrial exposure (copper or lead smelting) — the most common heavy-metal poisoning; can also result from intentional poisoning or contaminated drinking water

Arsenic Neurotoxicity — Mechanism

Arsenic increases reactive oxygen species and disrupts the redox balance with antioxidants, driving oxidative stress that damages DNA and lipids and activates pro-inflammatory transcription factors. In parallel, arsenic activates p38 MAPK/JNK3 signaling and destabilizes the neuronal cytoskeleton. Together these pathways reduce nerve conduction velocity and increase axonal degeneration — producing peripheral neuropathy and neuropathic pain (and, through separate downstream effects, contributing to CNS injury as well).
Arsenic-induced oxidative stress and cytoskeletal disruption converge on reduced nerve conduction velocity and increased axonal degeneration, producing peripheral neuropathy and neuropathic pain.
LO2 Clinical Presentation
Lead — Adult
  • Initial presentation: diffuse or colicky abdominal pain
  • Distal, symmetric, upper >> lower extremity neuropathy — motor >> sensory
  • Early: wrist-drop from radial neuropathy; Late: foot-drop
  • Sensory loss is usually absent
  • Autonomic involvement occurs at a later stage
Lead — Pediatric

Presents as encephalopathy rather than peripheral neuropathy.

Mercury
  • Organic mercury is most toxic to the CNS, but can also cause distal paresthesias and sensory neuropathy — motor function is typically spared
  • Metallic mercury can cause weakness and sensory loss secondary to axonal loss
Thallium

Adult: distal symmetric axonopathy affecting large fibers; primarily a sensory neuropathy; delayed autonomic neuropathy may also occur.
Pediatric: presents as encephalopathy.

Arsenic

  • Acute exposure leads to encephalopathy
  • Chronic exposure: slowly progressive, symmetric sensorimotor axonopathy
  • Burning paresthesias are the initial finding
  • Weakness greater in the lower extremities than the upper; may progress to complete flaccid paralysis
  • Loss of tendon reflexes
  • Sensory loss in a stocking-glove pattern; vibration and position sense are most affected
General reference: heavy metal toxicity affects far more than the nervous system — this overview also covers cardiovascular, renal, respiratory, and reproductive effects, plus chelation therapy. That broader systemic/treatment content is beyond this topic's LO 1–2 scope but is included here for context.

💡 Pearl

A useful organizing pattern across these metals: in both lead and thallium poisoning, pediatric exposure tends to present as encephalopathy while adult exposure tends to present as peripheral neuropathy — age shifts where the toxin does its most visible damage.

LO 1–2 Toxic Length-Dependent Sensorimotor ESRD Complication

🧪 Uremic Neuropathy

Uremic (polyneuropathy) is a distal, symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy — more severe in the lower than upper limbs — that develops as a complication of chronic kidney disease and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) due to the accumulation of uremic toxins. It is one of the most common neurologic complications of chronic kidney disease.

🎯 How Boards Would Word It

"A 58-year-old man with a 12-year history of chronic kidney disease (GFR 10 mL/min, not yet on dialysis) reports several months of burning discomfort and cramping in both feet, followed by numbness that has begun creeping up toward his ankles. On examination, vibration sense and ankle reflexes are diminished bilaterally, and strength is preserved. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

A length-dependent, distal, symmetric sensory-predominant polyneuropathy arising in a patient with advanced CKD and a GFR in the symptomatic range (<~10–12 mL/min) is the classic fingerprint of uremic polyneuropathy. Everything below unpacks that answer — and the one red-flag presentation that should make you doubt it.

LO1 Natural History

Epidemiology & Etiology

  • Seen primarily in patients with renal disease; one of the most common neurologic complications of chronic kidney disease
  • ≈70% of patients with chronic renal failure show some evidence of neuropathy — reported rates range from 60% to over 90% of ESRD patients depending on the diagnostic criteria used
  • Caused by elevated uremic toxins in the circulation secondary to renal failure
  • Patients typically become symptomatic once renal function falls below ≈15% (GFR <~10–12 mL/min) — usually 10–15 years after the onset of renal disease

Pathophysiology

Underlying pathology is primary axonal degeneration ("dying-back" neuropathy) with secondary segmental demyelination, most severe distally. Several contributing mechanisms have been proposed:

  • Hyperkalemia & hyperphosphatemia: drive oxidative stress and free-radical injury to peripheral nerves; chronic hyperkalemia causes persistent membrane depolarization, which may trigger reverse activation of the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger → calcium influx → axonal death. Potassium satisfies all proposed criteria for a true uremic neurotoxin
  • Middle molecule hypothesis: proposed that solutes of 300–12,000 Da (e.g., β2-microglobulin, parathyroid hormone) were neurotoxic; largely disputed due to lack of conclusive evidence for any single molecule
  • Protein-bound uremic toxins: indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate are associated with nerve dysfunction via oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction
  • Other proposed contributors: neurotransmitter imbalance, transketolase inhibition by uremic solutes, neuroimmune/inflammatory pathways
Three converging insults from failing kidneys: toxin build-up, electrolyte imbalance (including hyperkalemia), and fluid retention — all of which contribute to peripheral nerve injury in ESRD.
LO2 Clinical Presentation

Sensorimotor Axonopathy — Slowly Progressive, Length-Dependent

  • Begins distally in the lower limbs and progresses proximally, eventually involving the upper limbs
  • Early symptoms: paresthesias, paradoxical heat sensation, muscle cramps, numbness and tingling; restless legs syndrome may be an early harbinger, though this is not proven
  • Early signs: loss of vibratory sense and reduced/absent ankle deep tendon reflexes; distal "stocking" sensory loss
  • Autonomic dysregulation can occur: postural hypotension, impaired sweating, diarrhea, constipation, and impotence — mild autonomic involvement is common, though overt clinical dysfunction is uncommon
  • Pain is not typically present initially, but can dominate in late stages
  • Long-term: weakness, diminished deep tendon reflexes, imbalance, and muscle atrophy
Stage 1 — Asymptomatic

≈19% of ESRD patients (neuropathy symptom score)

Stage 2 — Non-Disabling

≈48% of ESRD patients

Stage 3 — Disabling

≈14% of ESRD patients

Red Flag — Don't Mistake This for Uremic Neuropathy

A rapidly progressive, motor-predominant neuropathy developing over days to weeks should raise suspicion for a demyelinating neuropathy (Guillain-Barré syndrome or CIDP) rather than uremic neuropathy. This distinction matters clinically — demyelinating neuropathies respond to immunotherapy, whereas uremic neuropathy does not.

💡 Pearls

  • Nerve conduction evidence of uremic neuropathy can be seen even in children and teenagers with renal failure
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome is a frequent complication of uremic neuropathy
  • Key differentials to exclude in an ESRD patient with neuropathy: concurrent diabetic neuropathy (diabetes is a leading cause of ESRD), demyelinating neuropathies (CIDP, GBS), and critical illness polyneuropathy in acutely ill patients

Beyond LO 1–2 (for context)

Management is not part of this topic's required scope, but briefly: renal transplantation is the only known cure (clinical recovery over 3–6 months for mild disease, up to 2 years for severe disease); dialysis stabilizes but rarely reverses neuropathy, and progressive neuropathy despite adequate dialysis is itself a marker of dialysis inadequacy; keeping serum potassium controlled between sessions may reduce severity; symptomatic neuropathic pain is treated with tricyclics (e.g., amitriptyline) or anticonvulsants (e.g., gabapentin).

LO 1–4 Nutritional Length-Dependent, Motor-Dominant "Dry Beriberi"

🌾 Thiamine (Vitamin B1) Deficient Neuropathy

Thiamine deficiency can produce a symmetrical peripheral neuropathy — one of several nutritional deficiencies capable of damaging peripheral nerve function. Historically called "beriberi" (Sinhalese for "I cannot, I cannot"/weakness), classically from diets of polished white rice. Beriberi has two forms: wet beriberi (high-output cardiac failure with edema, seen with high-carbohydrate diets and intense activity) and dry beriberi (weakness from peripheral neuropathy alone, seen with poor caloric intake and sedentary behavior). This page focuses on dry beriberi, given its association with peripheral neuropathy. (Related nutritional neuropathies: pyridoxine/B6, cobalamin/B12, folate, riboflavin, vitamin E.)

🎯 How Boards Would Word It

"A 34-year-old woman, six months post-bariatric surgery with persistent vomiting and significant weight loss, presents with three weeks of progressively ascending weakness and numbness in her legs — initially concerning for Guillain-Barré syndrome. On exam she also has confusion and horizontal nystagmus. Lumbar puncture shows normal CSF protein. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

This stem is deliberately built to look like GBS and then undercut it: symptom duration >3 weeks, confusion and nystagmus, and normal CSF protein (no albuminocytologic dissociation) all argue against GBS and toward thiamine deficiency neuropathy (dry beriberi with Wernicke overlap) in a patient with a classic risk factor (bariatric surgery + protracted vomiting). Everything below unpacks that answer.

LO1 Natural History

Etiology & Epidemiology

  • Thiamine is an essential water-soluble vitamin that cannot be synthesized by humans and must come from the diet (primarily grains); it is a coenzyme for >24 enzymes, including transketolase (myelin sheath maintenance), pyruvate dehydrogenase (Krebs cycle/ATP production), and 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase (neurotransmitter synthesis)
  • Total body stores are only 30–100 mg with a half-life of ≈2 weeks — stores can be depleted in as little as 2–6 weeks of deficient intake, with symptoms appearing as early as 1 week after depletion
  • Causes: alcohol use disorder (up to 80% of affected individuals may be deficient), malnutrition/starvation (anorexia, bulimia), bariatric surgery (especially with protracted vomiting/rapid weight loss), hyperemesis gravidarum, malabsorption syndromes, diets of polished rice or cassava, chemotherapy-associated vomiting, high-dose loop diuretics, chronic TPN, duodenal stenosis/duodenitis/gastric phytobezoars, chronic diarrhea, and increased utilization states such as pregnancy
  • Sedentary behavior can worsen/exacerbate these risk factors
  • Epidemiology: uncommon in high-income countries except with severe alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, or post-bariatric surgery; suboptimal levels reported in up to 80% of alcoholics, up to 98% of diabetics, and ≈1/3 of dialysis patients with altered mental status; remains prevalent in malnourished populations globally

Pathophysiology & Prognosis

  • Impairs carbohydrate metabolism → accumulation of pyruvate and lactate, reduced ATP production, increased oxidative stress
  • In the PNS: axonal degeneration (sural nerve biopsy shows axonal degeneration with scattered myelin ovoid formation), driven by failure of energy-dependent axonal transport and oxidative nerve damage; transketolase's role in myelin maintenance means its loss also contributes to demyelination
  • In the CNS, vulnerable structures include the mammillary bodies, thalamus, periaqueductal gray, and walls of the 3rd/4th ventricles — the anatomic basis of Wernicke encephalopathy
  • Prognosis if untreated: progression to severe, irreversible axonal damage with permanent sensory loss, motor weakness, and muscle atrophy. Unrecognized Wernicke encephalopathy can progress to irreversible Korsakoff syndrome (permanent dementia, amnesia, confabulation, gait abnormalities). Fatal outcomes can occur — from cardiovascular collapse in wet beriberi, or from the neurologic sequelae of untreated Wernicke encephalopathy. Delays in starting thiamine therapy are directly linked to worse outcomes
Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) is a cofactor for pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase, and transketolase — explaining why deficiency disrupts both energy metabolism and myelin maintenance. Mnemonic shown: B1 = Brain + 1 Heart (Wernicke-Korsakoff + wet beriberi).
LO2 Clinical Presentation

Dry Beriberi — Length-Dependent, Large-Fiber Sensorimotor Axonal Neuropathy

  • Acute or subacute onset of paresthesias and weakness in the legs with loss of tendon reflexes; can develop over 2–12 weeks
  • Sensory: paresthesias, dysesthesias, and neuropathic pain beginning in the distal lower extremities; glove-and-stocking sensory loss to pinprick and vibration
  • Motor: distal weakness beginning in the feet and ascending progressively; muscle atrophy predominantly in the legs
  • Reflexes: reduced or absent, particularly at the Achilles
  • Autonomic: may include sinus tachycardia and other autonomic features
  • Electrophysiology shows an axonal degeneration pattern
  • Thiamine deficiency can also produce CNS involvement (Wernicke-Korsakoff) and/or cardiac involvement (wet beriberi) alongside the neuropathy
  • Patients often present with significant weight loss (mean ≈20 kg), vomiting, and diarrhea

Distinguishing Acute Beriberi from Guillain-Barré Syndrome

An acute presentation can mimic GBS, with rapidly progressive ascending weakness. Features favoring beriberi over GBS: symptom duration >3 weeks, confusion, nystagmus, vocal cord dysfunction, normal CSF protein (absent albuminocytologic dissociation), elevated lactate, and absence of sural sparing on nerve conduction studies.
Wet Beriberi

High-output cardiac failure with edema; seen with high-carbohydrate diets and intense physical activity.

Wernicke-Korsakoff Syndrome

Confusion, ataxia, oculomotor abnormalities (gaze-evoked nystagmus, impaired abduction, conjugate gaze palsy), memory loss, and confabulation.

Tropical Ataxic Neuropathy

Sensory neuropathy with ataxia, bilateral optic atrophy, and sensorineural hearing loss — associated with cassava-based diets.

Cranial Nerve Involvement

Cranial nerves II, III, IV, VI, VII, VIII, IX, and X may be affected.

The four faces of vitamin B1 deficiency on one patient: Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, oculomotor dysfunction, gait ataxia) and Korsakoff syndrome (amnesia, confabulation) affecting the CNS; dry beriberi (symmetric sensorimotor peripheral neuropathy, muscle wasting) and wet beriberi (dilated cardiomyopathy, high-output cardiac failure, edema) affecting the PNS and heart. (Source: AMBOSS)
LO3 Diagnostic Workup

A thorough dietary and social history (alcohol use, nutritional intake, bariatric surgery, eating disorders, protracted vomiting, rapid weight loss) is essential — a low threshold of suspicion is warranted, as thiamine deficiency is frequently misdiagnosed. Physical exam looks for distal sensory loss and weakness in a stocking-glove distribution, diminished/absent deep tendon reflexes, oculomotor/ataxia/mental status changes (Wernicke triad), and signs of heart failure (wet beriberi).

💡 Practical Point

Testing should be performed before repletion — abnormal values normalize rapidly once thiamine is given. In practice, diagnosis is often made empirically: because thiamine repletion is low-risk and delayed treatment risks irreversible harm (Korsakoff syndrome), treatment is frequently started before confirmatory lab results return.
LO4 Treatment

Thiamine Replacement — the Cornerstone of Treatment

ContextRegimen
Acute/severe (Wernicke-Korsakoff)100–200 mg IV three times daily × 3 days, then 100 mg oral daily long-term. Guidelines vary — EFNS recommends 200 mg IV TID; Royal College of Physicians recommends up to 500 mg IV TID
Beriberi neuropathyFDA label: 10–20 mg IM three times daily for up to 2 weeks, then oral multivitamin with 5–10 mg thiamine daily for 1 month. Some sources propose 20–30 mg/day; no single standard regimen exists
Long-term maintenance50–100 mg orally daily
Prophylaxis (high-risk: malnutrition, AUD)100 mg orally daily

Critical Precaution

Thiamine must be given before glucose in deficient patients — carbohydrate metabolism consumes thiamine as a cofactor and can precipitate or worsen Wernicke encephalopathy.
Medical Management
  • Correct the underlying cause (nutritional rehabilitation, alcohol cessation, malabsorption management)
  • Assess and replete concurrent deficiencies — pyridoxine was low in ≈50% of patients in one series
  • Dietary counseling; monitor/manage cardiovascular complications of wet beriberi
Rehabilitation

Physical therapy is an important adjunct for motor weakness and gait ataxia. Motor recovery tends to exceed sensory recovery; most patients show substantial functional recovery by 3–6 months after starting supplementation, though sensory deficits may persist.

Expected Treatment Response by System

  • Cardiac symptoms (wet beriberi): may respond rapidly
  • Oculomotor abnormalities (Wernicke): improve quickly
  • Gait ataxia: improves gradually, often incompletely
  • Peripheral neuropathy: may take months to improve; motor recovery exceeds sensory recovery
  • Korsakoff syndrome: many patients are left with permanent residual amnesia despite treatment

💡 Pearl

It has been proposed that the peripheral neuropathy of "alcoholic beriberi" may actually reflect a multiple B-vitamin deficiency syndrome rather than thiamine deficiency alone (Ropper et al.) — a useful reminder to screen for and replete other B-vitamins (especially pyridoxine) rather than treating thiamine in isolation.
