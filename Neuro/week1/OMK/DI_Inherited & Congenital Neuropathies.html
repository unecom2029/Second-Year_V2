01 Natural History
Definition & Epidemiology

A heterogeneous, inherited polyneuropathy syndrome with 80+ distinct genetic etiologies — some demyelinating (CMT1), some axonal (CMT2). Collectively the most common form of hereditary peripheral neuropathy worldwide, distinguished by its pattern of genetic transmission, slow progression, and symmetric distribution. Named for the three neurologists (Charcot, Marie, Tooth) who first described it; also called Hereditary Motor and Sensory Neuropathy (HMSN) or peroneal muscular atrophy.

  • Prevalence ≈ 1/2500 — one of the most frequently encountered inherited neurologic syndromes
  • CMT1 is the most common form (incidence 0.07–0.15/yr, prevalence ~1/4000); CMT2 is roughly half as frequent
  • Inheritance is usually autosomal dominant, but can be autosomal recessive or X-linked
  • Prognosis: slow progression, but normal life expectancy is maintained
Willard — PNS Degenerative Dx · + AMBOSS (inheritance pattern)
Pathophysiology

80+ genes have been implicated, most encoding components of the Schwann cell myelin sheath. Regardless of which part of the neuron is affected, the shared endpoint is failure of signal transmission to target tissue.

Willard — PNS Degenerative Dx

CMT1 — Demyelinating

  • CMT1A (70–80% of CMT1): AD duplication of PMP22, chromosome 17 — most common CMT subtype overall
  • CMT1B: mutation in MPZ (myelin protein zero) — earlier onset than CMT1A, some axonopathy too
  • CMT1X: X-linked GJB1 gene → connexin-32, a myelin gap-junction protein
  • Chronic demyelination/remyelination cycles → onion-bulb formation; large (sensory) fiber loss, distal > proximal, extending into the dorsal columns (fasciculus gracilis/cuneatus)

CMT2 — Axonal

  • Accounts for 12–36% of all CMT cases
  • CMT2A (most common CMT2 subtype): AD mutation in MFN2, encoding the mitochondrial fusion protein mitofusin-2
  • Axonal degeneration without demyelination or onion-bulb formation
  • Defective mitochondrial fusion → mitochondrial dysfunction → neuronal/axonal death

Pearl

Classic NCS distinguishing pearl: CMT1 (demyelinating) → markedly ↓ conduction velocity. CMT2 (axonal) → ↓ amplitude with relatively preserved velocity. Sensory neurons (longest axons — feet/toes first) are hit earliest and hardest in both.

02 Clinical Presentation
Overview

Both types produce a progressive, length-dependent sensorimotor polyneuropathy — motor loss causes muscle atrophy ("use it or lose it"), while sensory loss begins in the feet/toes first, since these have the longest axons and are most vulnerable to dysfunction. Early motor symptoms: foot/leg weakness → foot drop, high-steppage gait. Late: hand involvement (fine motor tasks become difficult).

Willard + Osmosis

CMT1

  • Insidious onset, late childhood–early adolescence
  • Difficulty running → progressing to difficulty walking
  • Motor: lower extremity first, upper extremity later; frequent weakness, ankle sprains; stumbling, foot slap, foot drop; trouble on uneven ground
  • Sensory: rarely severe; sensory ataxia (↓ proprioception)
  • Palpable hypertrophic nerves (onion-bulb) in some cases
  • Deformities: distal atrophy → "stork-leg"/"inverted champagne bottle" legs; peroneal muscular atrophy; pes cavus + hammer toes (pied en griffe); kyphoscoliosis

CMT2

  • Later onset — typically 2nd–3rd decade of life
  • Distal weakness, atrophy, and diminished (not absent) DTRs — more focused at the ankle than the leg/UE
  • Sensory loss present but less severe than CMT1
  • Variable foot deformity; nerves are not palpable (no onion bulbs)
  • Overall course tends to be milder than CMT1
Pes cavus + hammer toe — abnormally high medial longitudinal arch, dorsiflexed MTP joints with plantarflexed IP joints of the lesser toes (AMBOSS)
Clinical photo — pes cavus with severe hammer toe deformity in a CMT patient (Willard, clinical teaching photo)
Red Flag / Safety Note

Avoid neurotoxic medications (e.g., vincristine) in patients with known or suspected CMT — these can precipitate a severe, rapid exacerbation of neuropathy.

Hereditary Neuropathy with Liability to Pressure Palsies

LO 1–2 HNPP AD — PMP22 deletion Recurrent focal palsies Onset ~20–30y
🎓 How Boards Would Stem It

A 24-year-old law student presents with sudden numbness and weakness in her right hand after a long study session with her elbow propped on a hard desk. This is her third episode of transient limb numbness in two years — a prior episode caused foot drop after she sat with her legs crossed during a long flight, resolving over several weeks. She has no significant past medical history, but her mother has had similar "nerve" episodes.

01 Natural History
Definition & Epidemiology

A degenerative, demyelinating sensory and motor neuropathy seen primarily in young adults, closely related to CMT in its genetic basis. The peripheral myelin sheath is fragile and easily damaged by slight, everyday compression — pressure that would not trouble an unaffected person (carrying a heavy bag, leaning on an elbow, sitting with crossed legs). Also known as tomaculous neuropathy, compression/entrapment neuropathy, or familial pressure-sensitive neuropathy.

  • Prevalence: 7–16/100,000 (Willard) — MedlinePlus cites a lower estimate of 2–5/100,000; ranges vary by source/population
  • Onset usually 20–30 years old, mean 26.5y (can range from childhood to late adulthood)
  • Slightly more frequent in men than women (67:57 in a metastudy of 124 cases)
  • Prognosis: does not affect life expectancy; individual episodes usually recover completely, but repeated episodes can cause permanent weakness or sensory loss
Willard — PNS Degenerative Dx · + MedlinePlus (NIH)
Etiology & Pathophysiology

Autosomal dominant; ~20% of patients have a de novo mutation. HNPP is the molecular mirror image of CMT1A: where CMT1A duplicates PMP22 (3 gene copies), HNPP involves deletion of one PMP22 copy on chromosome 17, leaving only one functional copy. PMP22 protein normally helps myelin tolerate and recover its structure after compression — with only half the normal dose, myelin can no longer withstand routine pressure.

Pathology: focal conduction abnormalities at entrapment sites; nerve biopsy shows tomacula — focal, sausage-like swellings along the axon.

Pearl

HNPP sits at the same locus as CMT1A but with the opposite dosage: CMT1A = PMP22 duplication (3 copies → progressive, symmetric demyelinating neuropathy) vs. HNPP = PMP22 deletion (1 copy → episodic, pressure-triggered focal demyelination). Same gene, reciprocal mechanism, very different phenotype.

02 Clinical Presentation
Overview

Recurrent, transient, focal mononeuropathies — acute onset and painless initially, though limb pain (especially in the hands) can occur. An episode may last minutes to months; recovery is usually complete, but repeated incidents in the same nerve can leave permanent weakness or sensory loss. Any peripheral nerve can be involved, but the pattern favors sites that cross common compression points (wrist, elbow, knee).

Exam findings during/after an episode: muscle weakness, reduced deep tendon reflexes, sensory loss, and — with repeated episodes — atrophy. Pes cavus may also be present. Severity is highly variable: many people never realize they have the disorder, while others experience prolonged disability.

Hereditary Sensory and Autonomic Neuropathy

LO 1–2 HSAN CIP/HSAN spectrum Small (C) + autonomic (B) fiber loss Analgesia → self-mutilation risk
🎓 How Boards Would Stem It

A 9-year-old boy is brought to clinic after his parents noticed a non-healing ulcer on the sole of his foot that he never complained about. History reveals he has bitten his own fingertips since infancy and has sustained several unnoticed kitchen burns. Exam shows preserved light touch and proprioception, but absent pinprick and temperature sensation in the distal extremities, along with dry, non-sweating skin on the feet.

01 Natural History
Definition & Epidemiology

A group of small-fiber (C-fiber) peripheral neuropathies that also involve fibers of the autonomic nervous system (B-fibers). HSAN is now grouped with a related set of disorders termed Congenital Insensitivity to Pain (CIP), together referred to as CIP/HSAN — the shared feature across this spectrum is loss of small-caliber fibers, producing analgesia and, frequently, self-mutilation.

  • HSAN1: rare, autosomal dominant, juvenile onset; exact prevalence unknown but very low
  • HSAN2: autosomal recessive, severe early onset
Willard — PNS Degenerative Dx

HSAN1

  • Etiology: mutation in SPTLC1 (serine palmitoyltransferase, subunit 1), chromosome 9 — the rate-limiting enzyme in ceramide synthesis
  • Pathophysiology: both demyelination and axonopathy seen on biopsy; neuron loss in the dorsal root ganglia and sympathetic ganglia

HSAN2

  • Etiology: unknown
  • Pathophysiology: both small- and large-fiber loss seen on biopsy

Pearl

Small-fiber (C-fiber: pain, temperature, autonomic) loss with relative sparing of large, myelinated fibers (epicritic touch/proprioception) = classic dissociated sensory loss. Patients keep light touch and position sense but lose pain/temperature — so injuries go unnoticed, which is exactly why plantar ulcers and self-mutilation follow.

02 Clinical Presentation

HSAN1

  • Onset between the 2nd and 4th decade of life
  • Analgesia — feet before hands — with sensory dissociation (epicritic/touch systems intact)
  • Neuropathic pain
  • Plantar ulcers
  • Loss of sweating (anhidrosis)
  • Eventually, weakness presents in the lower extremity

HSAN2

  • Early and severe onset
  • Fingers and toes involved (not just lower-extremity-first)
  • Both small- and large-fiber loss → significant, more global sensory loss
  • Notable loss of sweating
  • Signs of other autonomic failure are otherwise absent
Red Flag / Safety Note

Because pain sensation is lost, injuries — burns, cuts, plantar ulcers — routinely go unnoticed, and repeated unprotected tissue damage (including self-mutilation) can follow. Protective sensation and injury prevention are as central to management as the neuropathy itself.

Special Subtype — HSAN Type IE (HSAN1E)

A distinct subtype caused by an autosomal dominant DNMT1 mutation (DNA methyltransferase 1), presenting as a triad of: progressive sensorineural hearing loss (usually moderate–severe deafness by age 20–35), dementia (onset in the 30s — personality change, irritability, apathy, or impulsivity may precede cognitive decline), and a peripheral sensory neuropathy of the feet/legs beginning in adolescence or early adulthood (unnoticed injury → ulcers/infection → possible amputation). Seizures and sleep disturbance can also occur. Severity and age of onset vary, even within the same family.
