🧬 Guillain-Barré Syndrome

SYN: Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy · Acute idiopathic neuropathy · (some sources: subacute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy)
G
"Gus"
How the boards write this diagnosis
A previously healthy adult presents with progressive, symmetric weakness that started in the feet and legs and has been ascending over the past several days, now making it hard to climb stairs or rise from a chair. He mentions a diarrheal illness (or upper respiratory infection) 1–3 weeks ago that has since resolved. On exam, deep tendon reflexes are absent in the legs, and he describes tingling in a stocking-glove distribution. Vitals show intermittent blood pressure swings and a fast heart rate.
Watch for: the antecedent-infection timeline + symmetric ascending pattern + areflexia is the whole signature — swap in ophthalmoplegia/ataxia/areflexia instead and the stem is pointing at the Miller-Fisher variant, not classic GBS.

Definition

A heterogeneous disease — most commonly an acute, often severe, fulminating inflammatory polyradiculoneuropathy. Thought to represent a post-infectious polyneuropathy, since most cases follow the onset and resolution of an infection, typically gastrointestinal.

1 Natural History

Epidemiology

  • Rare, but the most common acquired demyelinating polyneuropathy and the most common cause of acute generalized weakness
  • ~1 case/million/month → annual incidence ≈ 1–2 per 100,000 (up to 3.25/100,000 in some regions)
  • Mean age of onset ≈ 40 years; incidence rises with age
  • Male > female (slight predominance, ~1.5:1)

Etiology

  • Post-infectious: ~70% of cases occur < 1 month after a GI or upper-respiratory infection
  • Campylobacter jejuni — identified in 20–30% of adult GBS cases in North America, Europe, and Australia; the single most commonly associated pathogen
  • Human herpesviruses (esp. CMV), also EBV
  • Other associations: HIV, sarcoidosis, lymphoma, SLE, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Zika virus, influenza, SARS-CoV-2
  • Rare small upticks in incidence reported after certain vaccinations (e.g., seasonal influenza vaccine — listed as a recognized vaccine injury in the US)

Pathogenesis — molecular mimicry

Peripheral nerve biopsies show perivenular and endoneurial infiltration by lymphocytes and macrophages. The prevailing mechanism is molecular mimicry: surface epitopes on the triggering organism (classically the lipooligosaccharide of C. jejuni) structurally resemble gangliosides in peripheral nerve membranes. The resulting antibody response cross-reacts with the nerve itself.

Antecedent infection (e.g., C. jejuni)
→
Antigen presentation → T cell / B cell activation
→
Plasma cells produce cross-reactive antibodies (molecular mimicry vs. gangliosides)
→
Antibody binds peripheral nerve gangliosides
→
Complement activation + macrophage recruitment
→
Schwann cell / myelin injury (Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy, AIDP) or axolemma / node of Ranvier injury (Acute Motor Axonal Neuropathy, AMAN)
Fig. — Molecular mimicry pathogenesis. Ganglioside-like epitopes on C. jejuni's outer membrane and axolemma structurally mimic host gangliosides, provoking crossreactive antibody production. In Acute Motor Axonal Neuropathy (AMAN), anti-GM1/anti-GD1a antibodies fix complement directly at the node of Ranvier/axolemma → membrane attack complex + macrophage invasion → axonal injury. In Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (AIDP), antibody targets an unknown Schwann-cell/myelin antigen → macrophage-mediated demyelination.
· Subtypes (classified by electrodiagnostic findings + antibody profile)
Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (AIDP) ~90% US/Europe

Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy — the classic, most common form. Antibodies target myelin membranes → demyelination-remyelination cycles. More pain and cranial nerve involvement than axonal forms. Associated with C. jejuni and CMV.

Acute Motor Axonal Neuropathy / Acute Motor-Sensory Axonal Neuropathy (AMAN / AMSAN) 30–65% in parts of Asia

Acute motor axonal neuropathy (± sensory involvement in AMSAN). Anti-GM1 / anti-GD1a antibodies → direct axonal loss via complement/macrophage attack. Classically linked to C. jejuni. Faster progression, slower recovery, more residual deficit than Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy (AIDP).

Miller-Fisher Syndrome

Targets CN III/IV/VI, dorsal root ganglia, and muscle spindles. Anti-GQ1b (and anti-GT1a) antibodies in ~90%. Classic triad: ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia. Nerve conduction studies usually normal.

Course & prognosis (untreated / natural trajectory)

  • Symptoms progress rapidly, peaking at ~2–4 weeks, then plateau for months before resolving partially or completely
  • ~80% regain the ability to walk unaided by 6 months
  • 3–7% mortality from complications (respiratory failure, PE, cardiac arrest, sepsis); up to 20% left with significant residual disability
  • Symptom resolution can take >5 years in some patients
  • A chronic, relapsing form that persists / recurs beyond ~8 weeks is reclassified as CIDP, not GBS
2 Typical Clinical Presentation

Motor findings

  • Ascending, symmetric, flaccid paralysis — begins distally in the lower limbs, progresses proximally, may reach upper limbs/trunk ("stocking-glove" distribution of involvement)
  • Hyporeflexia → areflexia, typically beginning in the lower limbs — a hallmark finding
  • Full presentation typically develops over 1–2 weeks (rarely beyond 4 weeks)
  • Respiratory muscle weakness → mechanical ventilation required in ~1/3 of cases

Sensory & pain findings

  • Sensory changes present but typically much less prominent than motor loss
  • Paresthesias in a stocking-glove distribution
  • Pain in ~2/3 of patients — back and limb pain, arthralgias, cramping, aching (both nociceptive and neuropathic); often an early symptom

Cranial nerve & bulbar involvement

  • Facial and oropharyngeal muscle involvement in ~50% of cases — facial diplegia (bilateral facial nerve palsy) is the most frequently affected cranial nerve pattern
  • Ophthalmoparesis in ~15% of cases

Autonomic dysfunction (~65% of cases)

  • Cardiac arrhythmias, extreme blood pressure fluctuations, baroreflex abnormalities
  • GI hypersecretion / dysfunction, urinary bladder dysfunction (retention)
Fig. — Distribution of clinical findings in GBS. Motor: ascending symmetric flaccid paralysis, reduced/absent reflexes, facial palsy, respiratory muscle weakness → potential respiratory failure. Sensory: back/limb pain, peripheral symmetric paresthesia (stocking-glove). Autonomic: cardiac arrhythmia, intestinal dysfunction. (© AMBOSS)

⚠ Red flags — features suggesting a more severe course

  • Rapid progression to peak disability (especially < 7 days)
  • Bulbar palsy / dysphagia, bilateral facial weakness, neck weakness
  • Prominent autonomic instability (arrhythmia, marked BP swings)
  • Any of the above should raise concern for impending respiratory failure — these patients need close, escalating observation

💡 Pearl — GBS vs. CIDP timing

The single clinical detail that separates the two demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathies in this section: GBS peaks by ~4 weeks and then improves; if progression continues beyond 8 weeks, the diagnosis becomes CIDP instead.

🧬 Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy

SYN: CIDP · Chronic inflammatory polyradiculoneuropathy
C
"Chuck"
How the boards write this diagnosis
A middle-aged man reports symmetric weakness in both his hands and feet — and, notably, his hips and shoulders too — that has been slowly worsening over the past several months. There's no preceding infection to point to. He's started tripping more often and says his hands feel clumsy, like he can't tell where his fingers are without looking (impaired proprioception). Exam shows diminished reflexes throughout, with weakness that's out of proportion to how little sensory loss he has.
Watch for: the giveaway is the clock, not the pattern — same symmetric, proximal + distal, areflexic picture as "Gus" (GBS), but stretched out past 8 weeks instead of peaking within 4.

Definition

The most common chronic inflammatory neuropathy. Presents as a symmetrical, demyelinating sensory and motor polyneuropathy affecting both proximal and distal limbs, of at least 2 months' duration. At least five variants are recognized.

1 Natural History

Epidemiology

  • Annual incidence ≈ 1 per 100,000; prevalence 3–9 per 100,000
  • Male predominance (roughly 4:1.5, i.e., ~2–3:1 male > female)
  • Affects adults, with incidence increasing with age; peak incidence 40–60 years

Etiology & predisposing factors

  • Chronic, progressive, acquired immune-mediated disorder lasting > 8 weeks — the defining time-course difference from GBS
  • Unidentified antigenic trigger — not linked to any particular preceding pathogen (unlike GBS's post-infectious pattern)
  • A waxing-and-waning (relapsing-remitting) form can occur, more often in younger individuals
  • Predisposing associations: diabetes mellitus, HIV infection, monoclonal gammopathies (e.g., MGUS), certain malignancies (e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma)

Pathophysiology

Macrophage-mediated inflammatory demyelination, disproportionately affecting proximal > distal nerve segments. Both cellular (activated T cells) and humoral (immunoglobulin + complement deposition) immunity are involved, with breakdown of the blood-nerve barrier permitting immune cell entry.

Pathology shows segmental demyelination and remyelination, producing characteristic "onion bulb" formations around nerves. In a subset of patients, autoantibodies target nodal and paranodal proteins (e.g., anti-neurofascin-155, anti-contactin-1, anti-Caspr1) — these detach myelin from the node of Ranvier and disrupt conduction directly, independent of classic macrophage-driven demyelination.

Blood-nerve barrier breakdown
→
T cell / cytokine / complement / immunoglobulin transmigration into endoneurium
→
Autoantibody binds node of Ranvier + complement fixation (C3b) → MAC (C5b-9)
→
Macrophages bind complement/antibody via CR/FcR → phagocytose myelin
→
Demyelination → Schwann cell remyelination cycles → onion bulb formation
Fig. — CIDP pathophysiology at the blood-nerve barrier (BNB). T cells, complement, cytokines, and immunoglobulins transmigrate into the endoneurium; B cells produce autoantibodies against an unidentified nodal antigen. Autoantibody binding fixes complement (C3b) at the node of Ranvier, forming the membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9); macrophages bind via complement/Fc receptors and phagocytose myelin → demyelination.
· Variants (≥ 5 recognized)
Typical CIDP

Symmetric motor + sensory involvement, both proximal and distal.

Distal CIDP

Sensory + motor involvement predominantly in the lower limbs / distal extremities.

Multifocal / Focal CIDP

Usually asymmetric; motor + sensory involvement predominant in the upper limbs.

Motor CIDP

Motor symptoms/signs predominate; if sensory conduction studies are also abnormal → termed "motor-predominant."

Sensory CIDP

Sensory symptoms/signs predominate; if motor conduction studies are also abnormal → termed "sensory-predominant."

Fig. — Distribution patterns across CIDP variants. Typical CIDP: symmetric proximal + distal motor and sensory involvement. Distal CIDP: sensory + motor, lower-limb predominant. Multifocal/focal CIDP: asymmetric, upper-limb predominant. Motor and sensory CIDP variants show isolated or predominant involvement of the respective modality.
2 Typical Clinical Presentation

Onset & motor findings

  • Gradual onset, developing over a few months (contrast with GBS's 1–4 week peak)
  • Symmetric sensory and motor polyradiculoneuropathy — motor loss >> sensory loss
  • Weakness with diminished tendon reflexes, affecting both proximal and distal muscles
  • Weakness may be somewhat more pronounced distally, but is not classically length-dependent the way axonal polyneuropathies are
  • Globally diminished or absent reflexes

Sensory findings

  • Sensory loss is large-fiber type — impaired proprioception and discriminative touch
  • Sensory ataxia can occur secondary to proprioceptive loss
  • Pain, when present, is generally moderate in nature (less prominent than in GBS)

Other features

  • Cranial nerve involvement occurs in some individuals, but is uncommon overall
  • Mild autonomic involvement possible — constipation, urinary retention
Fig. — Symptom overview in CIDP: muscle weakness, muscle loss, tingling/numbness, nerve pain, trouble walking, balance problems, poor (diminished) reflexes, tremors. (Everyday Health)

💡 Pearl — the "motor >> sensory, not length-dependent" signature

CIDP weakness is proximal + distal and disproportionate to sensory loss — this is what separates it from the classic length-dependent, distal, sensory-predominant pattern of most axonal polyneuropathies (e.g., diabetic polyneuropathy), and from GBS by its slow, >8-week (often relapsing) time course rather than a rapid 1–4 week peak.

🧬 Vasculitic Neuropathy

SYN: Systemic vasculitis · Periarteritis nodosa
V
"Val"
How the boards write this diagnosis
A middle-aged woman with a history of rheumatoid arthritis (or hepatitis C) presents with a painful, sudden foot drop on the right — she describes it as coming on over a couple of days, not gradually. Two weeks later she develops numbness and weakness in her left wrist as well. The pattern is asymmetric and jumps from one specific nerve to another rather than spreading evenly, and she reports burning, stabbing pain at each new site.
Watch for: this is the odd one out compared to "Gus" and "Chuck" — asymmetric, painful, and multifocal (mononeuritis multiplex) from the start, usually with a named autoimmune or infectious risk factor sitting right in the history.

Definition

The vasculitides are non-infectious, inflammatory diseases in which blood vessel walls are damaged by an inflammatory process. Polyarteritis nodosa is the classic example; several forms of diabetic neuropathy have recently been reclassified as vascular neuropathy as well. Peripheral nerve involvement occurs in ~1/3 of individuals with vasculitis, due to involvement of the epineurial blood vessels (the vasa nervorum) — hence "vasculitic neuropathy."

1 Natural History

Epidemiology

  • More common in adults
  • Can present as a complication of hepatitis C

Etiology & risk factors

  • Systemic rheumatologic disease — lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, Sjögren's syndrome
  • Infections
  • Medications — e.g., antibiotics (drug-induced hypersensitivity vasculitis)
  • Comorbid conditions — diabetes, hypertension, elevated cholesterol

Classification

Vasculitides associated with peripheral neuropathy are first divided into systemic vs. non-systemic disease. Systemic disease is further split into primary vasculitides (classified by vessel size) and secondary vasculitides (associated with another underlying disease process).

Primary Systemic (by vessel size)
  • Small vessel: microscopic polyangiitis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's), eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss), cryoglobulinemic vasculitis, IgA (Henoch-Schönlein) vasculitis
  • Medium vessel: polyarteritis nodosa (most common necrotizing vasculitis)
  • Large vessel: giant cell arteritis, Takayasu arteritis
Secondary Systemic
  • Connective tissue disease — RA, SLE, Sjögren's, systemic sclerosis, dermatomyositis, MCTD
  • Sarcoidosis, Behçet's disease, inflammatory bowel disease
  • Infection (HBV, HCV, HIV, CMV, leprosy, Lyme, HTLV-1)
  • Drug-induced, malignancy-related
Non-systemic / Isolated PNS ~60% of cases
  • Single-organ vasculitis of peripheral nerves ± surrounding muscle — accounts for nearly 60% of all vasculitic neuropathies
  • Diabetic radiculoplexus neuropathies (lumbosacral, cervical, thoracic; painless diabetic motor neuropathy)
  • Localized cutaneous polyarteritis nodosa
Fig. — Vasculitides organized by predominant vessel size, along a single vascular tree. Large vessel: Takayasu arteritis, giant cell arteritis. Medium vessel: polyarteritis nodosa, Kawasaki disease. ANCA-associated small vessel: microscopic polyangiitis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's), eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Churg-Strauss). Immune complex small vessel: cryoglobulinemic vasculitis, IgA (Henoch-Schönlein) vasculitis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis.
Fig. — Full classification of vasculitides associated with neuropathy: (I) primary systemic vasculitides by vessel size, (II) secondary systemic vasculitides by associated disease, (III) non-systemic/localized vasculitides, including the diabetic radiculoplexus neuropathies. (Gwathmey et al., Lancet Neurology, 2014)

Pathophysiology

Inflammation and ischemia in the vasa nervorum (the vessels of the epineurium) drive an axonopathy — decreased myelinated nerve fiber density, Wallerian-type degeneration, and regenerating axonal clusters.

Nerve biopsy of the epineurial vessels can show: dense transmural inflammatory cell infiltrate with fibrinoid necrosis of the vessel wall; fragmentation of the internal elastic membrane and tunica media; vascular/perivascular hemorrhage; acute thrombosis of the vessel lumen; and axonal degeneration with associated nerve fiber loss.

Systemic or localized inflammatory trigger (autoimmune, infectious, drug, malignancy)
→
Vasculitis of the vasa nervorum (epineurial vessels)
→
Vessel wall necrosis, thrombosis → ischemia of the nerve trunk
→
Axonal (Wallerian-type) degeneration + nerve fiber loss
→
Clinical mononeuropathy multiplex
2 Typical Clinical Presentation

⚠ The pattern that should raise suspicion

A peripheral neuropathy that is acute to subacute in onset, asymmetric, and painful should raise concern for vasculitic neuropathy.

Classic pattern — mononeuritis multiplex

  • Presents classically as multifocal mononeuropathies (mononeuritis multiplex)
  • Most often involves the common fibular (peroneal) nerve in the legs and the ulnar nerve in the arms
  • Typically, the individual mononeuropathies evolve into a symmetrical pattern over time

Patterns of evolution

  • Acute-to-subacute evolving mononeuritis multiplex, or a single mononeuropathy (rare)
  • Overlapping mononeuropathies
  • Asymmetric polyneuropathy in some patients
  • Progression to a symmetric polyneuropathy can follow
  • Course can be relapsing and remitting

Sensory, motor & reflex findings

  • Both sensory and motor fibers can be involved
  • Pain, dysesthesia, and paresthesias — sharp/stabbing pain, burning, or tingling — are typically present; may become hyperalgesic over time
  • Reflexes range from normal to diminished
  • Weakness can be present

⚠ Why this matters

Prompt diagnosis and treatment are important — vasculitic neuropathy can be disabling if left untreated.

💡 Pearl — the three-way distribution pattern

Across this section: Guillain-Barré syndrome is symmetric and ascending (peaks < 4 weeks), CIDP is symmetric proximal + distal (> 8 weeks), and vasculitic neuropathy is the odd one out — asymmetric, painful, and multifocal (mononeuritis multiplex) from the start, only becoming symmetric later in its course.
