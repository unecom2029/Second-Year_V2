Traumatic Spinal Cord Injury

UNE COM · Neurology · Disease Index

🦴 Traumatic Spinal Cord Injury

LO 1–6 M:F ≈ 4:1 Avg. age ~31–41y Cervical most vulnerable
Injury to the spinal cord from external trauma to the vertebral canal or an internally expanding mass lesion. Rarely life-threatening in itself, but potentially devastating — rendering a patient paraplegic or quadriplegic — with recovery closely tied to how quickly the cord is decompressed. Lesions are best framed as compressive (this topic) vs. non-compressive (vascular or inflammatory disease, covered separately).
1 Natural History

Epidemiology

  • Incidence ~28–55 (up to ~40–54 in more recent estimates) per million population; roughly 10,000–17,730 new US cases per year
  • Prevalence estimated at 183,000–300,000 individuals
  • Average age at injury ~31–41 years; M:F ≈ 4:1; the population is trending older over time

Etiology

Acute Compressive
  • Motor vehicle accidents — leading cause (~36–48%)
  • Falls (~17–27%, increasingly common in the elderly)
  • Violence (~5–29%)
  • Recreational/sports activities (~7–16%)
Other Acute/Subacute
  • Iatrogenic (surgical injury)
  • Spinal epidural abscess
  • Spinal epidural hematoma
  • Spinal epidural neoplasm
Chronic Compressive
  • Spondylotic myelopathy — one of the most common causes of progressive cord compression
  • Syringomyelia
Anatomic Notes
  • ~20% of spinal column injuries affect more than one level
  • Cervical spine most vulnerable (mobility, less structural support) — ~2/3 of tSCI involve the cervical cord

Pathophysiology — A Two-Phase Injury

Primary injury: direct mechanical/compressive forces (contusion, compression, laceration, distraction) disrupt neural tissue — immediate neuronal/axonal damage, demyelination, and disruption of the blood-spinal cord barrier.

Secondary injury: unfolds over hours to weeks and is the target of most acute interventions.

Classification Framework
By Location

Intramedullary — within the cord itself. Extramedullary — within the canal but outside the cord.

By Level

Cervical, thoracic, lumbar, or sacral cord / conus medullaris — each with a distinct presentation (see LO2).

By Pattern

Complete transection vs. a set of named incomplete syndromes — Brown-Séquard, central cord, anterior cord, conus medullaris, cauda equina (see LO2).

Prognosis If Untreated / By Initial Severity

Outcome is largely set by initial injury severity. Most patients with a sensorimotor-complete injury (AIS A) show no improvement in neurologic grade and minimal motor recovery at one year. Approximate 1-year AIS conversion (improvement) rates: A → 19%, B → 74%, C → 87%, D → 47%.

Most neurological recovery occurs within the first 6–9 months, fastest in the first 3 months. Thoracic-level and penetrating injuries carry a poorer prognosis. Historically, before modern management, mortality was extremely high — largely from respiratory and urological complications.

2 Typical Clinical Presentation

General Features

  • Sensory loss, numbness, and paresthesias in a dermatomal "level-down" pattern from the point of injury
  • Motor loss — paresis/paralysis with segmental-level flaccidity and spasticity below the lesion (an upper motor neuron pattern)
  • Gait imbalance; bladder and bowel dysfunction; altered sexual function; chronic pain syndromes
  • Respiratory compromise, especially with high cervical lesions

Incomplete Spinal Cord Syndromes
Central Cord (~14%)

Most common incomplete syndrome. Upper extremity weakness > lower extremity weakness. Classic setup: elderly patient, cervical spondylosis, hyperextension injury.

Brown-Séquard (~2%)

Hemisection — ipsilateral motor + proprioceptive loss, contralateral pain/temperature loss. Best prognosis among incomplete syndromes.

Anterior Cord (~6.5%)

Anterior spinal artery territory — motor + pain/temperature loss with dorsal column sparing (proprioception, light touch preserved).

Conus Medullaris

Bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction, perineal (saddle) sensory loss, only mild lower-extremity motor symptoms.

Cauda Equina

Lower motor neuron pattern — flaccid paralysis, areflexic bowel/bladder, variable/asymmetric lower-extremity sensory loss.

3 Diagnostic Workup

Imaging & Physical Exam

CT is the preferred initial study — fracture, subluxation, bony damage. MRI is complementary — ligamentous injury, disk herniation, and intramedullary pathology (edema, hemorrhage, cord compression). CT myelography is used if MRI is contraindicated.

Exam: pin-prick/light-touch sensory testing across dermatomes; motor testing of key muscle groups (C5–T1 upper extremity, L2–S1 lower extremity) graded 0–5; deep tendon reflexes; rectal exam for sacral sparing (voluntary anal contraction, deep anal pressure); vertebral column palpation/percussion.

4 Treatment

Surgical Management

Early decompression (within 24 hours) is associated with significantly better neurological outcomes — more than double the likelihood of a ≥2-point AIS improvement (RR 2.76). Includes removal of compressive elements (bone fragments, disk material, hematoma) and spinal stabilization/instrumentation when appropriate.

Hemodynamic Management

Maintain MAP ≥85 mmHg for up to 7 days post-injury to optimize spinal cord perfusion, generally with ICU monitoring. Use caution with vasopressors (e.g., phenylephrine) — risk of pulmonary edema.

Pharmacologic Therapy — Controversial

Methylprednisolone within the first 8 hours may reduce secondary injury via anti-inflammatory effects per some guidelines, but evidence is conflicting, higher doses carry more complications, and it is not universally recommended.

Cell-Based / Stem Cell Therapy

Among the most promising emerging strategies — trials show feasibility and safety of intraspinal cell transplantation, but no therapy has yet fully restored function.

Neuromodulation

Epidural and functional electrical stimulation show promising improvements in motor, respiratory, and autonomic recovery.

5 Monitoring & Long-Term Complications

System-by-System Complications

  • Cardiovascular: hemodynamic instability; autonomic dysreflexia (injuries above ~T6 — dangerous hypertensive episodes triggered by bladder/bowel distension); thromboembolic disease
  • Respiratory: respiratory failure, atelectasis, pneumonia; ventilator-dependent care for high cervical injuries
  • GI: ileus, constipation/impaction, gastric/duodenal ulcers
  • GU: UTIs (~62% cumulative incidence in year 1), neurogenic bladder requiring catheterization programs
  • Dermatologic: pressure ulcers (~41% cumulative incidence in year 1)
  • Musculoskeletal: osteoporosis (DEXA screening recommended), heterotopic ossification, fractures, overuse syndromes
  • Pain / spasticity: neuropathic + musculoskeletal pain (higher in motor-incomplete injuries); spasticity more common in motor-complete + cervical injuries, occasionally needing intrathecal drug delivery

Monitoring Pearl

Patients with motor-complete injuries (AIS A/B) have significantly higher rates of most secondary complications than motor-incomplete patients — systematic monitoring through the subacute and chronic phases is essential for early detection.

6 Prevention & Health Promotion

Adaptive Equipment & Functional Restoration

  • Adaptive equipment for ADLs, mobility, and independence
  • Phrenic nerve stimulation / diaphragmatic pacing for ventilator-dependent patients with injuries above C4 — freedom from mechanical ventilation, improved speech and mobility, lower healthcare costs
  • Functional electrical stimulation (FES) — muscle conditioning, locomotor training, prevention of disuse atrophy
  • Epidural electrical stimulation — promising results for locomotor and respiratory recovery

Bladder Management

Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) is the preferred method — fewer complications/infections than continuous indwelling catheters. The Brindley-Finetech Vocare bladder system (sacral anterior root stimulator) is an option for bladder emptying in selected patients. Long-term urological surveillance protects renal function and prevents UTIs, hydronephrosis, and urolithiasis.

Skin & Bone

Skin integrity / pressure-relief programs; osteoporosis screening with DEXA + fall prevention.

Bowel & Sexual Health

Bowel continence programs; sexual health and fertility counseling — pregnancy rates in cord-injured individuals have improved over the past decade.

Respiratory & Thermal

Respiratory conditioning/preventive care; thermal dysregulation awareness during exercise, particularly for injuries at T8 and above.

Psychosocial

Psychosocial adaptation, vocational rehabilitation, and recreational skills training.

Primary Prevention (of the Injury Itself)

Motor vehicle safety (seatbelts, airbags), fall prevention in the elderly, violence prevention, and sports safety measures remain the primary strategies for preventing traumatic SCI in the first place.
