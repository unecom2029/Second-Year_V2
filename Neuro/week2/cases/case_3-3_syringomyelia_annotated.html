Eighteen Months After a Whiplash, a Slowly Splitting Cord

36-year-old male, college faculty member · 18-month history of progressive limb weakness and sensory loss · new dyspnea at presentation

Localization Finding Terminology Pattern Workup / Trajectory
Chief Complaint

A 36-year-old man is referred for evaluation by his family physician. He is presenting with progressing weakness and sensory loss in the upper and lower extremities1 and with recent onset of dyspnea2.

History of the Chief Complaint

He first noticed a mild weakness and loss of sensation to temperature in the upper extremities 12 months previously3; the onset of weakness in the lower extremities has occurred in the past 4 months4. He states that both the weakness and the sensory loss have progressively worsened. He has noticed the onset of shortness of breath in the last month.

Medical History

He had enjoyed good health until 18 months ago when he suffered a road traffic accident. While stopped at an intersection he was struck from behind by a driver who was talking on a cell phone. He received a whiplash cervical injury5. Following the accident, a neurological exam was within normal limits, however he complained of persistent headache as well as neck and arm pain. He wore a cervical collar for two months following the accident and still experiences neck pain and paraspinal muscle spasms for which he is taking medication. He has no history of blood transfusions, denies IV drug abuse6, and has not been out of the country except for an occasional trip to Canada. He denies any recent history of noticeable viral or bacterial infections7. He denies any history of respiratory tract illnesses. He denies any change in weight or appetite.

Medications

Voltarin to relieve the neck pain and Flexeril for the paraspinal muscle spasms8.

Social History

He is a faculty member at a small liberal arts college, has never married, and lives alone. He does not smoke or consume alcohol and denies any sexual activity.

Family History

His mother and father are still alive and in good health. He has no siblings.

General Physical Examination

This is an awake, oriented male, appearing older than his stated age and with noticeable muscle wasting in the upper extremities9. His heart rate is 90, blood pressure is 127/85, temperature is 98.7ºF, and respirations are 19. His weight is 181 lbs. During respiratory movements his sternum moves anteriorly, but little lateral motion occurs along the subcostal margins. On inhalation, his sternocleidomastoid muscle becomes prominent10. Respiratory movements are rapid but of short duration. His skin is moist and supple. His chest is clear to auscultation and abdomen is soft to palpation with no tenderness. No lymphadenopathy is detected in the axilla or groin area.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status. He is awake, oriented for person, place and time, and has an appropriate memory and knowledge base. Speech is clear and meaningful. He can follow three- and four-step commands, but is hampered by his weakness.

Cranial Nerves. A full range of eye movements is present11; visual acuity is 30/20 in the right eye and 40/20 in the left eye without glasses. Pupillary reflexes are present to direct and consensual light. Hearing is intact to finger rub at both ears. Gag and corneal reflexes are intact and facial movements are full. The uvula is symmetric and the tongue protrudes on the midline.

Motor Systems. Strength in the upper right extremity is 4/5 at the deltoid, 3/5 at the triceps, 3/5 at the biceps, and 2/5 at the brachioradialis; grip strength is 3/5. In the left upper extremity strength is 4/5 at the deltoid, 3/5 at the triceps, 2/5 at the biceps, and 2/5 at the brachioradialis; grip strength is 4/5. The lower extremity strength exam finds the quadriceps at 3/5, the gastroc at 3/5 and anterior tibialis at 4/5 on the right. On the left, the quadriceps is 2/5, the gastroc is 3/5 and anterior tibialis is 4/5. Both upper extremities have diminished deep tendon reflexes at the elbow and wrist; muscular fasciculations and atrophy are present bilaterally12. Both lower extremities have elevated deep tendon reflexes, and the plantar reflex is extensor bilaterally13.

Sensory Exam. Patient lacks sensation to temperature and pinprick in a cape-like distribution14 over the chest and shoulders, extending throughout the upper extremities to the fingertips. The upper limit of the band of analgesia is approximately C6 and the lower limit is T115. Vibratory sense, discriminative touch, and proprioception were intact16 throughout his chest and upper extremities. Normal sensation was found elsewhere over the body.

Follow-up

Examination in three months finds analgesia spreading onto his chest wall as low as T317; the weakness in his extremities has increased, with notable loss of deep tendon reflexes in the upper extremity and increased deep tendon reflexes in the lower extremity18. No change in mental status is noted19.

Questions
  1. Has the patient experienced any changes in consciousness or cognition, language function or spatial recognition, personality or emotional behavior, or any loss in memory?
  2. Does the patient exhibit any loss of vision and if so, where in the visual field is the loss? Are there any visual hallucinations present in this patient?
  3. Are there any changes in cranial nerve function and if so, are they signs of suprasegmental or segmental level defects?
  4. Are there any changes in motor function such as reflexes, muscle tone, movement, or coordination and if so, are they signs of suprasegmental or segmental level defects?
  5. Are there any changes in sensory function and if so, what levels of the body have experienced this change?
  6. What is the clinical temporal profile of this patient's neurologic problem: is the onset of neurologic findings acute or insidious; is the course of the neurologic disease chronically progressive, fluctuant or stable?
  7. Based on the presenting signs and symptoms do you think the distribution of the neurologic pathology is focal, multifocal or diffuse?
  8. Based on the answers to these questions develop a differential diagnosis of the patient's neurological problem?
  9. If the origin of the pathology is vascular, what arterial supply is most likely involved with the lesion in this patient?
Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough
Working through the case questions above in order — where, how far, how fast, why, what to order, what happens next, and what to do about it.
1. Localization

A central cervical cord lesion

The signature clue is dissociated sensory loss — pain and temperature gone, vibration/touch/proprioception intact — which only happens when a lesion selectively damages the crossing anterolateral fibers near the central canal while sparing the peripherally-located dorsal columns.

Layered on top: lower motor neuron (segmental) signs in the arms at the lesion's own level — atrophy, fasciculations, hyporeflexia — plus upper motor neuron (suprasegmental) signs in the legs below the lesion — hyperreflexia, spasticity, Babinski. That's the classic central cord syndrome signature of a process expanding outward from the middle of the cord. A normal cranial nerve exam and unchanged mental status confirm it never extends above the cervical cord.

2. Distribution

Focal, but actively enlarging

Not multifocal — this is one contiguous process, not scattered lesions like MS plaques. Not diffuse either — a discrete sensory level exists rather than symmetric whole-body involvement.

The lesion began confined to roughly C6–T1 and has since extended down to T3 on follow-up, consistent with a single expanding intramedullary process rather than several independent lesions.

3. Time Course

Chronic, steadily progressive

No fluctuation or remission at any point. The first symptom (temperature loss in the hands) appeared roughly six months after the whiplash injury, and every subsequent finding has only added onto the picture — never resolved.

That slow, one-directional trajectory over 18 months fits a structural lesion steadily enlarging far better than an inflammatory or vascular process, both of which tend to declare themselves faster and often fluctuate.

4. Differential Diagnosis

Ranking the possibilities

DiagnosisFitsArgues against
Post-traumatic cervical syringomyelia
Most likely		 Whiplash injury with a 6–12 month latency before symptoms; slowly progressive cape-like dissociated sensory loss; segmental (LMN) signs at the lesion level plus suprasegmental (UMN) signs below it; measurable interval growth (T1→T3 sensory level). Diagnosis ultimately requires MRI confirmation — can't be made on history and exam alone.
Intramedullary spinal cord tumor (astrocytoma/ependymoma) Can produce an identical central-cord picture: dissociated sensory loss, mixed UMN/LMN signs, slow progression. Less specific temporal link to a discrete inciting trauma; ultimately distinguished from a syrinx only by imaging, not by history or exam.
Cervical spondylotic myelopathy Prior neck trauma, chronic neck pain, "myelopathy hand" weakness pattern, spastic gait. Doesn't typically cause a sharp, bilateral, dissociated sensory level like this one.
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) Mixed UMN + LMN signs, fasciculations, muscle atrophy, respiratory muscle involvement. ALS spares all sensory modalities by definition — this patient's dissociated sensory loss and sensory level essentially exclude it.
Subacute combined degeneration (B12 deficiency) Can cause combined UMN + LMN signs. Classically hits the dorsal columns first (vibration/proprioception loss) with pain/temperature spared — the exact opposite of this patient's dissociation pattern.
Multiple sclerosis Progressive neurologic deficits in a younger adult. Usually relapsing-remitting rather than steadily progressive at this age; doesn't typically produce an isolated dissociated sensory level without other white-matter findings.
5. Diagnostic Workup

What to order, in order

  1. MRI cervical & thoracic spine, with and without contrast. Confirms and characterizes a syrinx directly (a fluid-signal cavity within the cord), defines its rostrocaudal extent, and distinguishes it from an enhancing mass that would need a very different workup.
  2. MRI brain (posterior fossa). Screens for an associated Chiari I malformation, the most common co-driver of syringomyelia even when trauma is also in the picture; a positive finding changes surgical planning.
  3. Serum B12 / methylmalonic acid. Quick, inexpensive way to exclude subacute combined degeneration, especially given the overlapping UMN + LMN picture.
  4. EMG / nerve conduction studies. Characterizes the denervation pattern in the hands; sensory nerve action potentials (SNAPs) should be normal since the lesion is central rather than in the peripheral nerves — helps exclude a peripheral neuropathy or plexopathy mimic.
  5. Pulmonary function testing. Given his new dyspnea and accessory-muscle breathing pattern, baseline spirometry quantifies respiratory muscle involvement and gives a number to track if the lesion keeps expanding.
6. Prognosis

Slow, but not static

Left untreated, a syrinx tends to enlarge slowly but relentlessly — this patient's own three-month follow-up already documents that growth in real time. Expect continued caudal spread of the sensory level, worsening leg spasticity, and — most concerning given his current breathing pattern — a real risk of progressive respiratory compromise if the lesion reaches phrenic-innervating segments or enough thoracic intercostal levels.

Surgical decompression or shunting can halt progression and sometimes produce partial recovery, but outcomes are best before extensive, longstanding cord damage accumulates — an argument for urgent rather than routine imaging here.

7. Management

Supportive first, definitive next

  • Physical/occupational therapy — hand strengthening, adaptive equipment for grip weakness, gait and safety training for the spastic legs
  • Spasticity management — stretching program now; consider baclofen or tizanidine if leg spasticity becomes functionally limiting
  • Continue symptomatic care for chronic neck pain/spasm (current NSAID + muscle relaxant regimen)
  • Baseline and serial pulmonary function testing given his dyspnea and accessory muscle use
  • Urgent neurosurgical referral for MRI-based evaluation of shunt or decompression candidacy
  • Post-operative rehabilitation and continued neurologic monitoring if surgery is pursued
Concept Deep-Dive
The tract-level anatomy that explains why this particular combination of findings points where it does — figures pulled from the chapter, plus one schematic for the mechanism itself.
Pattern

Anterolateral (Spinothalamic) System

Figure 3-9, Medical Neuroanatomy Ch. 3

The axons carrying pain and temperature cross to the opposite side almost immediately, in the anterior white commissure right next to the central canal — the first structure a centrally-expanding cavity encounters. That's exactly why this patient lost pain and temperature bilaterally, in a band matching the levels the process currently occupies, before almost anything else was affected.

Localization

Dorsal Column–Medial Lemniscal System

Figure 3-6, Medical Neuroanatomy Ch. 3

These fibers for vibration, discriminative touch, and proprioception travel up the periphery of the cord, tucked against the dorsal surface, far from the central canal. A syrinx has to grow enormous before it reaches this far outward — exactly why this patient's dorsal column function stayed completely normal through 18 months and two documented exams, and why any future loss here would be a late, worrying sign.

Localization

Lateral Corticospinal Tract

Figure 3-8, Medical Neuroanatomy Ch. 3

These fibers run in the lateral funiculus — further from the central canal than the anterior commissure, but still well within reach of a moderately-enlarged syrinx. Compression here produces this patient's leg spasticity, hyperreflexia, and Babinski sign: upper motor neuron signs appearing below the level of his central lesion, exactly as this tract's anatomy predicts.

Pattern

Segmental vs. Suprasegmental Injury

Figure 3-11, Medical Neuroanatomy Ch. 3

A segmental (lower motor neuron) lesion denervates muscle directly, producing flaccid weakness, atrophy, and lost reflexes — this patient's arms. A suprasegmental (upper motor neuron) lesion leaves the local reflex arc intact but removes its descending brake, producing spastic weakness and hyperreflexia — his legs. Having both signs at once, at two different levels of the same body, is the calling card of a single lesion sitting between those two levels.

Trajectory

How a Syrinx Grows

Crossing fibers (commissure) Anterior horns Lateral corticospinal tract Dorsal columns

A syrinx starts as a small cavity at the central canal and expands outward in a fairly predictable order — first the crossing anterolateral fibers, then the anterior horns, then the corticospinal tracts, and only very late the dorsal columns. This patient's 18-month history — sensory loss first, then hand weakness/atrophy, then leg spasticity, then dyspnea — traces almost exactly along that same anatomic sequence.

Trajectory

Topography and the Growing "Cape"

Figure 3-10, Medical Neuroanatomy Ch. 3

Within the anterolateral tract, fibers from cervical dermatomes sit medially and fibers from more caudal dermatomes stack on laterally. As a central syrinx enlarges outward, it recruits progressively more lateral (i.e. lower) fibers into the lesion — exactly why this patient's band of analgesia crept downward from a C6–T1 border to as low as T3 over just three months. The topography itself predicts the direction of clinical spread.
