Sudden Paraplegia in an 82-Year-Old Man

Working Dx: Anterior Spinal Artery Syndrome (Thoracic Cord Infarction)

Chief Complaint

An 82-year-old, right-handed man with a long history of illness was brought to the emergency room by his family; he was in acute distress with back pain, and unable to walk.

History of Complaint

Two days prior, the patient experienced severe back pain radiating into both legs that remitted promptly when lying down1. The next day he experienced similar transient pain in the back and the legs. Later that day, while experiencing an episode of severe back pain, he lost all strength in his legs2 and was rushed to the hospital.

Medical History

The patient had a previous history of transurethral prostatectomy and bilateral orchiectomy for carcinoma of the prostate3, left hemicolectomy for adenocarcinoma of the rectum, and arteriosclerotic heart disease and congestive heart failure4.

General Physical Examination

He was stable and reclining in bed. He was awake, cooperative, afebrile, and appeared older than his stated age. Funduscopic examination revealed bilateral ocular opacities obscuring visualization of the fundi. External auditory canals were patent. No cervical lymphadenopathy was detected. Blood pressure was 160/90 mmHg, pulse rate was 48 beats per minute with occasional premature beats5. There was a grade 2 blowing apical systolic murmur6. Bilateral basilar crackles were present in the lungs on inspiration and bilateral jugular venous distention7 was demonstrable in the neck. Peripheral pulses were intact and equal at the wrists and ankles. Pitting pretibial edema was present. A colostomy stoma8 was present in the lower left quadrant of the abdomen. Otherwise the abdomen was soft to palpation with normal bowel sounds and no aortic bruits.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status. He was alert and oriented to person, place and time; memory and affect were appropriate for his age9. Speech was clear and meaningful. He showed appropriate concern for his condition and was a good historian.

Cranial Nerves. His visual fields were intact and eye movements were full10; hearing, to finger rub, was diminished bilaterally. His pupillary, corneal, and gag reflexes were intact; facial expressions were appropriate; uvula elevated symmetrically and tongue protruded on the midline. When asked, he could elevate his shoulders symmetrically with appropriate strength11.

Motor Systems. His strength and muscle tone were absent in both lower extremities12 and deep tendon reflexes were absent at the knee and ankle13. His strength and reflexes in the upper extremities were appropriate for his age14. Grip strength was intact. His urinary bladder was atonic15, however, this had been present since his last surgery.

Sensory Exam. There was a well-defined sensory level at T1016, below which he had lost sensation to pinprick and temperature17. Touch, vibratory, and position sense were intact throughout his body and face18.

Follow-up

He was treated with steroids and supportive measures without improvement19. Five weeks after the onset of paraplegia he died from a sudden cardiorespiratory arrest20.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

From Exam Findings to Diagnosis

01 · Localization

Thoracic cord, anterior 2/3

Intact mental status, cranial nerves, and upper-extremity strength and reflexes place the lesion below the cervical cord. The sharp T10 sensory level plus bilateral flaccid paraplegia with absent knee/ankle reflexes, alongside preserved touch, vibration, and position sense, localizes to a transverse lesion of the anterior two-thirds of the thoracic cord — sparing the dorsal columns.

02 · Distribution

Focal, single level

A single, sharply demarcated level rather than a multifocal (e.g., demyelinating) or diffuse, length-dependent (e.g., polyneuropathy) process. The clean bilateral symmetry and transverse level point to one vascular territory, not a patchy or peripheral distribution.

03 · Time Course

Acute, over ~48 hours

Two days of transient, position-dependent back and leg pain escalated to sudden, fixed flaccid paraplegia during a pain episode. This stuttering-then-abrupt pattern fits an evolving vascular occlusion far better than the days-to-weeks course of myelitis or the insidious course of a slow-growing mass.

04 · Differential Diagnosis
DiagnosisFitsArgues against
Complete spinal cord transection The emergency team's initial impression — flaccid, areflexic, no pain/temperature below T10, exactly what a transection looks like Touch, vibration, and position sense are fully preserved; a true transection cannot spare the dorsal columns
Most likelyAnterior spinal artery syndrome, from aortic disease Abrupt onset after transient prodromal pain; dissociated sensory loss with dorsal columns spared; flaccid paraplegia; heavy atherosclerotic disease burden; sharp T10 cutoff matching the known limit of the great medullary artery's territory No angiographic confirmation reported — the diagnosis here is clinical and inferential
Pathologic vertebral collapse (metastatic) Known prostate and rectal cancer, either of which can seed vertebral bone; a collapsed body can retropulse a fragment into the cord Preserved vibratory/position sense is unusual for a mechanical/compressive injury severe enough to cause complete flaccid paraplegia
Cardioembolic event (mural thrombus) Failing heart with a murmur — a plausible source of embolus The vessels feeding the anterior spinal artery branch off the aorta at sharp angles that emboli tend not to follow, making this anatomically unlikely
Osteoporotic compression fracture Elderly, likely sedentary and osteopenic; a collapsed vertebra could buckle a fragment into the canal without any tumor at all Same objection as the metastatic version — doesn't explain preserved dorsal column function
05 · Diagnostic Workup
  1. Emergent MRI of the thoracic spine, with and without contrast.The single most urgent test — a compressive lesion (metastatic or osteoporotic vertebral collapse) must be excluded before attributing the deficit to a vascular cause. Also the study that would have caught the preserved-dorsal-column pattern immediately.
  2. Look for the classic cord-infarct MRI pattern.A T2-hyperintense signal confined to the anterior cord, sparing the dorsal columns, supports infarction in the anterior spinal artery territory, and directly confirms this isn't a transection.
  3. CT angiography of the aorta, prioritized over a cardiac workup.Given his exam, an abdominal aortic aneurysm that has dissected along the origin of the great medullary artery is the leading mechanism — far more likely here than an embolus, since these vessels leave the aorta at sharp angles that emboli don't tend to follow.
  4. If the aneurysm is chronic, look for collateral anastomoses before any repair.Long-standing flow reduction can recruit collateral vessels between levels; modern imaging can confirm these exist so a surgeon can safely address the aneurysm without also cutting off the cord's remaining supply.
  5. CSF analysis, if MRI is unrevealing or atypical.Helps exclude an infectious or inflammatory myelitis; cord infarction usually shows a normal or only mildly elevated protein.
06 · Prognosis

Guarded — and permanently flaccid

If the occluded vessel is the dominant supply below the thoracolumbar junction, the infarct doesn't stop at one level — it takes out the ventral horn all the way down to the conus. That matters for what recovery could ever look like: the textbook expectation that a cord lesion evolves from flaccid to spastic below the level assumes there's cord left below to generate that reflex activity. If the infarct destroys the gray matter all the way to the conus, as his likely did, there is no distal segment left to become spastic — he would have stayed flaccid indefinitely. He did not improve with steroids and died five weeks later of cardiorespiratory arrest, a reflection of his systemic vascular disease burden rather than a direct complication of the myelopathy.

07 · Management
  • Bladder management — catheterization and infection monitoring, compounded by his pre-existing atonic bladder
  • Bowel regimen, coordinated with his existing colostomy
  • DVT and pressure-ulcer prophylaxis given immobility
  • Pulmonary toilet, given his basilar crackles and heart failure
  • Physical therapy, rehab positioning, caregiver education
  • Corticosteroids, as trialed here — of unproven benefit once infarction is complete, unlike in inflammatory myelitis
  • Set realistic rehab expectations: if the infarct reaches the conus, don't count on the usual flaccid-to-spastic conversion — he may stay flaccid, which changes bracing and care planning
  • Optimize cardiovascular risk factors and cardiac status
  • Surgery only if a compressive lesion is identified on imaging
Concept Deep-Dive

The Neuroanatomy Behind the Case

Vascular Anatomy

Arterial Supply to the Spinal Cord

A schematic based on the classic cord cross-section: one anterior spinal artery feeds the front two-thirds of the cord through central branches, while paired posterior spinal arteries feed the dorsal columns — the anatomic basis for this patient's dissociated deficit.

Localization

Anterior 2/3 vs. Posterior 1/3 Territory

This patient lost strength and pain/temperature sensation below T10 but kept touch, vibration, and position sense — exactly what you'd expect if only the anterior two-thirds of the cord, the anterior spinal artery's territory, was infarcted.

Vascular Anatomy

The Artery of Adamkiewicz & Watershed Risk

The cervical cord gets 3–4 radiculomedullary feeders; the thoracolumbar cord typically gets only one — the artery of Adamkiewicz — which is why this territory infarcts so much more easily. And because this vessel leaves the aorta at a sharp angle that emboli rarely negotiate, the classic culprit isn't a clot from the heart — it's a dissecting abdominal aortic aneurysm shearing across the vessel's origin, which is almost certainly what happened here.

Pattern Recognition

Flaccid → Spastic — Unless There's No Cord Left

This is the textbook rule — but it assumes there's cord left below the lesion to generate that hyperreflexia. His infarct likely ran all the way down to the conus, destroying the ventral horn itself rather than just the tract above it. With no distal segment left, he would have had nothing to become spastic with; he'd have stayed flaccid indefinitely. Treating "it'll turn spastic eventually" as a universal rule is exactly the naive assumption to avoid.

Localization

Sensory Levels as Landmarks

A "sensory level" is described by dermatome, and T10 lines up with the umbilicus. Finding the level clinically — as was done here — is often the fastest way to localize a cord lesion before any imaging is obtained.

Pattern Recognition

Cord Infarct vs. Epidural Compression

FeatureCord infarction (ASA syndrome)Epidural compression
OnsetMinutes to hours, often after a pain prodrome that resolves with restDays, progressive
PainTransient, positional, then an abrupt fixed deficitPersistent, often worse lying down; may follow a radicular band
Sensory patternDissociated — pain/temperature lost, touch/vibration/position sparedUsually all modalities affected together
Early reflexesAbsent (spinal shock)Variable, depends on severity
Steroid responseTypically noneOften improves if inflammatory edema is present

Given his cancer history, this patient's presentation had to be checked against compression — but his dissociated sensory loss and lack of steroid response both point toward the vascular column instead.

Pattern Recognition

Same Sensory Pattern, Two Different Lesions

FeatureCentral cord syndromeThis patient (ASA syndrome)
PatternMotor loss + protopathic (pain/temp) loss + preserved epicritic (touch/vibration/position) senseThe same pattern: motor loss + protopathic loss + preserved epicritic sense
MechanismAn expanding intrinsic lesion (e.g., syrinx) growing into the anterior white commissure and ventral horn from the inside outAn extrinsic vascular event — the anterior spinal artery's territory infarcts while the posterior third is untouched
LevelClassically cervicalThoracic here, but the mechanism can strike any level the anterior spinal artery supplies
Why they look alikeBoth take out the same two structures — the corticospinal tract and the crossing spinothalamic fibers near the anterior cord — while leaving the posterior columns alone. Identical sensory dissociation, completely different disease.

A dissociated sensory pattern tells you *what part* of the cord is damaged, not *why*. The same protopathic-loss-with-epicritic-sparing pattern shows up whether the anterior cord is being invaded from within or starved of blood from without — level, time course, and history are what separate the two.
