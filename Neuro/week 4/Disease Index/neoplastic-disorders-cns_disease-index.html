Classification of CNS Neoplasms

Orienting framework for every disease in this section — not itself a graded LO, so no vignette or quiz here. This is the classification tree the whole index hangs off of; come back to it whenever a question turns on which family a tumor belongs to.

Why This Matters

Malignant gliomas make up about 70% of malignant primary brain tumors diagnosed annually. Survival is grim without treatment: 12–15 months for glioblastoma, 2–5 years for anaplastic gliomas. Classification used to be based purely on histopathology and aggressiveness grading; it has since been revolutionized by cell genetics, and further changes are expected as more tumor-associated genes are discovered.

New WHO Classification of Adult-Type Diffuse Gliomas (2021)
Basis: IDH Enzyme Status

The 2021 WHO classification is built on detailed gene analysis plus histopathology, giving far more accurate diagnosis/prognosis and enabling individualized treatment. It has become the world standard for tumor diagnosis.

Classification hinges on the isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) enzyme:

  • Wildtype (IDH-wt): catalyzes isocitrate → alpha-ketoglutarate (normal function)
  • Mutant (IDH-mt): metabolizes isocitrate → 2-hydroxyglutarate, an oncometabolite that prevents DNA demethylation, leading to widespread hypermethylation

IDH-Mutant Tumors — Less Aggressive

  • 40+% of Grade 2–3 astrocytomas are IDH-mt
  • Only ~2% of Grade 4 astrocytomas are IDH-mt
  • Tend to appear in younger patients
  • Median age at dx: Grade 3 IDH-mt astrocytoma = 37; Grade 4 IDH-mt astrocytoma = 47

Subdivision Logic

IDH-wt gliomas are graded 2/3 vs. 4 by histocytology, with further differentiation possible via EGFR and TERT promoter mutations. Grade 3 = "anaplastic astrocytes"; Grade 4 = termed "secondary glioblastomas" in this source.

IDH-mt (IDH1/IDH2) gliomas are subdivided by presence/absence of the 1p/19q codeletion:

  • No codeletion → graded 2/3/4 by histocytology; a CDKN2A mutation reclassifies the tumor as Grade 4 Glioblastoma (a.k.a. primary glioblastoma/glioblastoma multiforme)
  • 1p/19q codeletion present → classified as oligodendroglioma, Grade 2 or 3
Pearl

All Grade 3 gliomas are termed anaplastic gliomas — defined by increased cell density, atypical cell nuclei, vascular proliferation, and necrotic cells.

Source discrepancy — flagged, not resolved

This page states Grade 4 IDH-wildtype gliomas are "typically termed secondary glioblastomas." Conventionally, primary/de novo GBM is IDH-wildtype and secondary GBM (progressing from a lower-grade astrocytoma) is IDH-mutant — the opposite pairing. Preserved here verbatim from the source since it may be tested as written; worth double-checking against the professor's slides or asking in class if this looks like a typo.

Overview: Glioma Grading & Classification

Category-level framework for the five glioma panels in this section — not itself a graded LO, so no vignette or quiz here.

Definition

Gliomas are the most common of the primary brain tumors, including astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, and ependymomas. Two major forms are recognized:

  • Narrow, non-infiltrating distributions — e.g. pilocytic astrocytoma
  • Diffuse distributions — termed infiltrating astrocytomas and oligodendrogliomas

Grading is based on histopathology and genetics, and is a key criterion for diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment approach.

Non-Infiltrating Astrocytomas

Grade I — Pilocytic Astrocytoma

Low proliferation, greatest possibility for cure of any glioma. Often has alterations in the MAPK pathway — the BRAF gene and the FGFR1 gene.

Infiltrating Astrocytomas

IDH-Wildtype

20–30% of low-grade gliomas are IDH-wildtype — and despite being "low-grade," these are typically more aggressive with worse prognosis than the equivalent-grade IDH-mutant form.

  • Grade II — diffuse/low-grade astrocytoma & oligodendroglioma: increased cell proliferation and infiltration
  • Grade III — diffuse anaplastic astrocytoma & anaplastic oligodendroglioma: increased proliferation and infiltration
  • Grade IV — glioblastoma (a.k.a. glioblastoma multiforme): severe proliferation and infiltration

IDH-Mutant (IDH1 or IDH2)

70–80% of low-grade gliomas are the IDH-mutant form — these are generally less aggressive than their equivalent-grade wildtype counterpart.

  • Grade II/III IDH1/IDH2 — astrocytoma and oligodendroglioma
  • Grade IV IDH1/IDH2
Two different denominators — don't merge these stats

This page states IDH-mt tumors are 70–80% of low-grade gliomas overall, while the Classification overview states IDH-mt tumors are "40+%" of Grade 2–3 astrocytomas specifically. Both are transcribed as given rather than reconciled — they likely reflect different denominators (all low-grade gliomas, including oligodendrogliomas which skew heavily IDH-mt/codeleted, vs. astrocytomas alone).

Pilocytic Astrocytoma

LO 1–3 WHO Grade I Most Curable Glioma BRAF Alteration ~70–75%
How boards would word it

"A 10-year-old boy is brought in by his parents for a two-month history of morning headaches with vomiting and increasing clumsiness. His parents report he has been bumping into furniture and has developed a wide-based, unsteady gait. Examination reveals dysmetria on finger-to-nose testing and truncal ataxia. Fundoscopic exam shows bilateral papilledema. MRI of the brain shows a well-circumscribed cystic mass in the right cerebellar hemisphere with an enhancing mural nodule, displacing the fourth ventricle. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Pilocytic astrocytoma (WHO Grade I)
What to look for: peak age 8–13 plus cerebellar signs (ataxia, dysmetria) plus signs of obstructive hydrocephalus/raised ICP (morning headache, vomiting, papilledema from a posterior fossa mass) plus the classic MRI pattern of a cystic lesion with an enhancing mural nodule together clinch this diagnosis. The lateralized cerebellar hemisphere location is what separates it from medulloblastoma, which classically arises in the midline vermis.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition & Epidemiology

Two major forms of astrocyte-derived tumors are recognized: narrow, non-infiltrating (pilocytic astrocytoma) and diffuse (traditionally termed "astrocytomas"). Pilocytic astrocytoma is the WHO Grade I glioma — a relatively benign, slow-growing astrocytoma of children, typically seen in the cerebellum. Synonyms: spongioblastoma, juvenile astrocytoma.

Accounts for ~6% of intracranial tumors; incidence >1 per 100,000/year. It is the most frequent brain tumor of infants, with peak incidence at 8–13 years of age.

Location by Age

  • Older children: cerebral hemispheres and spinal cord
  • Younger children: optic nerve, optic chiasm, brainstem, cerebellum

Frequency of Distribution (Descending)

  • Cerebellum
  • Optic nerve and chiasm
  • Cerebral hemisphere — medial temporal lobe
  • Brainstem
Pathophysiology
MAPK pathway alteration — BRAF gene (~70–75% of cases)
→
Low-proliferation, well-circumscribed astrocytoma
→
Slow growth over months–years
→
Greatest possibility for cure of any glioma
Prognosis if Untreated

Because growth is slow, clinical history often precedes diagnosis by months to years. Prognosis with resection is favorable — this is the most curable glioma, with median survival >10 years and cure possible with complete surgical resection.

LO2 — Clinical Presentation
Presentation by Location
  • Cerebellum: ataxia + increased ICP (morning headache, vomiting, papilledema)
  • Optic pathway: decreasing visual acuity
  • Hypothalamus: endocrine abnormalities
  • Brainstem: cranial nerve deficits
  • Cerebral hemisphere: seizures + increased ICP
Pearl — Localization

Pilocytic astrocytoma is more typically lateralized within a cerebellar hemisphere; medulloblastoma is more typically midline (vermian) — useful for distinguishing the two both by exam findings and on imaging.

How boards would word it

"A 58-year-old man presents with three weeks of worsening headaches, subtle personality changes noted by his wife, and one episode of word-finding difficulty. He has no significant past medical history. Examination reveals mild expressive aphasia. MRI of the brain shows an infiltrative, non-necrotic mass centered in the left temporal lobe extending into the frontal lobe, with mild surrounding edema and no significant contrast enhancement. Answer: Aggressive course with a worse prognosis than an equivalent-grade IDH-mutant astrocytoma
What to look for: an older adult (median age ~55) with an infiltrative temporal/frontal lobe astrocytoma lacking necrosis or vascular proliferation looks histologically "lower-grade" — but IDH-wildtype status alone predicts a dismal, aggressive course with poor treatment response, worse than the same histologic grade would suggest if it carried an IDH mutation instead.
T2W, T2 FLAIR, T1 post-contrast, and ADC map — an IDH1-wildtype diffuse astrocytoma (WHO Grade II) in the brainstem, with deep white matter invasion and <33% necrosis
LO1 — Natural History
Definition & Epidemiology

Grade II/III astrocytomas carrying wildtype IDH are rare but aggressive tumors that typically occur in older individuals, with a worse prognosis than the equivalent-grade IDH-mutant (IDH1/2) astrocytoma. Synonyms: well-differentiated astrocytoma, fibrillary diffuse astrocytoma, low-grade astrocytoma, "astrocytoma" (Grade II); Grade III gliomas are often termed anaplastic gliomas.

This IDH-wt Grade II/III subgroup is a heterogeneous, rare form of diffuse tumor. Median age at diagnosis ~55 years; males > females. Prognosis is dismal with poor response to known treatments — Grade III tumors show greater metastatic potential and shorter overall survival than Grade II tumors.

Pathology / Distribution

Broad distribution in the brain; commonly the temporal lobe extending into the base of the frontal lobe. Infiltrative, with low-to-moderate cellular density.

Histopathology (Grade II → III)

Cellular and nuclear atypia; hypercellularity — both worsen going from Grade II to Grade III. Lacking significant features of Grade IV glioma such as vascular proliferation and tissue necrosis.

Genetics
  • TERT promoter (TERTp) mutation — Telomerase Reverse Transcriptase promoter
  • EGFR mutation — Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor
  • PTEN — Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog
  • TP53 — the most common mutation (49%) in low-grade gliomas; drives progression to higher grade
  • CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion — cyclin-dependent kinase; leads to a rapidly growing tumor that transforms to glioblastoma (Grade IV) with very poor prognosis
  • 7+, 10− — chromosome 7 gain, chromosome 9 loss
Reclassification Trigger

If an IDH-wt Grade II/III glioma expresses TERTp mutation, EGFR mutation, or the 7+, 10− chromosomal signature, it is reclassified as a Grade IV Glioblastoma — termed "secondary glioblastoma" in this source.

LO2 — Clinical Presentation
Presentation (Similar for Grade II and III)
  • Seizures
  • Headaches
  • Cognitive changes
  • Focal neurologic signs related to tumor location
Pearl

Don't be fooled by a "low-grade-looking" astrocytoma on histology in an older patient — IDH-wildtype status alone is enough to predict an aggressive course, independent of grade. Always check IDH status, not just histologic grade.

Glioma IDH-wt Grade IV (Glioblastoma)

LO 1–2 Most Common Malignant Primary Brain Tumor "GBM" — Term No Longer Recommended Median OS 12–18 mo (Treated)
How boards would word it

"A 66-year-old man presents with a two-week history of progressively worsening headaches, new-onset word-finding difficulty, and one witnessed focal seizure with secondary generalization. His wife also notes several weeks of personality change and increasing forgetfulness. Examination shows mild right-sided pronator drift, expressive aphasia, and papilledema. MRI of the brain with gadolinium reveals a large, heterogeneous, ring-enhancing mass in the left frontal lobe with a necrotic center, extending across the corpus callosum into the right frontal lobe, surrounded by extensive T2/FLAIR signal abnormality. Molecular testing shows no IDH1/IDH2 mutation. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, WHO Grade 4
What to look for: an older adult (median age ~64–66) with a subacute, days-to-weeks progression of headache, cognitive/personality change, and focal deficits, plus the classic "butterfly glioma" MRI pattern — a ring-enhancing, necrotic mass crossing the corpus callosum with surrounding vasogenic edema — is the textbook presentation of IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, the most common and most aggressive primary malignant brain tumor.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

Diffuse IDH-wildtype Grade 4 gliomas — glioblastoma — are the most common form of primary malignant brain tumor and the most aggressive in local spread. The tumor carries a normally functioning (wildtype) IDH enzyme but expresses at least one of: EGFR amplification, TERT promoter mutation, or the 7+, 10− chromosomal signature. Its older name, "glioblastoma multiforme," is no longer recommended.

Epidemiology

  • Most common and most malignant glioma; >90% of all glioblastomas are IDH-wildtype
  • Incidence ~3–6 per 100,000/year; >50% of all primary malignant brain tumors, ~14% of all CNS tumors
  • Median age at diagnosis 64–66 years; incidence peaks at 74–84
  • Males > females (~1.3:1); highest in non-Hispanic white populations
  • 1-year overall survival ~53.7%

Etiology

  • Sporadic in the vast majority of cases; cause largely unknown
  • Only well-established environmental risk factor: ionizing radiation (e.g. prior cranial irradiation)
  • <5% familial — Li-Fraumeni (TP53), NF1, Lynch/Turcot syndrome, Cowden disease (PTEN)
  • Atopic disease history (asthma, eczema, allergies) is inversely associated with risk
  • No convincing link to cell phone use, occupational exposure, or diet
Pathology & Diagnostic Criteria

Per WHO 2021, diagnosis requires a diffusely infiltrative IDH-wildtype astrocytic glioma with either classic Grade 4 histology (necrosis and/or microvascular proliferation) or at least one molecular hallmark: EGFR amplification, combined gain of chromosome 7/loss of chromosome 10 (+7/−10), or a TERT promoter mutation.

Rapid growth, diffuse infiltration, high recurrence rate — most commonly supratentorial white matter. Highly infiltrative along white matter tracts, perivascular spaces, and the subpial zone, making complete resection essentially impossible.

Histopathology
  • Cellular pleomorphism
  • Nuclear atypia, frequent mitotic figures
  • Microvascular proliferation
  • Pseudopalisading necrosis
Molecular Alterations & Prognostic Markers

Frequently co-occurring alterations: EGFR amplification, PTEN mutation/loss, CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion, TERT promoter mutation. MGMT promoter methylation (~35–45% of cases) is an important favorable prognostic biomarker. IDH-wildtype status means these tumors lack the D-2-hydroxyglutarate oncometabolite and the associated G-CIMP phenotype that characterizes the more favorable IDH-mutant gliomas.

Prognosis if Untreated

Without any treatment (best supportive care only), prognosis is uniformly fatal and rapidly progressive — historical trials showed median survival of only 3.5–5.2 months with supportive care after surgery alone (e.g. the ANOCEF trial: 3.9 months in patients >70; the Brain Tumour Study Group 69-01 trial: ~14 weeks).

Even with standard treatment, median OS improves only to ~12–17 months, and 5-year survival is just ~2–7%. MGMT-methylated tumors: ~17.1 months vs. ~12.4 months for unmethylated tumors — MGMT status is one of the strongest prognostic factors, alongside age, performance status, and extent of resection.

LO2 — Clinical Presentation
Presentation

Symptoms typically develop over days to weeks, reflecting the tumor's rapid growth kinetics — much faster than the insidious course of lower-grade gliomas.

  • Headache (~30–38%): more common than in low-grade gliomas; often progressive, worse in the morning, with nausea/vomiting
  • Cognitive decline / behavioral change (~36%): memory impairment, personality change, executive dysfunction, confusion — especially with frontal or temporal involvement
  • Seizures (~24%): new focal seizures ± secondary generalization — notably less common here than in lower-grade gliomas (24% vs. 58%)
  • Focal neurologic deficits (~20% each for motor deficits and aphasia): hemiparesis, hemisensory loss, visual field cuts, language disturbance
  • Signs of raised ICP: papilledema, nausea/vomiting, altered consciousness — especially with obstructive hydrocephalus
Classic Imaging Appearance

On neuroimaging, glioblastoma classically appears as a large, heterogeneous, ring-enhancing mass with central necrosis on gadolinium-enhanced MRI, surrounded by extensive T2/FLAIR signal abnormality representing vasogenic edema and infiltrating tumor. The "butterfly glioma" pattern — bilateral extension across the corpus callosum — is characteristic but not universal.

Typical glioblastoma workup sequences — T1 without and with gadolinium contrast, FLAIR, susceptibility-weighted imaging, and DCE plasma-volume/Ktrans perfusion maps
Prolonged T2/FLAIR abnormality (A) and a gadolinium-enhancing, necrotic mass (B) on MRI, with histology (C) showing a mitotic figure (black arrowhead), microvascular proliferation (white arrowheads), and pseudopalisading necrosis (asterisk)
Pearl

Some molecularly defined glioblastomas — reclassified from histologically lower-grade astrocytomas based on molecular markers alone (EGFR amp / +7,−10 / TERTp, without necrosis or microvascular proliferation on the slide) — can present atypically, as expansile, T2-hyperintense lesions with minimal or no contrast enhancement, mimicking a lower-grade glioma radiographically. Don't let a "bland-looking" MRI rule out glioblastoma if the molecular markers say otherwise.

Glioma IDH-mt Grade II/III

LO 1–3 Younger Patients (Peak 35–44) Better Px than IDH-wt Equivalent Median OS ~7–11 yrs
How boards would word it

"A 38-year-old woman presents after her first-ever witnessed seizure. She has no prior neurologic history, and her neurologic exam is entirely normal. MRI of the brain shows a nonenhancing, T2/FLAIR-hyperintense lesion in the left frontal lobe with well-defined borders and minimal mass effect; the lesion is homogeneously hyperintense on T2 but shows a thin hypointense rim on FLAIR. Biopsy shows positive IDH1 R132H immunohistochemistry, loss of ATRX expression, and a negative 1p/19q codeletion test. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Diffuse astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, WHO Grade 2
What to look for: a young adult (peak 35–44) with a new-onset seizure — the classic presenting symptom — plus a nonenhancing frontal lobe lesion showing the T2-FLAIR mismatch sign (homogeneous T2 hyperintensity with a hypointense FLAIR rim) is a fairly specific pattern for an IDH-mutant, 1p/19q-non-codeleted astrocytoma. ATRX loss supports astrocytic lineage, and the negative 1p/19q codeletion is what rules out oligodendroglioma — a positive codeletion would have pointed there instead.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition & Epidemiology

Diffuse Grade 2/3 IDH1- or IDH2-mutant gliomas are classified molecularly as either astrocytomas or oligodendrogliomas. They are slow-growing (indolent), located in the cerebral white matter, and carry a host of co-associating gene mutations — accounting for roughly 15–20% of all diffuse gliomas in adults.

Incidence ~0.5–1.0 per 100,000 person-years (Grade 2 and 3 similar). Onset peaks at 35–44 years — much younger than IDH-wildtype glioma. Slight male predominance (1.3:1); most frequent in the white population. No definitive environmental risk factor is established; the IDH mutation is thought to be an early, likely initiating event in gliomagenesis.

Co-Associating Mutations

  • 1p/19q codeleted → tumor is termed oligodendroglioma, not astrocytoma
  • TP53 (tumor suppressor) → astrocytic lineage
  • ATRX loss → astrocytic lineage
  • TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase)
  • CDKN2A homozygous deletion → upgrades to Grade 4 regardless of histology

Distribution

Diffuse cerebral white matter tumor; some forms also involve temporal white matter. Shows a frontal lobe predominance, often adjacent to the rostral lateral ventricles and within eloquent cortex (motor/language/cognitive areas).

Pathophysiology
IDH1/IDH2 mutation
→
α-ketoglutarate converted to D-2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), an oncometabolite (~100-fold accumulation)
→
Competitive inhibition of α-KG-dependent dioxygenases
→
DNA/histone hypermethylation blocks differentiation; oxidative stress; glutamine/glutamate dependence
→
Subclonal mutations accumulate over time → progression to higher grade
Histopathology (Grade 2 vs. 3)

Grade 2: well-differentiated astrocytes, moderately increased cellularity/atypia, no or very few mitoses. Grade 3 (formerly "anaplastic astrocytoma"): increased cellularity, distinct nuclear atypia, marked mitotic activity — but, unlike Grade 4, still lacks necrosis and microvascular proliferation.

Don't miss this

Homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion upgrades the tumor to Grade 4 regardless of how benign the histology looks — always check this even on a slide that otherwise reads as Grade 2/3.

Prognosis if Untreated

These tumors grow continuously but slowly (~4 mm/year), infiltrate normal brain tissue, and inevitably recur — the natural history is one of relentless progression to higher grade with accelerated growth and neovascularization over time. Median overall survival is ~7–11 years; even a "biopsy-only," minimal-intervention cohort showed a median OS of 5.6 years (vs. 10.2 years with resection), underscoring how much of this trajectory reflects the tumor's intrinsic behavior. IDH-mutant astrocytomas progress faster than IDH-mutant oligodendrogliomas.

LO2 — Clinical Presentation
Presentation

First-time seizure is the most common presenting symptom, especially for Grade 2 tumors — possibly related to 2-HG's structural similarity to glutamate, which may contribute to cortical excitability.

  • Headaches (~40% of patients at presentation)
  • Focal neurologic deficits (limb weakness, language difficulty) depending on tumor location
  • Symptoms of intracranial hypertension (nausea, vomiting)
Pearl

The indolent course often produces subtle symptoms that get overlooked, delaying diagnosis — median time from symptom onset to surgery is ~5–6.5 weeks. Most patients have excellent functional status at diagnosis (ECOG 0 in ~93%).

Glioma IDH-mt Grade IV

LO 1–3 Better Px than IDH-wt Grade IV Median Age 47 Median OS 21–42 mo
How boards would word it

"A 22-year-old man with a known history of a low-grade IDH-mutant astrocytoma diagnosed three years ago presents with a new generalized tonic-clonic seizure and two weeks of worsening headaches. Repeat MRI of the brain now shows a large, infiltrative, ring-enhancing mass with a necrotic core in the left frontal lobe, larger and more aggressive-appearing than his prior imaging. Repeat biopsy confirms an IDH1-mutant astrocytic glioma with vascular proliferation and necrosis, and no 1p/19q codeletion. Which of the following best describes this tumor?"

Answer: Diffuse astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, WHO Grade 4 — transformation from his prior lower-grade IDH-mutant astrocytoma
What to look for: a known lower-grade IDH-mutant astrocytoma that develops new necrosis and vascular proliferation on repeat biopsy has transformed to Grade 4 — this tumor typically evolves from a lower-grade IDH-mutant glioma rather than presenting de novo. Histologically it looks identical to IDH-wildtype glioblastoma (necrosis + vascular proliferation), but the retained IDH mutation means a meaningfully better prognosis than IDH-wildtype Grade 4 disease.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

Grade 4 IDH-mutant gliomas are very aggressive diffuse tumors. Although malignant, they carry a better prognosis than the IDH-wildtype Grade 4 counterpart.

Epidemiology

  • Most common in adolescents and young adults
  • Rare in younger children (under 9 years of age)
  • Median age at diagnosis 47 years
  • Median overall survival 21–42 months
  • Slight male predominance

Etiology

Genetic mutations. Typically evolves from a lower-grade IDH-mutant glioma rather than arising de novo.

Pathophysiology
Initial mutation in IDH1/IDH2
→
Additional chromosomal abnormalities (e.g. CDKN2A/B mutation)
→
Progression from lower-grade IDH-mutant glioma to Grade 4 disease
Pathology

Vascular (microvascular) proliferation and tumor necrosis — histologically indistinguishable from IDH-wildtype Grade 4 glioma. Predominant frontal lobe location.

Prognosis

Despite being histologically Grade 4 (necrosis + vascular proliferation, same as glioblastoma), retained IDH-mutant status confers a meaningfully better prognosis than the IDH-wildtype Grade 4 counterpart — median OS 21–42 months vs. ~12–18 months for IDH-wildtype glioblastoma.

Pearl — Ties back to the Classification overview

This tumor is the genuine "secondary glioblastoma" of classic teaching — it typically arises via transformation from a lower-grade IDH-mutant astrocytoma, unlike IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, which is usually primary/de novo. See the flagged discrepancy on the Classification overview panel, where the source material paired these terms the opposite way.

LO2 — Clinical Presentation
Presentation

Medulloblastoma

LO 1–3 Cerebellar Midline (Children) Most Common Malignant Pediatric Brain Tumor 5-yr Survival ~50–80%
How boards would word it

"A 6-year-old girl presents with a two-month history of early morning headaches and vomiting, along with a new unsteady, wide-based gait that has worsened over recent weeks. Her parents also note occasional double vision. Examination reveals truncal ataxia, horizontal nystagmus, and bilateral papilledema. MRI of the brain shows a large, midline mass filling the fourth ventricle with contrast enhancement and associated non-communicating hydrocephalus. Biopsy reveals sheets of small, round, blue cells with scant cytoplasm arranged around central areas of neuropil. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Medulloblastoma
What to look for: a young child with a short (<3 month) history of raised-ICP symptoms (morning headache, vomiting, papilledema) plus cerebellar signs (truncal ataxia, nystagmus) from a midline fourth-ventricle mass, with classic "small round blue cell" histology forming Homer-Wright rosettes, is the textbook picture of medulloblastoma. The midline location and small-round-blue-cell histology are what separate it from pilocytic astrocytoma, which is more often lateralized and shows Rosenthal fibers instead.

LO1 — Natural History
Definition

A malignant, invasive embryonal tumor of the cerebellum, most often seen in children and young adults.

Epidemiology

  • Accounts for ~20–25% of CNS tumors in children — the most common malignant pediatric brain tumor
  • Peak incidence in the first decade of life
  • Midline presentation in children (cerebellar vermis); can be more lateral in adults
  • By definition, exclusively located in the cerebellum
  • Associated with Turcot syndrome

Behavior & Prognosis

  • Highly malignant, but notably radiosensitive
  • 5-year survival now >50%; modern series report 60–80%, approaching ~75% with adequate resection + radiotherapy (including prophylactic spinal radiation)
  • Derived from cerebellar granule cell precursors at the edge of the fourth ventricle
Molecular Subgroups

At least four molecular subgroups are recognized:

  1. WNT-activated type
  2. SHH-activated, TP53-wildtype type
  3. SHH-activated, TP53-mutant type
  4. Non-WNT/non-SHH type
Prognostic Genetics

Alterations in Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) pathway genes → very low survivability. Alterations in the WNT/β-catenin pathway → typically a survivable lesion. Other poor prognostic factors: inadequate resection, presence of drop metastases, ERBB2 mutation.

LO2 — Clinical Presentation
Presentation

Clinical signs are typically present for less than three months before diagnosis.

  • Common: headaches (↑ICP), persistent vomiting (↑ICP), truncal ataxia (sometimes spasticity), papilledema (↑ICP), nystagmus
  • Less common: limb ataxia, dysdiadochokinesia
Red Flag

Invasion/compression of the fourth ventricle → non-communicating hydrocephalus → raised ICP. Drop metastases to the spinal cord via CSF/leptomeningeal seeding are common and can cause paraplegia — this is why the diagnostic workup includes CSF cytology and often the entire neuraxis is imaged.

Meningioma

LO 1–3 Benign (Grade I) 80–85% F > M ~2.2:1 6th–7th Decade
How boards would word it

"A 62-year-old woman is found to have an incidental extra-axial mass on brain MRI obtained after a minor head injury. She reports no neurologic symptoms, though admits to occasional mild headaches over the past year. MRI shows a round, sharply demarcated, homogeneously enhancing extra-axial mass along the right frontal convexity, with a thickened, enhancing tail of adjacent dura. There is no evidence of brain invasion. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Meningioma (WHO Grade I)
What to look for: an older woman with an incidentally discovered, homogeneously enhancing, extra-axial mass showing a dural tail sign is the classic meningioma pattern. The extra-axial location (displacing rather than invading brain), round well-demarcated margins, and dural tail together distinguish it from an intra-axial glioma.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

Meningiomas are usually slow-growing, benign tumors arising from the meningeal coverings of the brain — specifically the meningothelial (arachnoid cap) cells of the arachnoid layer/villi. A small percentage are malignant and aggressive.

Meningioma types by location (convexity, intraventricular, olfactory groove) and the meningeal layers it arises from — dura, arachnoid, pia (illustration: Cleveland Clinic)
Meningioma locations across the neuraxis — parasagittal, olfactory groove, suprasellar, clivus, foramen magnum, and cerebellar (illustration: Johns Hopkins Neurosurgery)

Epidemiology

  • ~37% of all primary intracranial neoplasms — the most common benign primary brain tumor in adults
  • Incidence ~8.33 per 100,000 person-years
  • Most common in the 6th–7th decades of life (>65 yrs); rare in children
  • Females > males (~2.2:1)
  • In children, often associated with NF2

Etiology / Risk Factors

  • Mostly idiopathic
  • Established risk factor: ionizing radiation (radiotherapy, dental X-rays)
  • Sex hormones may play a role
  • SV-40 viral infection implicated
  • Multiple meningiomas → consider NF2
Common Locations

Convexities of the cranium, along the falx, and the skull base — see LO2 for the full location-by-symptom breakdown.

Pathology

Usually solitary, lobulated, encapsulated tumors attached to the meninges — grayish-white, firm, with a granular cut surface. Typically expand and compress CNS tissue rather than invade it, though they can invade skull bone (not itself a sign of malignancy).

Microscopic Findings

  • Whorls of meningothelial cells ("onion-peel" arrangement)
  • Psammoma bodies
  • Increased vascularity

Imaging Modality of Choice: MRI

Plain MRI: round, sharply demarcated extra-axial lesion; T1 iso-/hypointense, T2 iso-/hyperintense; dural tail sign.

CT

Hyperdense or isodense, well-demarcated extra-axial mass; possible calcifications. Often the modality that first picks up an incidental meningioma during workup of unrelated headache/seizure.

Other

Angiography can also be useful. Genetic typing of tumor cells. Biopsy is rarely needed — most meningiomas are diagnosed on imaging alone; reserved for inoperable tumors or inconclusive imaging.

Pearl

The dural tail sign and homogeneous, "snowball"-like enhancement on contrast MRI are the highest-yield clues that a mass is extra-axial and meningeal (meningioma) rather than an intra-axial glioma.

Ependymoma

LO 1–3 4th Ventricle (Children) Molecular WHO 2021 Classification 5-yr Survival 65–90%
How boards would word it

"A 4-year-old boy presents with a one-month history of morning vomiting, increasing lethargy, and an enlarging head circumference noted by his pediatrician. His parents also report new-onset unsteadiness when walking. Examination reveals ataxia and bilateral papilledema. Answer: Ependymoma
What to look for: a young child (median age ~5) with signs of raised ICP (vomiting, lethargy, increasing head circumference) plus ataxia from a fourth-ventricle mass, with classic perivascular pseudorosette and blepharoplast histology, is the textbook picture of ependymoma — the single most common tumor of the fourth ventricle in children.

LO1 — Natural History
Definition

Ependymomas are a heterogeneous group of primary CNS tumors of glial cell origin, found throughout the ventricular system of the brain and spinal cord.

Epidemiology

  • 5–10% of primary brain tumors in the first two decades of life
  • Median age at diagnosis: 5 years
  • Most common tumor of the fourth ventricle
  • Distribution is age-dependent: intracranial (4th ventricle) most common in children; spinal most common in adults
  • Average survival post-surgery ~4 years for 4th ventricle tumors; overall 5-yr survival 65–90% depending on grade

Etiology

  • Mostly unknown; thought to arise from radial glial cells
  • Spinal ependymomas can be associated with an NF2 gene mutation (chromosome 22); ~20% of NF2 carriers develop an intramedullary spinal ependymoma
  • Recurrence rate depends on location, grade, molecular subtype, and treatment
Pathology

Most are well-differentiated tumors with relatively slow growth. Cohesive tumor cells with intercellular junctions form tubules; classic histology shows perivascular pseudorosettes and rod-shaped bodies near the nucleus (blepharoplasts).

Craniopharyngioma

LO 1–3 Suprasellar / Rathke Pouch Remnant Bimodal Age Distribution Most Common Childhood Supratentorial Tumor
How boards would word it

"An 8-year-old boy presents with six months of headaches, and his parents note he has fallen behind his peers in height over the past year. He has also been unusually thirsty and urinating frequently, both day and night. Visual field testing reveals bitemporal hemianopsia. MRI of the brain shows a suprasellar, partially cystic, calcified mass compressing the optic chiasm and extending toward the third ventricle. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Craniopharyngioma (likely adamantinomatous, given his age)
What to look for: a child with growth failure (growth hormone deficiency), polyuria/polydipsia (diabetes insipidus), and bitemporal hemianopsia from optic chiasm compression, plus a suprasellar calcified cystic mass on MRI, is the classic craniopharyngioma triad — endocrine dysfunction + visual field defect + suprasellar mass. The bimodal age distribution (child → adamantinomatous; older adult → papillary) is a useful subtype clue.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

Craniopharyngiomas are common childhood tumors derived from squamous epithelial cell rests — remnants of Rathke's pouch (an ectodermal derivative). These typically cystic tumors lie above the sella turcica (suprasellar), applying pressure to the optic chiasm and extending into the third ventricle.

Craniopharyngioma — suprasellar tumor compressing the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and optic nerves (illustration: Cleveland Clinic)

Epidemiology

  • Rare overall — ~350 new cases/year in the US
  • Bimodal age distribution: 5–14 years (adamantinomatous); 50–70/75 years (papillary)
  • Accounts for 7–10% of all childhood tumors; the most common childhood supratentorial tumor

Histologic Variants

  • Adamantinomatous (common): CTNNB1 (β-catenin/Wnt pathway) mutation; reticular epithelial cells, frequent calcifications, cysts, keratin nodules
  • Papillary: metaplastic squamous cells; calcifications/cysts rare; no keratin nodules

Arises in the suprasellar region and can extend intrasellarly, compressing adjacent structures — the pituitary gland, hypothalamus, infundibular stalk, optic chiasm, and ventricular system — producing a well-known constellation of endocrine, visual, and hydrocephalic effects (see LO2).

Differential

Other sellar/suprasellar masses to consider: pituitary adenoma, Rathke cleft cyst.

Ganglion Cell Tumors (Gangliocytoma)

LO 1 WHO Grade 1 LEAT Peak Age 10–30
How boards would word it

"A 14-year-old girl with a two-year history of recurrent focal seizures, refractory to two anti-seizure medications, undergoes MRI of the brain. Imaging reveals a small, well-circumscribed, partially calcified cortical lesion in the right temporal lobe with minimal surrounding edema and mild contrast enhancement. Resection is performed, and histology shows abnormal, large, mature multipolar neurons without any accompanying neoplastic glial component. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Gangliocytoma (WHO Grade 1)
What to look for: a child/young adult with longstanding, medication-refractory temporal lobe epilepsy plus a small calcified cortical lesion with minimal mass effect/edema is the classic picture of a long-term epilepsy-associated tumor (LEAT). The key distinguishing histologic feature — large mature neurons without a neoplastic glial component — is what separates gangliocytoma from its close cousin, ganglioglioma (which has both neuronal and neoplastic glial elements).
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

A CNS tumor composed of mature-looking neurons. When the tumor also contains neoplastic glial-like cells, it is instead termed a ganglioglioma.

Epidemiology

  • Very rare — 0.1–0.5% of all brain tumors, but 1–3% of tumors in epilepsy surgical series
  • Occurs mostly in the first three decades of life; peak age 10–30 years
  • WHO Grade 1; one of the long-term epilepsy-associated tumors (LEATs), frequently discovered as the cause of epilepsy

Pathology & Location

  • >80% found in the temporal lobes; can arise anywhere in the neuroaxis
  • Slow-growing, indolent — does not undergo anaplastic transformation (unlike ganglioglioma, which rarely can)
  • Histology: large, often multipolar mature neurons, sometimes binucleated, with cytoplasmic vacuolization; microcalcification can be seen
Key Distinguishing Features

Absence of neoplastic glial cells (present in ganglioglioma) and absence of a small-round-blue-cell/neuroblast component (which would instead suggest medulloblastoma or neuroblastoma). Immunophenotype confirms a purely neuronal lineage: synaptophysin+, neurofilament+, chromogranin-A+, MAP2+, GFAP−.

Not to be confused with dysplastic cerebellar gangliocytoma (Lhermitte-Duclos disease) — a different, cerebellum-specific entity despite the similar name.

Pearl

GFAP-negative is the single most useful immunostain here — a positive GFAP would suggest a glial (or mixed glioneuronal) component, pointing toward ganglioglioma instead.

Neurofibromatosis Type II (NF2)

LO 1–2 NF2 Gene (Chr 22) — Merlin Bilateral Acoustic Schwannomas Onset ~20 yrs
How boards would word it

"A 22-year-old woman presents with progressive bilateral hearing loss and tinnitus over the past year, along with recent unsteadiness. She also reports blurry vision and was recently diagnosed with cataracts. Family history is notable for a mother who required surgery for "multiple brain tumors" in her 30s. MRI of the brain with contrast reveals bilateral enhancing masses at the cerebellopontine angles involving the internal auditory canals, along with two separate dural-based enhancing masses along the falx. Which of the following is the most likely underlying diagnosis?"

Answer: Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2)
What to look for: bilateral vestibular (acoustic) schwannomas — the hallmark, essentially pathognomonic finding of NF2 — combined with multiple meningiomas, early-onset cataracts, and a family history of multiple CNS tumors (autosomal dominant inheritance) together clinch NF2. This is a CNS-predominant syndrome, distinct from the peripheral-nerve/skin-predominant NF1.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

Neurofibromatosis is a familial tumor syndrome — really a spectrum of hereditary neurocutaneous syndromes. Two distinct forms exist, each with a separate genetic origin: NF1 characteristically involves the peripheral nervous system; NF2 characteristically involves the central nervous system.

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (left) vs. type 2 (right) — associated tumors and cutaneous/ocular findings (illustration: AMBOSS)

Epidemiology & Genetics

  • NF1 (for contrast): common, autosomal dominant, incidence ~1/3,000–4,000
  • NF2: much less common — 1/40,000–50,000 (possibly as high as 1/25,000 by newer molecular-genetic estimates)
  • NF2 gene located on chromosome 22; encodes merlin (schwannomin), a tumor suppressor

Associated Tumors (NF2)

  • Acoustic (vestibular) schwannomas — classically bilateral
  • Multiple meningiomas
  • Ependymomas (including spinal cord)
  • Spinal cord/meningeal gliomas
  • Nodular Schwann cell growths in the spinal cord
  • Meningoangiomatosis; glial hamartia
LO2 — Clinical Presentation
Presentation

Typical onset is around 20 years of age.

Neurologic

  • Vestibular nerve + other cranial nerve involvement
  • Mass effect from intracranial meningiomas
  • Intra-/extramedullary spinal cord and meningeal tumors
  • Peripheral neuropathies

Eye

  • Cataracts
  • Epiretinal membranes
  • Retinal hamartomas

Skin

  • Cutaneous tumors
  • Skin plaques
  • Subcutaneous tumors

Specific features depend on tumor type and location — but bilateral vestibular schwannomas are the defining, most consistent feature of NF2.

Pearl

Merlin (NF2 gene product, chromosome 22) parallels neurofibromin (NF1 gene product, chromosome 17) — both are tumor-suppressor proteins, but their loss produces very different tumor spectra: NF1 → peripheral neurofibromas/café-au-lait spots/optic gliomas; NF2 → bilateral vestibular schwannomas/multiple meningiomas/ependymomas.

Tuberous Sclerosis Complex

LO 1–2 Phakomatosis TSC1/TSC2 — Hamartin/Tuberin Prevalence ~1/6,000
How boards would word it

"A 2-year-old boy is brought in for evaluation of new-onset seizures characterized by sudden head bobbing and flexor spasms occurring in clusters, along with recent behavioral regression. On examination, his mother points out several hypopigmented, ash-leaf-shaped macules on his trunk present since birth, along with a rough, orange-peel-textured plaque over his lower back. Brain MRI shows several calcified subependymal nodules along the lateral ventricle walls, as well as multiple cortical hamartomas. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Tuberous sclerosis complex
What to look for: infantile spasms (head bobbing, flexor/extensor spasms in clusters, often with regression) plus hypopigmented ash-leaf macules (present since birth) and a shagreen patch (orange-peel plaque, classically lumbosacral), plus subependymal calcified nodules and cortical tubers on imaging, together clinch TSC — a classic phakomatosis with skin, brain, and other organ hamartomas.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

Tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) is a neurocutaneous genetic disorder with systemic manifestations, including CNS involvement. It belongs to the group of neurocutaneous disorders called phakomatoses.

Tuberous sclerosis complex — skin, brain, heart, and kidney manifestations (illustration: AMBOSS)

Epidemiology & Genetics

  • Autosomal dominant with highly variable penetrance (or spontaneous mutation)
  • Prevalence ~1/6,000
  • TSC1 (chromosome 9q34) encodes hamartin; TSC2 (chromosome 16p13) encodes tuberin

Pathophysiology

Mutation of these tumor-suppressor genes → loss of function → unchecked cell growth → hamartoma/tumor development. Cortical tubers are benign brain hamartomas; systemic pathology (renal, cardiac, skin) is present as well.

Prognosis

Renal disease and epilepsy are the most common causes of death; life expectancy can be normal if symptoms are mild and complications are managed.

Mnemonic — HAMARTOMAS

Hamartomas, Ash-leaf spots, Mental (intellectual) disability, Adenoma sebaceum, renal Angiomyolipoma, Rhabdomyoma, Tumor suppressor genes (TSC1/TSC2), autOsomal dominant, Mitral regurgitation, Astrocytomas, Seizures, and Shagreen patches.

  • Shagreen patch — flesh-colored, orange-peel-textured plaque, classically lumbosacral
  • Ungual fibromas — subungual/periungual papules

  • Retinal mulberry tumors (hamartomas)
  • Cardiac rhabdomyoma (>50% of patients; can cause mitral regurgitation/CHF)
  • Renal cysts, angiomyolipoma, (rarely) carcinoma
Axial CT — bilateral, hyperdense, calcified subependymal nodules along the lateral ventricles, plus a subcortical cystic lesion in the right occipital lobe
T2-weighted MRI of the abdomen with gadolinium — large renal angiomyolipoma (left kidney), a classic tuberous sclerosis renal finding

Primary CNS Lymphoma

LO 1 ~1% of Intracranial Tumors Immunosuppression / EBV-Associated B-cell, Large Cell Type
How boards would word it

"A 68-year-old man with poorly controlled HIV/AIDS (CD4 count 40) presents with two weeks of progressive personality changes and word-finding difficulty, along with new left-sided weakness. MRI of the brain with contrast shows a solitary, homogeneously enhancing periventricular mass. Stereotactic biopsy reveals sheets of malignant lymphoid cells with an angiocentric growth pattern, and in situ hybridization is positive for Epstein-Barr virus. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Primary CNS lymphoma
What to look for: an immunocompromised patient (HIV/AIDS with a low CD4 count is the classic risk factor) with a periventricular, homogeneously enhancing mass and EBV-positive malignant lymphoid cells showing an angiocentric pattern, is the classic picture of primary CNS lymphoma — distinguished from secondary CNS lymphoma by originating intracranially, with extracranial spread (if any) being a late event.
Axial MRI (A, B: T1 post-contrast; C: FLAIR) — homogeneously enhancing periventricular lesions in the right thalamus (A) and left frontal lobe (B), each with hyperintense surrounding edema on FLAIR (C); can look identical to toxoplasmosis in HIV/AIDS and often needs further workup to distinguish
Sagittal MRI — primary CNS B-cell lymphoma centered on the sella turcica and hypothalamus, extending to the tectum
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

In primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL), the disease initiates intracranially; metastatic extracranial spread, if it occurs, is a late event. This must be distinguished from secondary CNS lymphoma — metastatic spread to the brain from a non-Hodgkin lymphoma located elsewhere in the body.

Epidemiology

  • Accounts for ~1% of intracranial tumors
  • Incidence rising in older patients (>60; highest in the 70s)
  • ~1,500 new cases/year in the US
  • Strong risk factor: immunosuppression (e.g., poorly controlled HIV/AIDS) and/or EBV infection — relatively common in immunocompromised patients; suspected patients should be tested for HIV

Pathology

  • Majority: B-cell lymphoma, large cell type
  • Tumors in immunosuppressed patients typically contain the EBV genome
  • Malignant lymphoid cells with mitotic activity, necrosis, and a characteristic angiocentric pattern (surrounding vessels)
  • Can present as a single focal mass or multifocal lesions
Natural Behavior & Prognosis

Responds initially to radiation and steroid therapy, but tumors tend to recur. Overall prognosis is variable — typically better than other primary CNS tumors, but less favorable than non-CNS lymphomas.

Pearl

Glucocorticoids can rapidly shrink PCNSL and obscure biopsy findings (the "ghost tumor" effect) — this is why steroids are typically withheld until diagnostic tissue is obtained, except in life-threatening raised ICP.

Metastatic Disease

LO 1–3 Most Common CNS Neoplasm Overall 8× More Common than Primary Brain Tumors Lung > Breast > Melanoma
How boards would word it

"A 61-year-old man with a 40-pack-year smoking history and known non-small cell lung cancer presents with two weeks of progressive headache, right-sided weakness, and one witnessed generalized seizure. MRI of the brain with contrast reveals three well-circumscribed, ring-enhancing lesions at the gray-white junction of both cerebral hemispheres, each surrounded by a disproportionately large amount of vasogenic edema. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?"

Answer: Metastatic brain disease (from his known lung primary)
What to look for: a patient with a known systemic malignancy (lung cancer is the most common primary source overall) presenting with multiple, well-circumscribed lesions at the gray-white junction (the classic location for hematogenous spread, reflecting the arterial watershed) with disproportionate edema, is the classic pattern for brain metastases — a solitary primary glioma or abscess is far less likely to be multifocal at the gray-white junction.
LO1 — Natural History
Definition

The seeding of a distal neoplastic event into the central nervous system. Metastatic events are the most common cause of CNS neoplasm overall — brain metastases are 8 times more common than primary brain tumors.

Metastatic brain tumor — hematogenous seeding from a lung cancer primary (illustration: Cleveland Clinic)

Epidemiology

  • Intracranial masses appear in 25% of all patients with systemic cancer: 15% brain metastases, 5% leptomeningeal metastases, 5% dural metastases
  • 40% of patients with one detected lesion will have more

Primary Lesion Sources

  • Common: lung (35–64%; NSCLC most common primary, though melanoma and small-cell carcinoma have the greatest per-case propensity to spread to brain), breast (14–18%), skin melanoma (4–21%), renal, GI
  • Uncommon: prostate, pancreas, uterine, ovarian, Hodgkin lymphoma
Routes & Distribution

Route: mainly hematogenous spread; rarely direct spread from surrounding bone, meninges, or pituitary.

Brain Location

  • Can occur anywhere in the CNS
  • Cerebral hemispheres (80%)
  • Cerebellum (10–15%)
  • Arterial watershed zones most common — specifically the MCA/PCA junction

Spine Location

  • Classified as extradural, intradural extramedullary, or intradural intramedullary
  • Most are extradural, compressing the cord; arise via the vertebral venous plexus (of Batson)
  • Thoracic spine is the most common site
  • Especially breast, lung, prostate, kidney, thyroid, lymphoma, plasma cell dyscrasia

Primary CNS tumors do not metastasize to organs outside the CNS — spread across this boundary is one-directional in practice.

Drop & Leptomeningeal Metastases

Intradural extramedullary spinal metastases ("drop mets") and leptomeningeal metastases typically manifest as nodules along the spine/cauda equina, causing back pain with neurologic symptoms (e.g., limb weakness); detectable by lumbar puncture.

LO2 — Clinical Presentation
Presentation

Typically slowly evolving signs/symptoms, highly dependent on tumor location. Systemic disease usually presents first, but a CNS metastatic lesion can be the initial presentation — or can even manifest after the primary systemic disease has already been treated/eradicated.

  • Focal space-occupying lesions; surrounding edema contributes to raised ICP
  • Headache (~25%)
  • Hemiparesis (~25%)
  • Seizures (~15%)
  • Cognitive or behavioral changes (~15%)

Spinal Cord Presentation

Pain is the most common presenting complaint — can be axial or radicular — along with signs/symptoms of cord compression.

LO3 — Diagnostic Workup
Imaging

MRI imaging is the mainstay of workup. The classic pattern: multiple ring-enhancing lesions at the gray-white junction with disproportionate surrounding edema. Lumbar puncture can help detect leptomeningeal/drop metastases when suspected.

Pearl

A solitary ring-enhancing lesion isn't always metastatic disease — the differential includes abscess, glioblastoma, and demyelinating disease — but multiple lesions at the gray-white junction in a patient with known systemic cancer strongly favor metastasis.
