Medical Neuroanatomy · Chapter 8 · Thalamus (Hypothalamus / Ventral Thalamus)

Case Study 8-2

47-year-old, left-handed man — chronic diabetes insipidus, bitemporal visual field loss, behavioral rage, and hypogonadism, complicated by congestive heart failure

Suspected: suprasellar/hypothalamic mass (craniopharyngioma) Childhood-onset course Died of CHF, 1 month into admission
Localization Finding Term Pattern Endocrine / systemic

Chief Complaint

A 47-year-old, left-handed man status post MI was admitted to the hospital with a primary complaint of chest pain and shortness of breath of 3 weeks duration1. He also complained of a progressive narrowing of his visual fields2.

History of Chief Complaint

His chest pain, which had been relieved after his documented MI 6 months ago3, had begun to return. It was accompanied by dyspnea that worsened on exertion.

Medical History

Since childhood he had consumed at least a gallon of water a day4 and had had thirst, polyuria, and nocturia5. Having grown up with these symptoms, he had considered them normal. He had a long history of behavioral dysfunction that included rage and anger inappropriate for the situation6. He had a recent history of angina on exertion, and electrocardiographic analysis 6 months ago documented an acute myocardial infarct.

Family History

At the time of admission he was not married, lived alone, and admitted to having very little libido throughout his life7. His mother, father, and two siblings were alive and in good health. No one else in his family had exhibited his chief complaints.

General Physical Examination

He appeared to be a well-hydrated, well-nourished man, alert but with an anxious demeanor. He appeared his stated age. He weighed 240 pounds and his height was 66 inches8. His head was normocephalic. Fundoscopic examination revealed a normal cup-to-disc ratio; soft cotton wool patches were noted two disc spaces from the disc in the superior temporal retina bilaterally9; no aneurysms, hemorrhages, or papilledema was evident. His heartbeat was regular without gallops or thrills; an S2 systolic murmur was noted at the left sternal border. The apical beat was displaced to the left. Abdominojugular reflux was noted on application of abdominal pressure10. Respiration was labored, breath sounds were decreased; crackles and wheezes were heard at the bases of the lungs on auscultation. The abdomen was soft, without masses or tenderness. Temperature was elevated at 37.5°C and oscillated between 37.3°C and 37.9°C over a 24-hour period11. Skin was moist with good texture and turgor. Pretibial edema (+2) was present in the lower extremity.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status. The patient was awake and oriented for time and place; memory and knowledge were appropriate for his education. His speech was articulate and meaningful12. Although he was cooperative most of the time, he experienced bouts of rage, where he yelled at the attending staff and physician13.

Cranial Nerves. He had a full range of eye movements. Visual acuity was normal in the center of his fields but diminished rapidly to the sides; vision in the temporal fields was absent altogether14. Corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes were intact. His face was symmetric, with normal expression of emotion15. Hearing was diminished in the right ear more than in the left16. Uvula and palate were symmetric and elevated on the midline; the tongue protruded on the midline. Shoulder shrug was symmetric.

Motor Systems. Gross motor strength was equal in upper and lower extremities; deep tendon reflexes were 2/4 and equal in upper and lower extremities. Babinski response was physiologic17, with no muscular atrophy or hypertrophy. No drift or involuntary motion was detected.

Sensation. There was no loss of vibration or proprioceptive sense, and no loss of pain or thermal sense18 was evident.

Follow-up

During his hospital admission, marked thirst and daily intake of 4000 to 17,000 mL of fluid were noted. Urine outputs of 3500 to 18,000 mL daily were recorded. The specific gravity of his urine was always below 1.00519. One month after admission, the patient died of congestive heart failure20.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

1 · Localization

Chronic polydipsia/polyuria (diabetes insipidus), bitemporal-pattern visual field loss, lifelong hypogonadism, inappropriate rage, severe obesity, and an unstable low-grade temperature together localize this process to the hypothalamus and adjacent optic chiasm — not the thalamus proper. Each finding maps to a specific hypothalamic region: diabetes insipidus to the supraoptic/paraventricular-neurohypophyseal system, visual loss to chiasmal compression by a mass arising near the hypothalamus/pituitary, rage to the medial hypothalamus, and hypogonadism plus temperature instability to the anterior hypothalamus. Just as important are the negative findings — entirely normal motor, sensory, and cognitive/language exams — which tell you the lesion has not extended into the thalamus proper or the internal capsule, in direct contrast to Case 8-1.

2 · Distribution

This is a focal process — a single lesion at the hypothalamic-pituitary-chiasmal junction — whose effects look broad only because the hypothalamus packs so many distinct regulatory systems (autonomic, endocrine, behavioral, thermoregulatory) into a very small volume. It is not diffuse (cognition is fully intact) and, unlike Case 8-1, it is not organized around a vascular territory at all.

3 · Time Course

The hypothalamic story here is chronic and insidious, dating back to childhood (lifelong polydipsia, lifelong low libido) — the opposite of Case 8-1's acute, wake-up-stroke onset. That time course alone argues strongly against a vascular mechanism and toward a slow-growing congenital/developmental mass, such as a craniopharyngioma. Layered on top of this decades-long process is a second, separate, subacute timeline: chest pain and dyspnea recurring over 3 weeks, culminating in death from congestive heart failure one month into this admission.

5 · Diagnostic Workup

  1. MRI brain with contrast, dedicated sellar/suprasellar viewsCharacterizes the mass and its relationship to the optic chiasm, hypothalamus, and pituitary stalk.
  2. Formal visual field perimetryPrecisely maps the bitemporal defect and establishes a baseline to track treatment response.
  3. Paired serum/urine osmolality ± water deprivation testConfirms central diabetes insipidus and distinguishes it from primary polydipsia or nephrogenic DI.
  4. Full anterior pituitary hormone panelLH, FSH, testosterone, TSH/free T4, cortisol, prolactin, IGF-1 — screens for panhypopituitarism given the long-standing axis disruption.
  5. Cardiac workup — echocardiogram, BNP, troponin, repeat ECGCharacterizes the severity of his heart failure and prior MI, since this is the immediate threat to his life.
  6. Ophthalmology referralDirect fundus/optic nerve exam for optic atrophy from chronic chiasmal compression.

6 · Prognosis

His hypothalamic-pituitary disease, once identified, is often manageable for years with hormone replacement and, depending on resectability, surgery or radiation for a craniopharyngioma. His cardiac disease, however, is the immediate threat — and it proved fatal: he died of congestive heart failure one month into this admission. A fascinating central lesion does not have to be the most clinically urgent problem.

7 · Management

  • Desmopressin (DDAVP) to control diabetes insipidus and normalize fluid balance
  • Hormone replacement for any confirmed anterior pituitary deficiencies (testosterone, levothyroxine, hydrocortisone as needed)
  • Neurosurgical/oncologic evaluation of the suprasellar mass — biopsy, resection, and/or radiation depending on characterization
  • Ongoing ophthalmologic monitoring of visual fields
  • Cardiology management of heart failure and prior MI — guideline-directed medical therapy, diuresis, afterload reduction
  • Structured weight-management plan, recognizing hypothalamic obesity often resists behavioral intervention alone
  • Behavioral/psychiatric support and safety planning around his rage episodes

Concept Deep-Dive

Localization

Optic Chiasm & the Hypothalamus

A mass arising below/within the hypothalamic-pituitary region compresses the chiasm from beneath, hitting the crossing nasal retinal fibers first — the anatomic basis of this patient's bitemporal visual field loss.

Endocrine

The Diabetes Insipidus Pathway

A lesion disrupting the supraoptic/paraventricular nuclei or their hypothalamo-neurohypophyseal tract cuts off ADH delivery to the kidney — producing exactly this patient's massive, unconcentrated urine output (specific gravity persistently below 1.005).

Behavior / appetite

Medial vs. Lateral Hypothalamus

RegionNormal roleLesion effect
Medial (satiety center)Signals fullnessHyperphagia, obesity, rage
Lateral (feeding center)Signals hungerAnorexia, emaciation, apathy

His severe obesity and inappropriate rage both point toward the medial hypothalamus, whose destruction removes the satiety signal and disrupts nearby behavioral/limbic circuitry at the same time.

Thermoregulation

Anterior vs. Posterior Hypothalamus

RegionNormal roleLesion effect
Anterior (heat-loss center)Cooling / parasympatheticHyperthermia, cyclic fever
Posterior (heat-conservation center)Warming / sympatheticHypothermia, poikilothermy

His mild, oscillating temperature elevation over 24 hours (37.3–37.9°C) fits an anterior hypothalamic set-point disturbance far better than a sustained infectious fever.

Endocrine

Hypothalamic Control of the Pituitary

Hypothalamic releasing hormones (like GnRH) drive the anterior pituitary via the portal system. Disruption of this axis from childhood explains his lifelong low libido, and raises the possibility of broader panhypopituitarism worth screening for directly.
