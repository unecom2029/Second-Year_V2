Medical Neuroanatomy · Chapter 8 · Thalamus

Case Study 8-1

65-year-old, right-handed man — right hemiparesis, homonymous hemianopsia, dysarthria, and confusion

Suspected: left thalamic infarct HTN × 7y · DM × 4y Acute ("wake-up") onset
Localization Finding Term Pattern Vascular / workup

Chief Complaint

A 65-year-old man presented with right-sided hemiparesis1, homonymous hemianopsia2, dysarthria, and confusion3.

History of Chief Complaint

This 65-year-old, right-handed man was brought to the emergency room early in the morning by his family. They complained that he was unable to move his right hand, his speech was slurred, and that he appeared to be confused. He had been that way since shortly after he awoke that morning4.

Medical History

He was in good health until 7 years prior, when he was diagnosed as having hypertension5. One year later, he was admitted to the community hospital a day after experiencing a brief episode of quadriparesis, blurred vision, and nausea6. At that time Doppler studies of the carotids were normal7, as were lumbar puncture, electroencephalogram, and a computed tomographic (CT) scan. Diabetes was detected8 and he was consequently given a regimen of insulin and discharged. During the next 4 years, no known transient ischemic episodes occurred. The day prior to his most current admission, he had complained of intermittent weakness in his right hand9.

Family History

At the time of admission he was married, retired from military service, and had two children, both of whom were married. His father had had hypertension and died at 55 of coronary artery disease10; his mother was still living.

General Physical Examination

He was a stable, well-hydrated, well-nourished man in no acute distress who appeared his stated age. Funduscopic examination revealed arterial-venous nicking11 without hemorrhage or papilledema. His heartbeat was regular without murmurs or gallops. Blood pressure was 180/10012. Respiration and pulse were normal. Lungs were clear to auscultation. Abdomen was soft without masses. Skin was of good texture and temperature. Several small areas of actinic keratosis on the right posterior scalp were evident.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status. He was awake and alert, but disoriented with respect to time, place, and personal information, relying on his family members to supply much of the history. He had impaired recent memory and fund of knowledge13. (He said Kennedy was the current US President.) He confused the left and right sides of his body14. His speech was poorly articulated and perseverative; he used word substitutions and mispronounced words frequently; however, he had normal repetition of speech15.

Cranial Nerves. He had a full range of eye movements. There was a right homonymous hemianopsia16 present. Pupils were symmetric and bilaterally responsive to light, both direct and consensual. Hearing was normal in both ears. Corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes were intact. His face was asymmetric on spontaneous emotional expression (e.g., smiling) but not on voluntary movement (right "emotional" facial paralysis17). Discriminative touch was intact across his face18, bilaterally. The uvula was elevated on the midline; the tongue protruded on the midline. Shoulder shrug was symmetric.

Motor Systems. Strength in the limbs was +5/5 in the left arm and leg, +3/5 in the right arm, and +4/5 in the right leg19. Deep tendon reflexes were elevated in the right arm more than the right leg; they were physiologic on the left. A Babinski sign was noted on the right20.

Sensation. Pain, light touch, and vibration sense were normal, but discriminative touch and proprioception were impaired in the right hand21.

Follow-up

Re-examination at two months after his initial presentation found an alert but very confused gentleman with poor orientation for person, place and time. He had to be continually reminded of why he was being examined. His language remained poorly articulated and perseverative; however, there was some improvement in meaning since his last visit. He could smile symmetrically on command and also smiled symmetrically with emotional stimuli. The weakness in his right arm and leg had partially resolved; however, the deep tendon reflexes in these extremities remained elevated22.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

1 · Localization

The right-sided hemiparesis with hyperreflexia and a Babinski sign, right homonymous hemianopsia, and a dominant-hemisphere-type language pattern (paraphasic, perseverative, but with preserved repetition) all point to a single lesion on the left side of the brain, at a suprasegmental level. The specific mix — sensory, motor, visual, and cognitive/language deficits appearing together from what is very likely one small vascular lesion — is the signature of a thalamic localization: the thalamus packs sensory relay (VPL/VPM), motor relay (VL/VA), visual relay (LGN/pulvinar), and limbic/cognitive nuclei (anterior, dorsomedial) into a very small volume, so a single infarct can plausibly touch several of these systems at once, exactly as it appears to have done here.

2 · Distribution

The pathology is best described as focal — a single vascular territory within the left thalamus — even though its clinical footprint looks "multifocal" on the surface (sensory + motor + visual + cognitive + language). That apparent multifocality is a direct consequence of how tightly packed and functionally diverse the thalamic nuclei and their in/out fiber tracts are, not evidence of a truly diffuse or multi-site process. There is no exam finding here (e.g., bilateral signs, diffuse encephalopathy) that would push this toward a genuinely diffuse etiology.

3 · Time Course

This is an acute presentation layered on chronic, progressive vascular disease. He woke up with the deficit already present (true onset time unknown — a "wake-up stroke"), the day after a stuttering episode of intermittent right-hand weakness, and six years after a posterior-circulation-type TIA (quadriparesis, blurred vision, nausea) that first led to his hypertension and diabetes being diagnosed and managed. At two-month follow-up the motor deficit has partially improved while the cognitive/language deficits remain prominent — a trajectory typical of ischemic (rather than destructive hemorrhagic) injury, and one that reveals differential recovery across the affected thalamic systems.

5 · Diagnostic Workup

  1. Noncontrast CT headFirst priority — distinguishes acute ischemia from hemorrhage before any antithrombotic therapy is considered.
  2. MRI brain with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)Confirms and precisely localizes the thalamic infarct; CT is often unremarkable this early in a small lacunar stroke.
  3. MRA/CTA of the head and neckEvaluates the posterior circulation and thalamic perforator origins, given his history argues against a carotid source.
  4. Cardiac workup — EKG, telemetry, echocardiogramScreens for an embolic source (atrial fibrillation, mural thrombus) that would change secondary prevention.
  5. Fasting glucose / HbA1c, lipid panelReassesses control of his two known small-vessel risk factors, diabetes and hyperlipidemia risk.
  6. Repeat carotid duplexWas normal 6 years ago; worth repeating given new symptoms and the years elapsed.

6 · Prognosis

Thalamic strokes often show this same split trajectory: motor deficits driven by corticospinal fiber involvement tend to improve substantially over weeks to months (as seen here — right arm and leg strength partially recovered by two months), while cognitive, memory, and language deficits tied to the limbic and associative thalamic nuclei (anterior, dorsomedial, pulvinar/lateroposterior) are frequently more persistent. His continued disorientation and confusion at follow-up, despite motor improvement, fits this pattern and suggests durable involvement of the limbic thalamic territory.

7 · Management

  • Acute stroke protocol on arrival; reperfusion therapy eligibility limited by unknown ("wake-up") onset time
  • Antiplatelet therapy (e.g., aspirin) once hemorrhage is excluded on imaging
  • Statin therapy for secondary vascular prevention
  • Tight blood pressure control — his 180/100 on presentation needs an active management plan, not just monitoring
  • Optimize glycemic control / review his insulin regimen
  • Physical and occupational therapy for residual right-sided weakness
  • Speech-language therapy targeting his thalamic aphasia pattern
  • Neuropsychological evaluation and cognitive rehabilitation for persistent memory/orientation deficits

Concept Deep-Dive

Localization

Thalamic Nuclear Map

This patient's findings map onto at least three adjacent nuclear groups (AN/DM for memory, VL/VA-adjacent capsule for motor, VPL for sensory, pulvinar/LGN territory for vision) — exactly the kind of multi-system picture the textbook warns a single, well-placed thalamic lesion can produce.

Localization

The Limbic Thalamus & Memory

The anterior and dorsomedial nuclei form the "limbic thalamus." Their damage explains this patient's amnesia and disorientation directly — no cortical lesion is needed to account for his confusion and poor fund of knowledge.

Visual pathway

Post-Geniculate Hemianopsia

Because his hemianopsia is dense and complete rather than macula-sparing, the lesion most likely sits at or before the LGN — in the geniculate itself or the optic radiations just beyond it — not in the visual cortex.
