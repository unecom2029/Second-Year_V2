Block 5 · Medical Neuroanatomy · Chapter 07 · Case Study 7-1

Case 7-1

57-year-old woman with acute right ptosis and diplopia — a right oculomotor (CN III) palsy with pupil sparing, plus contralateral corticospinal signs. Working localization: right midbrain (crus cerebri / oculomotor fascicles) — Weber's syndrome.

Localization Finding Term Pattern Genetics / Workup / Trajectory

Chief Complaint

This 57-year-old, right-handed woman is presenting with a marked weakness of her right eyelid and double vision when her eye is forced open1.

History of Chief Complaint

She had experienced two episodes of blurred vision and headache on the day prior to admission2. That evening she went to bed early. She awoke with an inability to open the right eyelid3. When she held the eyelid open with her finger, she noted marked double vision.

Medical History

This patient had had moderate hypertension for 30 years4. The hypertension was managed using beta-blockers.

Family History

The patient has been married for 35 years and has three children in college. Both parents are living; her father has had a long history of hypertension5. She has a 25-pack-year history of smoking6 and claims to consume a moderate amount of alcohol each week.

Physical Examination

On examination she was stable, awake, oriented, and of anxious demeanor. The patient was well nourished, well hydrated, obese, and in poor physical condition; she appeared slightly older than her stated age. Optic discs had sharp edges8. Her chest was clear to auscultation and percussion. Her blood pressure was 160/1007; pulse, temperature, and respirations were physiologic. Abdomen was difficult to palpate because of her obesity; however, no tenderness was observed. Peripheral pulses were difficult to access; mild edema was present at the ankles but not at the wrists.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

The patient was alert and oriented to time and place. Speech was articulate and content was meaningful9. Memory and knowledge were appropriate for her background. She could follow two- and three-step commands and was an adequate historian.

Cranial Nerves

Visual fields were full to confrontation10. If asked to open her eyes, the right eyelid did not elevate beyond 2 mm; the left opened 10 to 12 mm. When the right eyelid was elevated by an external force, the right eye was deviated to the right and down11; she could effect no volitional medial or upward movement of the right eye12. The right eye did not respond to caloric testing, nor did it respond during attempted convergence13. A full range of motion was present with the left eye. Pupillary responses were intact bilaterally15. Although it did not move, the right eye would constrict with the left eye on attempted convergence14. Hearing was normal in both ears. A minor weakness was noted in the left corner of her mouth when she attempted to grimace16. Her palate elevated symmetrically, and corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes were intact17. Her tongue protruded on the midline and her shoulder shrug was symmetric and of physiologic strength.

Motor Exam

Strength was intact in all limbs. Deep tendon reflexes were physiologic and symmetric in the upper extremities and slightly increased in the left lower extremity compared to the right18. There was a left Babinski response19.

Sensory Exam

Pinprick, two-point discrimination, vibratory sense, and proprioception were present throughout face and body20.

Follow up

Follow up at four months post discharge reveals a persistent ophthalmoplegia involving the right eye and paralysis of the right eyelid21. Tendon reflexes are bilaterally symmetrical in all four limbs and there is no residual Babinski response on either side22.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

Localization

Right midbrain — crus cerebri / oculomotor fascicles

The right eye shows a complete CN III (oculomotor) deficit — ptosis, "down and out" positioning, and loss of medial/upward movement that persists even with caloric (vestibulo-ocular) stimulation, which localizes the problem to the nucleus/fascicle itself rather than a supranuclear gaze pathway. At the same time, the left corner of the mouth, left leg reflexes, and left Babinski sign point to a contralateral corticospinal (upper motor neuron) deficit. An ipsilateral CN III palsy plus a contralateral body deficit is the signature "crossed" pattern of a single lesion in the midbrain, where the exiting third nerve fascicles pass directly through the corticospinal fibers of the crus cerebri.

Distribution

Focal

Vision, hearing, sensation, and every other cranial nerve tested are intact, and mental status is entirely normal. The deficits cluster tightly around one anatomic neighborhood — the right oculomotor complex and the adjacent corticospinal tract — rather than scattering across multiple unrelated pathways. This is a single, discrete lesion, not a multifocal or diffuse process.

Time Course

Acute, with a possible warning sign

The deficit was fully present on waking, after two transient episodes of blurred vision and headache the day before — a pattern that fits an acute ischemic event, possibly with a preceding warning (TIA-like) episode. There is no gradual, weeks-to-months progression that would suggest a mass lesion.

Diagnostic Workup

Sequenced from "rule out the emergency" to "confirm and characterize"

1
Non-contrast CT head, emergently
Rules out hemorrhage first — the step that determines whether anything else (like antiplatelet therapy) is safe to start.
2
MRI brain with diffusion-weighted imaging
Confirms and precisely localizes an acute midbrain infarct; DWI is far more sensitive than CT for a small brainstem lesion like this.
3
MRA/CTA head and neck
Any new CN III palsy gets vascular imaging to exclude a compressive aneurysm — even though pupil sparing makes this less likely here, it's not excluded by exam alone.
4
Fasting glucose / HbA1c, lipid panel
Screens for diabetic microvascular disease and other modifiable atherosclerotic risk factors contributing to the event.
5
Cardiac workup (ECG ± echocardiogram)
Evaluates for an embolic source if the stroke mechanism isn't clearly a small penetrating-artery (lacunar) infarct.
Prognosis

Split recovery, as seen at 4-month follow-up

Small penetrating-artery infarcts of the crus cerebri often show meaningful recovery of the corticospinal (contralateral) component over weeks to months — exactly what happened here, with the left Babinski and hyperreflexia resolving by four months. The oculomotor deficit, however, tends to be less forgiving: injury to the nucleus or fascicles themselves is more likely to leave a persistent ptosis and ophthalmoplegia, as this patient experienced.

Management

Checklist

  • Acute stroke pathway: blood pressure and glucose control; assess thrombolysis/thrombectomy eligibility (wake-up stroke protocols rely on MRI mismatch criteria) once hemorrhage is excluded
  • Antiplatelet therapy for secondary prevention once imaging supports it
  • Strict long-term blood pressure control and statin therapy
  • Smoking cessation counseling
  • Supportive care for the eye: lid taping or a ptosis crutch for the eyelid, prism lenses or patching for diplopia
  • Neuro-ophthalmology follow-up to track any recovery of extraocular function
  • Physical/occupational therapy if any residual left-sided weakness or coordination deficit remains

Concept Deep-Dive

The neuroanatomy underneath this case's findings

Localization

The oculomotor nucleus and "down and out"

With every CN III–innervated muscle (medial rectus, superior rectus, inferior rectus, inferior oblique, plus the levator palpebrae) out of action, only the lateral rectus (CN VI) and superior oblique (CN IV) keep working — pulling the eye laterally and slightly downward. That is exactly the "deviated to the right and down" position this patient showed.

Pattern Recognition

Pupil-sparing vs. pupil-involving CN III palsy

Parasympathetic (pupillary) fibers ride along the outer surface of the CN III trunk, so a compressive lesion — like an aneurysm — squeezes them first and blows the pupil early. Ischemic lesions tend to damage the core somatic-motor fibers preferentially, sparing the pupil, exactly as seen in this patient.

Vasculature

Midbrain perfusion zones

This patient's lesion sits in the anterolateral penetrating-artery territory (basilar, posterior cerebral, collicular, or anterior choroidal branches) — the zone that supplies the oculomotor fascicles and crus cerebri together, which is exactly why one small infarct produces both deficits at once.

This patient's ipsilateral CN III palsy plus contralateral spastic-pattern signs (hyperreflexia, Babinski) — with sensation and the rest of cranial nerve function fully intact — maps directly onto Weber's syndrome rather than the ataxia-predominant paramedian syndrome or the tremor-predominant Benedict's syndrome.

Localization Logic

Why a "crossed" pattern means the brainstem

Cranial nerve nuclei give rise to nerves that exit at their own level, before any crossing happens — so a CN III deficit shows up on the same side as the lesion. Corticospinal fibers, by contrast, have already crossed in the medulla by the time they reach the limbs — so a lesion in the right crus cerebri produces weakness on the left. One lesion, two sides — that mismatch is the tell.

Framework

Sorting each finding into segmental vs. suprasegmental is what turns a long list of exam findings into a single anatomic story: one small lesion, hitting a segmental (cranial nerve) structure and a suprasegmental (motor tract) structure that happen to sit side by side.
