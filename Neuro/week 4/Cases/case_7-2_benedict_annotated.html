Block 5 · Medical Neuroanatomy · Chapter 07 · Case Study 7-2

Case 7-2

79-year-old man with transient loss of consciousness, a partial pupil-involving right CN III palsy, and contralateral intention tremor / ataxia — a right midbrain lesion touching the oculomotor root, red nucleus, and dentothalamic tract. Working localization: Benedict's syndrome, most likely cardioembolic (atrial fibrillation).

Localization Finding Term Pattern Genetics / Workup / Trajectory

Chief Complaint

A 79-year-old, right-handed, retired business executive was brought to his general practitioner's office by his son after suffering a momentary loss of consciousness1 followed by the onset of double vision and a tremor in his left arm2.

History of Chief Complaint

He complained of frequent dizzy periods over the last 5 days3.

Past Medical History

He had been married for 40 years; his wife had died 5 years earlier. He had been an executive for a large firm. After retirement he had been active socially and played sports. His past medical history was positive for rheumatic fever at age 64. For the past 5 months he had been experiencing periods of dizziness and fatigue5. He had a 30-pack-year history of smoking but quit completely 3 years prior6. He drank 2 to 3 ounces of alcohol socially per week.

General Physical Examination

He was alert and oriented, well nourished, and of average weight; he appeared his stated age. The patient frequently had to cover his right eye with his hand in order to move about the room7. Optic discs were clear with sharp borders. External auditory canals were patent. His neck was supple and there were no bruits over the carotid arteries. His larynx and pharynx were non-reddened. His chest was clear to auscultation and percussion; abdomen was soft without rigidity, tenderness, or organomegaly. Heart rate was irregularly irregular8. Peripheral pulses were intact; a pulse deficit was present, with the auscultated apical rate exceeding the radial pulse rate9. Blood pressure was 135/93, temperature was 37°C, and respirations were 16/min. No cervical, axillary, or inguinal lymphadenopathy was present.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

He was alert and oriented to time and place with memory and knowledge appropriate for his age10. He was articulate in speech and had good comprehension of spoken and written language. He gave a comprehensive history.

Cranial Nerves

On forward gaze, with the lid forcibly elevated, the right eye had an external strabismus11; on attempted left lateral gaze, the right eye drifted toward the midline12. The right pupil was larger than the left13. The right pupil was unresponsive to light shined in either eye; the left pupil was responsive to direct and consensual light14. The right eyelid elevated 4 mm, whereas the left elevated 13 mm on forward gaze15. With the right eyelid forcibly elevated, its visual field was full to confrontation. The visual field in the left eye was also full. The patient noted diplopia on attempted forward gaze. The diplopia was absent with the right eye covered and exacerbated when the right eyelid was fully elevated16. Hearing was normal in both ears. He had a full range of facial expressions. Jaw-jerk and corneal reflexes were normal; the palate was elevated on the midline; gag reflex was normal, and tongue protruded on the midline.

Motor Exam

Strength was slightly diminished in the left limbs; deep tendon reflexes were +2/4 on the right and +3/4 on the left17. Babinski response was equivocal on the left and not present on the right18. A tremor of intent was present in the left arm19. Finger-to-nose testing was normal on the right, but he was slightly off target when using the left upper limb20. The left arm and hand displayed an occasional jerky movement that the patient could not suppress21.

Sensory Exam

Pinprick and temperature sensation were normal throughout body and face; position sense and vibratory sensation on the left side of his body was diminished. This sensory loss was more noticeable in the upper than in the lower extremity22.

Follow up

Upon examination 3 months following hospital discharge, the patient had normal gross motor strength with +2/4 deep tendon reflexes about the elbow, wrist, knee and ankle bilaterally. Movements of the left extremities were smooth and steady with no indication of past-pointing remaining23. Position and vibratory senses were normal in all extremities on the right and the lower extremity on the left. He continued to experience a diminution of position and vibratory senses in the left upper extremity.24 He could move his right eye into all four quadrants but complained of a mild diplopia and demonstrated a slight external strabismus at rest25.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

Localization → distribution → time course → differential → workup → prognosis → management

Localization

Right midbrain — oculomotor root, red nucleus, dentothalamic tract

The right eye shows a partial, pupil-involving CN III palsy — a dilated, fixed pupil, incomplete ptosis, and exotropia at rest, but with some medial movement still preserved on attempted gaze. At the same time, the left arm shows an intention tremor, dysmetria, and involuntary jerky movements — an ataxic, hyperkinetic pattern rather than a spastic one, with only mild, equivocal corticospinal signs alongside it. An ipsilateral (right) CN III deficit plus contralateral (left) cerebellar-outflow signs, with just a hint of corticospinal overlap, is the signature of a lesion touching the right oculomotor nerve root, red nucleus, and dentothalamic tract together — the pattern classically described as Benedict's syndrome.

Distribution

Focal

Cognition, hearing, most cranial nerves, and cortical function are entirely preserved. The deficits cluster tightly around structures that sit next to each other in the midbrain tegmentum — the oculomotor root, red nucleus/dentothalamic tract, and medial lemniscus — rather than scattering across unrelated pathways.

Time Course

Escalating, then acute

Five months of intermittent dizziness and fatigue sharpened into more frequent spells over the final five days, then culminated in an abrupt event — momentary loss of consciousness followed by fixed focal deficits. This pattern fits a slowly building vascular/cardiac problem producing escalating transient episodes before a larger embolus lodges and causes a fixed infarct.

Diagnostic Workup

Confirm the arrhythmia, rule out the emergency, then localize and source the clot

1
ECG
Confirms and characterizes the arrhythmia already suggested by the pulse deficit — atrial fibrillation versus another cause of irregular rhythm.
2
Non-contrast CT head, emergently
Excludes hemorrhage before anything else, including any anticoagulation decision.
3
MRI brain with diffusion-weighted imaging
Confirms and precisely localizes the midbrain infarct — small brainstem lesions like this one are easy to miss on CT.
4
Transthoracic (± transesophageal) echocardiogram
Looks for a left atrial or appendage thrombus and for valvular disease — relevant given his childhood rheumatic fever.
5
MRA/CTA head and neck
Assesses the posterior circulation for atherosclerotic disease and excludes an aneurysm, given the pupil-involving CN III findings.
6
CHA₂DS₂-VASc risk stratification
Once hemorrhage is excluded, guides the anticoagulation decision for secondary stroke prevention.
Prognosis

Partial injury, partial — but real — recovery

Because the initial CN III involvement was partial rather than complete, and the dominant findings (intention tremor, dysmetria, involuntary movement) came from a cerebellar-outflow pathway with real capacity to recover, this pattern has more room to improve than a completely destroyed nucleus or fascicle. The 3-month follow-up shows exactly that pattern: the ataxic signs and gross weakness fully resolved and extraocular movement returned in all four quadrants, while the upper-extremity proprioceptive loss and mild residual diplopia/strabismus show that recovery, while substantial, was not complete.

Management

Checklist

  • Urgent cardiology evaluation for newly identified atrial fibrillation — rate or rhythm control
  • Anticoagulation for stroke prevention once hemorrhage is excluded and bleeding risk assessed — the highest-yield secondary-prevention step here
  • Acute stroke pathway: blood pressure and glucose management; assess thrombolysis/thrombectomy eligibility based on timing
  • Supportive care for diplopia (prism lenses or patching) and residual ptosis
  • Physical/occupational therapy for residual ataxia and proprioceptive loss
  • Fall-risk precautions, given his dizziness history and residual left-sided sensory loss
  • Cardiology/primary care follow-up to monitor rhythm control and anticoagulation

Concept Deep-Dive

The neuroanatomy underneath this case's findings

Localization

Red nucleus, the dentothalamic tract, and rubral tremor

The dentothalamic tract — carrying the cerebellum's outflow to the thalamus — passes directly through and around the red nucleus. A lesion here disrupts this cerebellar feedback loop, producing an ataxic, intention-type tremor and dysmetria in the contralateral limb, rather than the spastic weakness that a corticospinal lesion would cause.

Pattern Recognition

This time, the pupil is involved

In Case 7-1, ischemia hit the central somatic-motor fibers and spared the peripheral parasympathetic rim, sparing the pupil. Here, the pattern is reversed: the parasympathetic fibers are clearly affected (a dilated, fixed pupil) while some somatic motor function (partial lid elevation, partial adduction) survives — the pattern more typical of a compressive or partial-injury mechanism than a pure central infarct.

An ipsilateral CN III palsy with contralateral hyperkinesia (tremor, dysmetria, involuntary movement) and only mild corticospinal overlap maps onto Benedict's syndrome — a step further back (more tegmental) than the pure crus cerebri involvement of Case 7-1's Weber's syndrome.

Mechanism

Atrial fibrillation and the pulse deficit

In atrial fibrillation, the atria quiver instead of contracting in a coordinated way, so blood can stagnate and clot — especially in the left atrial appendage. Fragments of that clot can travel through the posterior circulation to lodge in a small midbrain artery. The pulse deficit itself (apical rate exceeding radial rate) is simply the bedside sign that some of those irregular beats are too weak to produce a felt peripheral pulse.

Localization

Why the arm, and not the leg

The medial lemniscus carries a rough somatotopic map that rotates as it climbs the brainstem. By the midbrain, the arm/face fibers sit closer to the medial, tegmental region while the leg fibers lie more laterally — so a medially-placed partial lesion like this one can knock out arm proprioception while sparing the leg, exactly the gradient this patient showed.

Framework

Pyramidal vs. cerebellar-outflow signs, applied to this patient

Sign typeFindings here
Pyramidal (corticospinal / UMN)Mild left weakness, +3/4 vs +2/4 reflexes, equivocal Babinski — present, but faint
Cerebellar-outflow (rubral)Intention tremor, dysmetria, involuntary jerky movement — clearly present, dominant

Sorting the findings this way makes the diagnosis almost write itself: the cerebellar-outflow column is loud and the pyramidal column is barely whispering, which is exactly why Benedict's syndrome — not Weber's — is the better fit here, even though the two territories are close enough in the midbrain to overlap a little.
