Left Posterior Cerebral Artery Territory Infarct — Alexia, Color Anomia & Transient Amnesia

Chief Complaint

A 33 year-old man with memory loss and complaining of visual hallucinations.

History of the Chief Complaint

A 33-year-old unemployed migrant worker1 was brought to the emergency room by the police. He had been found wandering the streets and rubbing his nose2. He was quite confused and disoriented, complaining of smelling burning odors3 and seeing large people and trucks4 in the emergency room. The patient could give no information on his activities for the last 3 hours5.

Medical History

The patient denied any recent or past history of trauma, and there were no indications of trauma6 (bruises, swelling, or discoloration) on his head or body.

General Physical Examination

This was a young man of good physical condition who was confused and disoriented, with noticeable defects in memory. Because of his memory dysfunction, his correct age was not known until obtained at a later date from his last place of employment7. His chest was clear to auscultation and palpation. The abdomen was soft, with no masses or tenderness. Lymph nodes were not palpable in axilla or groin. Blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respirations were normal.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

At the time of admission the patient was cooperative, yet confused and disoriented with respect to time and place8. He could not state his name or age but could describe and name the farm where he had last worked9; otherwise he could supply only a few details of his past life. He had experienced periods of global amnesia during the past 24 hours10 and described numerous visual and olfactory hallucinations. He was able to follow two- and three-step commands accurately but could not repeat the commands by memory after a delay of 5 minutes11. His speech and comprehension of language were appropriate; however, he was unable to read12. He could recite the first 10 letters in the alphabet with one or two mistakes14. He could write several words such as cat or dog and could copy words, but was unable to read what he had copied13. He could do simple one- and two-digit calculations. He was unable to identify colors and could not provide the correct name for many common objects, such as door or window, but he could describe these objects and state their use15.

Cranial Nerves

Optic discs were clear, with sharp borders; a pronounced deficit in the right visual field was present16. Jaw-jerk and gag reflexes were normal, facial expressions were complete and symmetric, palate and uvula elevated symmetrically, and the tongue protruded on the midline.

Motor Systems

His motor system was intact throughout his body, with normal deep tendon reflexes and no loss of strength17.

Sensorium

Pinprick, temperature, vibratory, and proprioceptive sensation were intact throughout his body and face.

Follow-up

The patient was admitted to the hospital for observation. After 24 hours the visual and olfactory hallucinations decreased18. Two days later he was discharged to a local community shelter for assistance. Examination 2 weeks later revealed that the defect in forming recent memories had cleared; however, he still could not recall events from 24 hours prior to his admission through the end of his first week of discharge19. He was capable of supplying his name and some details from his life, but still could not recall his age. He could do simple arithmetic calculations. However, the alexia and a pronounced anomia for visual objects and colors were observed to persist20.

Below the Case

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

1 · Localization

Left (dominant) PCA territory

Three findings triangulate the same left-sided lesion: a right homonymous hemianopia (left occipital/optic radiation damage), alexia without agraphia plus color anomia (a splenium-of-corpus-callosum disconnection between an intact right visual cortex and left language areas), and transient anterograde amnesia (hippocampal ischemia). A fully normal motor exam argues against the deeper penetrating branches that supply the internal capsule. One caveat worth flagging: global amnesia and confusion like this can also show up as a nonspecific cortical symptom after any sufficiently large infarct — from reduced perfusion pressure spilling over into adjacent, non-infarcted territory — so on its own it isn't as reliable a localizing sign as the hemianopia and alexia are; it's the combination with those focal findings that pins this down to the left PCA.

2 · Distribution

Focal — one vascular territory, several structures

The occipital cortex, splenium, and medial temporal lobe/hippocampus are anatomically separate but are all supplied by the same left posterior cerebral artery — so a single focal infarct in this territory naturally produces this whole multi-symptom picture, rather than this being truly "multifocal" disease.

3 · Time Course

Acute onset, mixed recovery

Sudden onset with an unaccounted-for 3-hour gap, hallucinations peaking then fading over ~24 hours, and a 2-week follow-up showing recovered new-memory formation alongside persistent alexia and color anomia — a partial, non-uniform recovery typical of an evolving-then-settling ischemic event.

4 · Differential Diagnosis
DiagnosisFitsArgues Against
Left PCA-territory infarction (occipital / splenial / mesial temporal)
Most Likely
 Alexia without agraphia + color anomia + right hemianopia + transient hippocampal-type amnesia + visual/olfactory hallucinations together are essentially the textbook description of a dominant PCA stroke. His young age (33) and no documented vascular risk factors are atypical for a routine atherosclerotic stroke — an embolic source or unusual risk factor still needs to be sought.
Toxic-metabolic encephalopathy (e.g., thiamine deficiency) Confusion, wandering, amnesia, hallucinations, and real nutritional vulnerability as an unemployed migrant worker. The exam is too focal (fixed right hemianopia, alexia, color anomia) for a typically diffuse metabolic process; the classic ophthalmoplegia/ataxia of Wernicke's isn't described.
Herpes simplex encephalitis Amnesia, olfactory hallucinations, and confusion fit its temporal-lobe predilection. No fever or seizures reported; the visual/splenial findings (hemianopia, alexia) aren't typical of HSE's usual pattern — needs LP/MRI to exclude.
Transient global amnesia (TGA) Dense, self-limited anterograde amnesia. TGA doesn't produce a visual field cut, alexia, or color anomia — and this patient's alexia/anomia persisted well beyond his memory recovery, which TGA shouldn't do at all.
Postictal state after an unwitnessed seizure Confusion, hallucinations, and amnesia for a discrete period. No seizure was witnessed, and fixed focal deficits persisting two weeks are not typical of a purely postictal (Todd's) phenomenon, which usually clears within hours to a couple of days.
5 · Diagnostic Workup
  1. Non-contrast CT head, then MRI brain with DWI
    Confirm and characterize the suspected left PCA-territory infarct across occipital cortex, splenium, and mesial temporal lobe.
  2. CTA/MRA of the head and neck (posterior circulation)
    Look for an embolic or thrombotic source, or a dissection — relevant given how young he is for a routine stroke presentation.
  3. Formal visual field testing (perimetry)
    Characterize and document the right homonymous hemianopia for baseline and rehab planning.
  4. Cardiac workup
    Echocardiogram for an embolic source and telemetry for arrhythmia (e.g., atrial fibrillation), since atherosclerotic risk factors are less likely to explain a stroke at his age.
  5. Labs, toxicology, and nutritional screen
    Glucose, electrolytes, toxicology, and thiamine — to identify or exclude a contributing toxic-metabolic component given his social history, even though it isn't the primary explanation for his focal signs.
  6. Neuropsychological testing
    Formally characterize the alexia, color anomia, and memory pattern, and establish a baseline for tracking recovery.
6 · Prognosis

Focal cortical/disconnection deficits from an established infarct — alexia without agraphia and color anomia — tend to improve only partially, and some patients are left with a lasting reading impairment. The memory pattern seen here (new-memory formation recovering, but a fixed retrograde gap bridging the injury period) is typical after transient hippocampal ischemia, and that residual gap is often permanent even as day-to-day memory function otherwise normalizes. The right homonymous hemianopia may improve somewhat but frequently remains at least partially fixed.

7 · Management
  • Acute/subacute stroke work-up (imaging, vascular imaging, cardiac evaluation) to identify and treat the underlying mechanism
  • Secondary stroke prevention — antiplatelet or anticoagulation depending on the identified etiology, plus risk-factor modification
  • Vision rehabilitation — compensatory scanning strategies and low-vision services for the right hemianopia and alexia
  • Speech-language pathology referral for alexia and anomia
  • Ongoing neuropsychological follow-up to track memory recovery
  • Nutritional/toxic-metabolic screening and repletion (e.g., thiamine) given his social vulnerability, as a secondary but real consideration
  • Social work / case-management support — he was discharged to a community shelter, so connecting him to consistent follow-up care is itself part of his management
Underlying Neuroanatomy

Concept Deep-Dive

Localization

Left PCA Vascular Territory

A single left PCA infarct naturally produces this patient's whole picture at once: the cortical branches explain his hemianopia and visual hallucinations, the splenial branch explains his alexia and color anomia, and the penetrating branches explain his transient amnesia.

Localization

Alexia Without Agraphia — a Disconnection

Right visual cortex sees the letters fine, but the damaged splenium blocks that visual information from ever reaching left-hemisphere language cortex — so he can't read. Writing uses a completely separate, undamaged pathway, so it stays intact. This exact combination is why he could copy "cat" or "dog" but couldn't read the words back.

Anomia vs. Agnosia

He couldn't name colors or objects like "door," but he could describe them and state their use — proof that recognition and conceptual knowledge are intact and only the naming link is broken. That distinction is what makes this anomia rather than a visual agnosia, and it's the same left occipital–splenial disconnection driving his alexia.

Trajectory

Same broad category — hippocampal-type amnesia — but a completely different trajectory. Case 10-2's bilateral anoxic injury left a permanent, isolated memory deficit; this patient's unilateral, PCA-territory ischemia mostly recovered on the memory side, but left him with fixed cortical deficits (alexia, color anomia) that anoxic-only injury wouldn't produce.

Pattern

Ischemic vs. Ictal Hallucinations

This patient's hallucinations come from ischemic tissue misfiring as it's dying and recovering — not from a seizure discharge like Case 10-1's uncinate fits. That's consistent with the timeline too: his hallucinations faded over 24 hours as the acute event settled, rather than recurring in brief stereotyped episodes.

Localization

Right Homonymous Hemianopia

Fibers carrying the right visual field cross at the chiasm and travel through the left optic tract, radiations, and occipital cortex. A lesion there — exactly where this patient's left PCA territory sits — produces a deficit in the right field, even though the eyes themselves are entirely normal. Classic PCA infarcts like this one also tend to spare the macula: the occipital pole representing central vision gets a dual blood supply from both the PCA and the MCA, so when the PCA is blocked, that small central strip often keeps working even as the rest of the field is lost.

Localization

Reading & Writing: Three Lesion Sites

A — visual cortex + splenium, dominant-hemisphere/PCA territory (this patient): the intact right visual cortex sees the page fine, but the disconnection means that information never reaches left-hemisphere language cortex — alexia without agraphia. B — the splenial crossing pathway alone, without the visual cortex damage: no clinical deficit, because normal reading doesn't strictly depend on that crossing when the primary visual input itself is intact. C — Exner's area, the motor-planning region for handwriting near the premotor/frontal eye fields, MCA territory: damage here gives the mirror-image syndrome, agraphia without alexia — he can still read, just can't plan the movements to write.

Term

Visual Field Deficits Along the Pathway

Lesion siteDeficit
Optic nerveMonocular loss (one eye)
Optic chiasmBitemporal loss (e.g., pituitary mass)
Optic tract / full radiations / cortexComplete contralateral homonymous hemianopia

Hitting every optic radiation fiber (as in this patient) gives a complete homonymous hemianopia; hitting only some of them gives a quadrantanopia instead, and which quadrant is lost depends on which subset of fibers is involved. This is why the size and exact location of a PCA-territory infarct — not just its vascular territory — determines whether a patient ends up with a full field cut or just a quadrant, and whether the macula is spared.
