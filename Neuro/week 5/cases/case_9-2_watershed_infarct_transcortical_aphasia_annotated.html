Paresis of the Distal Lower Extremity, Confusion, and Aphasia

52-year-old man — collapsed and unconscious for 2 days, then slow, incomplete recovery

Chief Complaint

A 52-year-old man with paresis of the distal lower extremity, confusion, and aphasia.

History of Chief Complaint

This 52-year-old, right-handed1 male was brought to the emergency room by ambulance after losing consciousness in a restaurant on a Sunday afternoon. Although he maintained vital signs, he remained unconscious for 2 days2, after which he began responding to external stimuli. Over a 2-week period he gradually regained consciousness. At this point he was re-examined for evaluation of future course.

Past Medical History

At the time of admission he was a post office employee, married, with three children, all in high school and living at home. His family stated that he did not smoke but admitted that he drank three or four glasses of beer per week3. He had been in good health up until the apoplectic episode. His father had died of cerebrovascular disease 10 years before, at the age of 644; his mother was living and in good health.

General Physical Examination

The patient was a well-nourished, well-hydrated male with male-pattern baldness and he appeared his stated age. He was awake and fully cooperative but was disoriented for time and place5. He had difficulty recognizing family members and hospital staff6. He was overweight and appeared anxious. His optic discs were clear and sharp, and visual acuity was good. The neck was supple, with no bruits7 or lymphadenopathy. The chest was clear to percussion; the abdomen was soft, with no masses or tenderness. Peripheral pulses were intact at the wrist and ankle. Skin was moist and warm.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

The patient was an awake, fully cooperative, but disoriented male. His volitional speech was extremely nonfluent, consisting of several short phrases, such as, "no… no... no... no... no," or "tat... tat… tat."8 He repeated these phrases many times when attempting to answer questions. He could, however, repeat complicated phrases following the examiner's lead, such as "no ifs, ands, or buts."9 Yet he could not recite the days of the week or months of the year when asked. Although he could understand simple commands (e.g., "Point to the door."), his comprehension of language was extremely poor. He never understood two- or three-step commands.10 He could read a few words aloud but could not comprehend what he had read11; he could not write or draw even simple figures12.

Cranial Nerves

He had a full range of eye movements but tended to keep his eyes positioned to the left while resting13. Visual acuity was difficult to test, but he was capable of reading 8-point type. Both pupils were reactive to light, direct and consensual. Hearing could not be tested accurately. Corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes were intact. There was a mild weakness in the right lower quadrant of his face15. The uvula was elevated on the midline, and the tongue protruded on the midline. Snout, grasp, and suck reflexes were not present14.

Motor Systems

Strength was diminished on the right, more so in the leg than in the arm16. Deep tendon reflexes were elevated on the right compared to the left. A Babinski sign was present on the right17, he was incontinent for urine and feces and was visibly upset when this occurred18.

Sensorium

The sensorium was difficult to examine because of the patient's poor mental status. Pinprick, temperature, vibratory, and proprioceptive senses appeared intact throughout the body and face, with the exception of some loss in the lower extremity on the right19.

Follow-Up

He remained in the hospital for two weeks and was then discharged to a nursing home for critical care. Re-evaluation at 4 months after hospital discharge finds little improvement in comprehension or language capabilities20. Gross strength had improved on the right side but deep tendon reflexes remained elevated21. His sensorium was still difficult to evaluate due to his poor language and comprehension skills.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough
From bedside findings to diagnosis
Localization

Left (medial) hemisphere — ACA territory, plus association cortex

A leg-greater-than-arm right hemiparesis with a Babinski sign points to the medial cortical surface (paracentral lobule) — anterior cerebral artery territory — rather than the lateral convexity. A leftward resting gaze preference is consistent with a destructive left frontal lesion. But the language findings go further than a single ACA infarct would explain: nonfluent output plus severely impaired comprehension plus preserved repetition together implicate damage to both anterior and posterior language-adjacent association cortex, sparing only the core perisylvian zone — a picture of transcortical mixed aphasia ("isolation of the speech area").

Distribution

Multifocal / diffuse, not focal

Global disorientation, impaired recognition of familiar people, damage spanning both anterior and posterior language networks, and a leg-predominant motor pattern together outgrow what a single, discrete arterial occlusion would produce. This looks like extensive or bilateral border-zone (watershed) involvement rather than one focal infarct.

Time course

Acute collapse, prolonged coma-like state, partial plateau

A sudden collapse and loss of consciousness (an acute event) was followed by an unusually prolonged unconscious period (2 days) — far longer than a typical embolic stroke — then gradual, incomplete recovery over 2 weeks, and little further change in language by 4 months. This "acute insult → severe global depression → slow, partial, plateauing recovery" shape is distinct from Case 9-1's sharper, more complete recovery curve.

Diagnostic workup

What to order, and why

1
Non-contrast head CT
First priority — excludes hemorrhage and can show early watershed-pattern hypodensities.
2
MRI brain with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)
Confirms and maps the distribution — looking specifically for bilateral or extensive border-zone (ACA-MCA, MCA-PCA) infarction rather than a single-vessel territory.
3
Cardiac monitoring — ECG, telemetry/Holter, echocardiogram
Looks for the arrhythmia or cardiac event most likely responsible for the initial global hypoperfusion and collapse.
4
Review of prehospital/ED vital signs
Characterizes the hypotensive episode that likely triggered watershed injury.
5
Carotid imaging (duplex or CTA)
Even without a bruit on exam, checks for proximal stenosis that could have predisposed to watershed infarction during a hypotensive episode.
6
Basic labs — glucose, electrolytes, troponin, toxicology
Screens for a metabolic, cardiac, or toxic contributor to the initial collapse.
7
Formal neuropsychological / speech-language evaluation
Characterizes the transcortical mixed aphasia and broader cognitive deficits to guide rehabilitation and care planning.
Prognosis

Motor improves; language largely does not

By 4 months, right-sided strength had improved, but comprehension and language were essentially unchanged from the acute presentation, and reflexes remained elevated. Compared with Case 9-1's partial language recovery after a smaller, single-territory infarct, this more guarded, largely flat trajectory fits a more extensive — likely bilateral or border-zone — pattern of cortical and association-area injury.

Management

Treat the trigger, then support and rehabilitate

  • Identify and treat the underlying cause of the hypoperfusion episode (e.g., arrhythmia management, addressing any hypotensive cause)
  • Cardiac monitoring, with antiarrhythmic therapy or pacemaker evaluation if indicated
  • Supportive care during the acute period — airway/nutrition support and DVT prophylaxis given the prolonged unconsciousness
  • Bladder and bowel care for incontinence
  • Physical and occupational therapy for the leg-predominant hemiparesis
  • Speech-language therapy, with expectations set realistically given the extent of association-cortex involvement
  • Long-term/custodial care planning given the degree of persistent cognitive-language impairment
Concept Deep-Dive
The neuroanatomy behind the exam
Isolation of the Speech Area

Transcortical mixed aphasia

Watershed injury can knock out the association cortex surrounding the core perisylvian language loop while leaving Broca's area, Wernicke's area, and the arcuate fasciculus connecting them intact. The result: the patient can't generate spontaneous speech and can't understand it either — because the surrounding cortex that supplies meaning and drives production is damaged — but the isolated island can still pass a spoken phrase straight through, so repetition survives.

This patient: nonfluent, perseverative output + severely impaired comprehension + preserved repetition of complex phrases ("no ifs, ands, or buts") — the defining triad of transcortical mixed aphasia.
Watershed Infarction

Why border zones are vulnerable

Border zones — the strips of cortex lying between two arterial territories (gold) — are furthest from either artery's main supply and depend on the weakest, most distal branches. A global drop in perfusion pressure (cardiac arrest, sustained hypotension) starves these zones first, even while the core of each named territory stays relatively protected.

This patient: a sudden collapse with prolonged unconsciousness is exactly the kind of global hypoperfusion event that produces watershed, not single-vessel, injury.
ACA Territory

Why the leg (and continence) were hit

The leg's cortical representation rolls over onto the medial hemispheric surface, fed by the ACA — right next to the anterior cingulate and superior frontal cortex, whose damage classically produces incontinence and behavioral change. That's why an ACA-territory (or ACA-border-zone) lesion produces a leg-predominant weakness plus incontinence, rather than the arm/face-predominant pattern of an MCA lesion.

This patient: leg > arm weakness, right Babinski, and urinary/fecal incontinence — a textbook ACA-territory constellation.
Frontal Release Reflexes

Snout, grasp, suck — reflexes reawakened

ReflexElicited byMeaning if present in an adult
SnoutLight tap near the lipsPuckering response — frontal lobe disinhibition
GraspStroking the palmInvoluntary grasping — often frontal/premotor damage
SuckTouching the lipsSucking movement — diffuse frontal or bilateral cortical disease

These reflexes are normal in infancy and are actively suppressed by maturing frontal cortex. Their reappearance in an adult ("frontal release signs") suggests loss of that inhibitory control — but they're inconsistently present even with clear frontal pathology, so a negative exam (as here) doesn't rule frontal damage out.

This patient: all three were tested and absent — a negative finding that neither confirms nor excludes his frontal/medial cortical injury.
Reading Without Understanding

Sounding out words vs. grasping their meaning

Converting print to sound doesn't require understanding it — that pathway can stay intact while the deeper route from word to meaning is damaged. This patient could sound out written words yet not comprehend them, showing a comprehension-level (association-cortex) deficit rather than a problem with basic reading mechanics.

Compare: this is not "alexia without agraphia" (a posterior cerebral artery/splenium syndrome where a patient can write but not read at all) — here the mechanical act of reading survives, but meaning does not, and writing is lost too.
