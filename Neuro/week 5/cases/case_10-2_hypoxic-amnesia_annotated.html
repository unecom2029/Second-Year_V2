A 55-year-old man with profound memory loss following cardiovascular surgery

Chief Complaint

A profound memory loss following cardiovascular surgery1

History of Chief Complaint

This is a 55-year-old, male government worker retired because of medical disability. During coronary surgery he suffered several extended periods of anoxia2 and has experienced severe neurologic consequences. He is presenting for a checkup, 4 years postsurgery.

Past Medical History

Five years ago, this patient was diagnosed with coronary artery occlusion, and bypass surgery was performed. During recovery, an atrial tear resulted in a significant loss of blood3. The patient experienced a 15-minute period of hypoxia4. The tear was repaired; however, during this second trip to the operating room, the patient's EEG was flat and his pupils were fixed5. The following day, further bleeding required a third trip to the operating room; again his pupils were fixed and the EEG was diminished in amplitude6.

Over the next 2 days the patient gradually regained consciousness. He had reduced strength and paresthesia in the left arm7. He also demonstrated severe memory loss and confusion with respect to time and place8. Prior to the surgical event, he had no history of neurologic signs or symptoms.

General Physical Examination

This was a well-nourished, well-hydrated, obese male who was alert and cooperative and in no acute distress. Carotid auscultation revealed a soft bruit without radiation on the right9. Heartbeat was regular; a grade III harsh systolic murmur, auscultated best at the second intercostal space on the right, with an S4 gallop was present10. Peripheral pulses were intact. Lungs were clear to auscultation and percussion. The abdomen was soft, without masses, tenderness, rigidity, or rebound. A +2 peripheral, dependent edema was noted in the lower extremities bilaterally11. Skin was sallow in appearance, with poor texture and turgor. A well-healed cicatrix of the anterior chest wall extended from the second intercostal space to the diaphragmatic area; a healed 2-cm cicatrix was noted in the left antecubital fossa; and a healed 1-cm cicatrix was located in the left subclavicular area.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status. The patient was disoriented with respect to time and place and could not describe the reason for his past hospital confinement or recall the history of his illness. However, his speech, naming, reading aloud, and comprehension were all normal for his age12. He rapidly forgot information recently expressed to him; he could not recognize any words presented to him 5 minutes previously13. He could not recall the names of staff or physicians in either the office or the hospital. Prior to his illness, he had had a strong interest in American history and politics. During the examination he could recall most of the president's names and supply some details concerning their era, but he could not identify the current president14 or describe any recent historical events. He frequently relied on his family members to provide any recent descriptions of his life. He did not recall why he was ill or any of the events occurring around the time of his cardiac surgery. No other significant personality changes or cognitive deficits were detectable15 in the patient.

Cranial Nerves. His visual fields were full to confrontation; fundoscopic examination revealed AV nicking and silver wire changes16 without exudates, hemorrhages, or papilledema. His hearing was slightly diminished in both ears17. A full range of facial expression was present, and the jaw-jerk reflex was normal. Palate and uvula elevated symmetrically, and the tongue protruded on the midline.

Motor Systems. Although the patient had motion in all extremities, strength was diminished on the left side, with slightly elevated deep tendon reflexes and a Babinski sign18. Strength in the left upper extremity was +3/5 and +4/5 in the left lower extremity19.

Sensation. Vibratory sense, proprioception, and discriminative touch were diminished but not absent in the left and normal in the right extremities20. Paresthesias (tingling sensations) were found involving the patient's left hand and forearm21.

Synthesis — from Small Group

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

01 · Localization

Two hits, one mechanism

The amnesia traces to CA1 — described in small group as "the main outflow pathway from the hippocampus." Damage there severs the declarative memory loop (cingulate → hippocampus → fornix → mammillary body → anterior thalamic nucleus → cingulate) at its exit point, so no new information can complete the loop and get consolidated.

Separately, the left-sided weakness and sensory loss were localized in small group to anoxic injury of the right precentral gyrus (motor) and right postcentral gyrus (sensory) — two more "hits" from the same systemic event, not one shared lesion with the amnesia.

02 · Distribution

Multifocal — argued three ways

Not focal: no single lesion explains memory loss, motor weakness, and sensory loss together. Not diffuse: a diffuse process is typically progressive and affects the brain fairly equally — "the anoxia is not affecting everything equally," as the small group put it; it's closer to random.

That leaves multifocal: several discrete "hits" — hippocampal CA1, right precentral gyrus, right postcentral gyrus — from several extended periods of anoxia, not following one vascular territory or one hemisphere symmetrically.

03 · Time Course

Acute insult, stable ever since

Small group flagged this as a slightly unusual "clinical temporal profile" to reason through: he's being examined 4 years later, but every finding was established acutely — two documented intraoperative events (flat EEG/fixed pupils, then diminished-amplitude EEG) — with a 2-day gradual return of consciousness.

"It's cute, it's stable" — the deficit hasn't progressed since; it was fixed at the time of the surgery, which argues against anything neurodegenerative.

04 · Differential Diagnosis

Working differential

DiagnosisFitsArgues against
Most likely
Multifocal anoxic injury: CA1 outflow-tract damage (hippocampus) + right precentral/postcentral gyrus injury		 Two documented intraoperative hypotensive/hypoxic events; CA1 damage explains the severe anterograde + brief retrograde amnesia; separate anoxic "hits" to the motor and sensory strips explain the left-sided findings; stable 4-year course. Requires accepting several co-occurring, non-contiguous lesions from one systemic event — but that's exactly the multifocal pattern the small group argued for, and it's a better fit than any single-lesion explanation.
Bilateral paramedian thalamic infarction (e.g., artery of Percheron) Can produce a severe amnestic syndrome with preserved language. Classically causes decreased arousal/vertical gaze palsy at onset, which isn't described; doesn't explain the lateralized left-sided motor/sensory signs.
Cardioembolic right MCA territory stroke Would explain the left hemiparesis and hyperreflexia/Babinski. Doesn't account for the bilateral, temporally graded memory loss, which is the dominant and defining feature here.
Chronic hypertensive small-vessel (lacunar) disease AV nicking/silver-wire changes confirm real, longstanding hypertension as a contributing risk factor. Lacunes don't explain an abrupt, perioperative-onset amnestic syndrome this severe; this is a contributing comorbidity, not the primary mechanism.
Neurodegenerative dementia (e.g., early Alzheimer's disease) Anterograde amnesia is a hallmark early feature. Onset is sharply time-locked to a specific perioperative event, not insidious; course is static over 4 years rather than progressive.
05 · Diagnostic Workup

Next steps

1
MRI brain with DWI/FLAIR
Look for chronic signal change in bilateral hippocampal CA1 and in the right precentral/postcentral gyri; also excludes a mass or hemorrhage as an alternative explanation.
2
Formal neuropsychological / memory battery
Quantify the anterograde deficit and map the retrograde gradient (e.g., Wechsler Memory Scale) to confirm the pattern already suggested by the presidents question.
3
Echocardiogram ± carotid duplex
Characterize the valvular disease behind the murmur/S4 gallop and screen for a thrombus or embolic source; carotid duplex quantifies the stenosis behind the soft right-sided bruit.
4
Outpatient EEG
Assess for residual diffuse slowing or epileptiform activity, given that both intraoperative EEGs were markedly abnormal (flat, then low-amplitude).
5
Ophthalmologic exam
Formally grade the hypertensive retinopathy (AV nicking, silver wire) to stage chronic vascular risk burden and guide blood pressure targets.
06 · Prognosis

Fixed deficit, ongoing risk

Four years out, the anterograde amnesia is almost certainly permanent — the CA1 outflow tract doesn't regenerate. Small group noted brief retrograde amnesia "doesn't always return," though it can gradually improve; his motor/sensory deficit has already plateaued.

His ongoing cardiac disease (murmur, S4, edema) and hypertension mean he remains at real risk for a second hypoperfusion event — arguably the more urgent forward-looking concern.

07 · Management

Checklist

  • Cardiology follow-up to optimize the valvular disease and heart failure signs driving hemodynamic instability
  • Blood pressure control to slow further hypertensive small-vessel injury
  • Carotid duplex surveillance given the bruit, to determine if stenosis warrants intervention
  • Neuropsychological rehabilitation — external memory aids and structured routines, since the underlying tissue loss isn't reversible
  • PT/OT for the residual left arm/leg weakness and ADL adaptation
  • Safety and caregiver planning, given his reliance on family for recent-event recall
Underlying Neuroanatomy — as Drawn in Small Group

Concept Deep-Dive

Two long-term memory systems run through the limbic lobe, and this case's amnesia touches only one of them.

Declarative memory is explicit and conscious — facts and events you can consciously state (episodic: your own history and experiences; semantic: general knowledge about the world). It depends on the hippocampal–cingulate loop below, with CA1 as the exit ramp out of the hippocampus.

Emotional memory is a form of nondeclarative (implicit) memory — an unconscious association between a stimulus and a physiological/emotional response (fear, rage, anger, sadness), built through the amygdala rather than the hippocampus, and triggered by a stress cascade (CRH → cortisol) instead of a "note card" of facts.

Both loops were drawn out in small group on the same board (below) — they share a couple of downstream stations (mammillary body, septal area) but diverge right at the entrance. That's exactly why this patient can lose his ability to form new facts and events while his personality and emotional responses stay completely intact.

The "CA1 neurons" label marking the declarative lesion site is this patient's exact injury.

pattern

Where This Fits: The Broader Memory Taxonomy

Sensory input passes through sensory memory (under 3 seconds) and short-term/working memory (7±2 items, under a minute, extended by rehearsal) before encoding into long-term memory. Long-term memory then splits into declarative (explicit/conscious — episodic and semantic) and nondeclarative (implicit/unconscious — priming, procedural, associative, nonassociative). Notice where emotional memory actually sits: it's a subtype of associative nondeclarative memory, anatomically implemented by the amygdala loop on the board drawing above — a completely separate system from the declarative loop this patient's CA1 lesion damaged.

localization

The Declarative Memory Loop — Gross Anatomy

The same loop drawn in small group, on real tissue: cingulate gyrus → hippocampus → fornix (splitting toward the septal area and the mammillary body) → mammillothalamic tract → thalamus (anterior nucleus) → back to the cingulate. It takes roughly 2-3 passes around this loop to consolidate into cortical long-term storage. CA1 is the exit ramp out of the hippocampus — this patient's CA1 damage means no lap could ever complete.

pattern

A Second, Parallel Loop: Emotional Memory

Triggered differently — by a stress response (CRH → cortisol activating the amygdala), not a "note card" of facts — and critically, this loop never touches the fornix or CA1; it exits via the stria terminalis instead, toward the habenula, interpeduncular nucleus, and the monoamine nuclei (locus coeruleus/NE, raphe/5-HT, VTA/dopamine). That's the anatomic reason this patient's personality, behavior, and emotional responses are completely normal despite his severe amnesia — the two systems share a couple of downstream stations but diverge right at the entrance.

Small group named CA1 specifically as "the main outflow pathway from the hippocampus" — the neurons carrying processed information out toward the fornix. It's also the hippocampus's most resource-hungry region, which is exactly why a global hypoxic insult disables the loop's exit ramp specifically, rather than damaging the hippocampus diffusely.

pattern

Severe Anterograde + Brief Retrograde Amnesia — One Mechanism

Small group's explanation: a memory needs several passes around the loop before it's safely stored in cortex. His knowledge of old presidents had already finished that process years earlier; the current president — something he'd only recently been learning — was still "new" when the loop was severed, so it's lost along with everything else around that time. That's the brief retrograde amnesia; going forward, nothing new can complete the loop at all, which is the severe anterograde amnesia. Small group noted the retrograde piece "doesn't always return," though it can gradually improve.

localization

Multifocal, Not Diffuse: Precentral & Postcentral Gyri

Small group's reasoning for multifocal over diffuse: a diffuse process is typically progressive and hits the brain fairly equally on both sides, but "the anoxia is not affecting everything equally" here — it's closer to random. It hit the hippocampus (memory), the right precentral gyrus (motor), and the right postcentral gyrus (sensory) somewhat unevenly. These particular gyri sit near the anterior watershed zone between vascular territories, which is the anatomic reason they're especially exposed during a global hypotensive episode like this one.

workup / vascular

Vascular Anatomy: The Circle of Willis

Relevant to this case's own question 9 ("if the origin is vascular, what arterial supply is most likely involved?"). The ACA and MCA share a border a few centimeters off the midline near the vertex — exactly where the precentral/postcentral gyrus "hits" sit — so a global hypotensive episode preferentially starves that border first. The PCA and anterior choroidal artery separately supply the hippocampus and amygdala via thin, distal penetrating branches with limited collateral reserve, which is the second reason those medial temporal structures are early casualties of the same systemic event.

term

AV Nicking & Silver-Wire Arterioles

AV nicking = a thickened, hypertensive arteriole compressing the vein it crosses. Silver-wire arterioles = arteriolar wall thickening that increases the light reflex to a metallic sheen. Neither finding is acute — both are markers of the patient's chronic hypertension, a standing risk factor separate from (but compounding) the acute multifocal anoxic injury that caused his amnesia and weakness.
