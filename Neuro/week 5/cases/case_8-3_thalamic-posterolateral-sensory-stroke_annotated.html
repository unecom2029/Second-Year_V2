Case Study 8-3

A 55-year-old man with sudden left-sided sensory loss and eyes-closed athetoid movements — a thalamic pure sensory stroke unfolding against a backdrop of longstanding myotonic dystrophy.

Chief Complaint

A 55-year-old man presents with left-sided sensory loss1 and athetotic movements2.

History of Chief Complaint

This 55-year-old man experienced a sudden onset3 of numbness in his left upper limb while eating supper. When it persisted, he consulted his family physician.

Medical History

At the time of examination he was unmarried and worked in a factory performing quality-control inspections. Both of his parents were alive, and he had lived with them all his life. He was diagnosed with myotonic dystrophy4 at 33 years of age; its course had been a slow, progressive increase in proximal muscle weakness5 since that time.

General Physical Examination

The patient was an awake and oriented male with significant muscle wasting, especially in the proximal limb muscles6. His movements were punctuated by occasional tonic muscle contractions of considerable force7. He appeared older than his stated age. The center of the lens in each eye was significantly grayed8, obscuring observation of the optic discs. His blood pressure, respiration, and temperature were all within the normal ranges. His chest was clear to auscultation. The abdomen was soft with no tenderness. A reducible mass was present in the inguinal region9 on the right. The cataracts, myotonia, and proximal muscle weakness were of long duration.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status. He was awake and oriented to time and place. Normal mental status was found on all tests except for a short-term visual memory deficit10 discovered during a neuropsychological examination at a later date; there was no significant amnesia or aphasia11.

Cranial Nerves. Visual fields were full to confrontation12 (however, visual acuity was poor), a full range of eye movements was possible, and no nystagmus was present. Facial expression was full and smiling was symmetric. His hearing was normal in both ears. Jaw-jerk and corneal reflexes were physiologic13. The palate was elevated on the midline and tongue protruded midline. Shoulder shrug was bilaterally symmetric. Swallowing and voice were normal.

Motor Systems. Extended periods of tonic muscle contractions followed some of his movements in all extremities; these lessened with repetitive motion14. Muscle strength was diminished, with considerable wasting present in the proximal muscles of the shoulders and pelvis. His tendon reflexes were diminished but symmetric15 in all extremities, and plantar reflexes were flexor16. His left hand exhibited movements of an athetoid character, but only when he closed his eyes17.

Sensation. He had loss of sensation on the left side of his torso and face, and left extremities for pinprick, light touch, proprioception, vibration, two-point discrimination, graphesthesia, and stereognosis18. There was an abrupt vertical boundary to the sensory loss along the midline of the torso19. No hyperesthesia or dysesthesia20 was noted.

Follow-up

Examination five months following this incident found the dystonic character of his movements unchanged21, and his strength remained diminished. The athetotic movements of the left hand still occurred when he closed his eyes, however the sensory losses on the left side of his body were no longer detectable22.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

From findings to localization

Built from the case findings plus the chapter's thalamic vascular-territory material (no small-group transcript was provided for this case).

01 · Localization

This is a suprasegmental, sensory-only syndrome localizing to the thalamus — specifically the ventroposterior nuclear complex (VPL for the body, VPM for the face), which relays pain, temperature, touch, proprioception, and vibration en route to the postcentral gyrus. Complete face + trunk + limb loss, a sharp midline boundary, an otherwise intact motor/cranial nerve/cognitive exam, and pseudoathetosis together form the textbook picture of Fisher's pure sensory stroke — distinguishing it from a cortical, brainstem, or peripheral lesion.

02 · Distribution

Focal — a single, discrete vascular territory (the posterolateral thalamus) rather than multifocal or diffuse disease. Every finding is unilateral and confined to one functional structure; there's no evidence of bihemispheric, multifocal, or systemic involvement.

03 · Time Course

Acute — numbness began abruptly during a meal, the classic vascular signature, rather than evolving over days as a mass lesion or demyelinating process would. The subsequent course was one of gradual improvement (sensory loss undetectable by 5 months), consistent with a single ischemic event rather than an expanding one.

04 · Differential Diagnosis

DiagnosisFitsArgues against
Posterolateral thalamic infarct (VPL/VPM territory)Most likely Acute onset; complete hemibody + face loss of every modality; knife-edge midline split; pseudoathetosis from proprioceptive loss; no motor, cranial nerve, or major cognitive deficit; resolved over months, typical of a small deep infarct. Nothing in the case argues against it.
Cortical (postcentral gyrus) pure sensory stroke Can also produce a contralateral pure sensory syndrome. Cortical lesions rarely split precisely at the anatomic midline, and usually spare some modalities (e.g. pain intact, discriminative touch lost) rather than losing all seven modalities equally.
Brainstem lesion (medial lemniscus / lateral spinothalamic level) Can cause hemisensory loss. A fully normal, symmetric cranial nerve exam is hard to reconcile with a brainstem lesion, where sensory and cranial nerve pathways are tightly packed together.
Peripheral nerve, plexus, or nerve root lesion Can cause focal sensory loss. Doesn't explain combined face + trunk + limb loss in one event, and peripheral/dermatomal patterns don't produce a sharp anatomic midline split.
Myotonic dystrophy — disease flare Explains the chronic motor findings (wasting, myotonia, reduced reflexes). DM1 is a myopathy — it doesn't cause acute-onset hemisensory loss or pseudoathetosis.

05 · Diagnostic Workup

1
Non-contrast CT head at presentationFast, bedside triage step to exclude hemorrhage before anything else.
2
MRI brain with DWIThe confirmatory study — expect restricted diffusion in the posterolateral thalamus (VPL/VPM). Small, deep infarcts like this are frequently CT-negative early on.
3
Vascular imaging (MRA/CTA)Look for disease along the PCA and its thalamogeniculate branches, the vessel implicated by the posterolateral territory.
4
Stroke etiology workup — echocardiogram, carotid/vertebral duplex, telemetry/EKGStandard secondary-prevention workup to identify a cardioembolic or large-vessel source.
5
Formal neuropsychological testingAlready how the visual memory deficit was uncovered in this case — useful to fully characterize posterolateral (pulvinar-adjacent) territory involvement.
6
Baseline myotonic dystrophy screening — EKG for conduction disease, pulmonary function testsNot for the stroke itself, but DM1's cardiac conduction defects and respiratory muscle weakness matter for his overall risk profile and any future anesthesia/procedures.

06 · Prognosis

Pure thalamic sensory strokes from small penetrating-artery territories, like the thalamogeniculate branches here, generally carry a favorable prognosis for the sensory deficit itself — consistent with the full resolution seen by 5 months. The main longer-term risks are central post-stroke (Dejerine-Roussy) pain, which can emerge after the numbness has already improved, and recurrent stroke, since a first thalamic infarct signals underlying cerebrovascular risk. His myotonic dystrophy will continue to progress independently of the stroke.

07 · Management

  • Acute stroke pathway — confirm on imaging, assess reperfusion-therapy eligibility based on time since onset
  • Identify and treat the underlying vascular etiology: antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy as indicated, statin, blood pressure control
  • Physical/occupational therapy focused on proprioceptive retraining and visual-compensation strategies for the pseudoathetosis
  • Monitor for central post-stroke (thalamic) pain and treat with neuropathic agents — gabapentinoids or TCAs — if it develops
  • Continue longitudinal myotonic dystrophy care as a separate chronic-disease track: cardiac conduction monitoring, respiratory function, cataract evaluation
  • Genetic counseling / family screening for myotonic dystrophy, given its autosomal dominant inheritance
Concept Deep-Dives

The neuroanatomy behind this case

Hand-built diagrams and reference tables — no lecture slides were provided for this case, so these are built to keep the format copyright-clean.

Localization
The Body–Face Sensory Relay (VPL & VPM)

VPL receives body sensation via the medial lemniscus (contralateral) and anterolateral system; VPM receives face sensation via the trigeminothalamic tracts. Both project topographically to the postcentral gyrus, building the sensory homunculus.

Tied to this patient

His sensory loss spans face, trunk, and limbs together — consistent with a lesion positioned centrally enough (or large enough) to involve both VPL and VPM at once, rather than sparing one representation the way a smaller, more restricted lesion often does.

Localization
The Posterolateral Vascular Territory

Artery: Thalamogeniculate and posterior choroidal branches of the posterior cerebral artery (PCA).

Shape: A dorsal wedge, lateral caudally and shifting medial rostrally.

Structures: Ventroposterior nuclei (VPL/VPM), portions of the ventrolateral nucleus, pulvinar, lateroposterior nucleus, and contributions to the centromedian nucleus.

Typical deficits: Hemibody sensory loss, hemiataxia, choreiform/athetoid movements, visual memory and perceptual dysfunction, and a risk of delayed central pain.

Tied to this patient

Every deficit on this list is present in this case — sensory loss, pseudoathetosis, and the visual memory deficit found on later testing — which is what makes the posterolateral territory the clean answer here.

Pattern clue
Why the Loss Splits Exactly at the Midline

A peripheral nerve, plexus, or root lesion produces patchy loss following that structure's own territory — never a perfectly straight line down the body's anatomic midline. The thalamus, by contrast, processes an entire contralateral body half through one relay structure.

Tied to this patient

The abrupt vertical boundary at his trunk midline is the single most useful bedside clue in this case — it's essentially impossible to produce with a peripheral lesion, and points straight at a central (thalamic) cause.

Terminology
Myotonic Dystrophy — Untangling the Chronic Findings
  • Cataracts — bilateral, early-onset posterior subcapsular lens changes, one of the most specific extramuscular clues
  • Myotonia — delayed muscle relaxation after contraction; improves with repetition (the "warm-up phenomenon")
  • Proximal weakness & wasting — slowly progressive since diagnosis at age 33
  • Inguinal hernia — from weakness of the abdominal wall musculature
  • Not commented on here, but worth screening: cardiac conduction disease, respiratory muscle weakness, frontal balding
Tied to this patient

Every baseline finding — weakness, wasting, myotonia, cataracts, hernia, even his diminished tendon reflexes — is fully explained by 20+ years of myotonic dystrophy. Subtracting that chronic "noise" is what lets the new, purely sensory thalamic problem stand out clearly.
