Acute Right-Sided Weakness & Aphasia

73-year-old, right-handed male — sudden right-sided weakness and loss of speech
Localization Exam finding Terminology Pattern recognition Workup / trajectory
Hover a highlight for a quick note · tap/click one to open the full explanation

Chief Complaint

A 73-year-old, right-handed male1 with rapid onset of right-sided weakness and aphasia.

History of Chief Complaint

This 73-year-old, right-handed male was in excellent health until 7 o'clock on the evening of his admission, when he suddenly dropped his pipe out of his right hand2 while sitting on the back porch of his daughter's house. Although he did not experience a syncopal episode3, his family reported that he was obtunded for a few minutes4. Since this episode, he has been unable to speak5. He was brought directly to the emergency room.

Medical History

His medical history was unremarkable. There was no history of peptic ulcer disease or of any previous myocardial infarction or rheumatic fever.

Social History

At the time of admission, the patient had been retired for 4 years from his position as an executive with a large firm in the southern portion of the country. He had been active in recreation for the past 10 years. He was in Maine visiting his daughter's family as part of an extended vacation touring the country in a recreation vehicle.

Medications

He was not on any prescription medications and the family denied that he had used any non-prescription medications outside of an occasional NSAID for periodic musculoskeletal pains.

General Physical Examination

The patient was an elderly male who appeared younger than his stated age. He was alert, cooperative, and followed three-step commands. His neck was supple with bilateral high-pitched carotid bruits6. No hemorrhages were present in the conjunctiva. No nail bed hemorrhages were present. Heart sounds were regular with no murmurs, rubs, or gallops. No easy bruising, bleeding, hematochezia, hemoptysis, or hematuria were noticeable. He denied any stomach pain. Skull and spine were atraumatic. No cranial or orbital bruits were heard. Also, no history of migraine or other previous neurologic illnesses was given.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

The patient was awake and oriented to person, place and time. He had a markedly decreased output of speech and answered only in monosyllables of yes or no7. If asked for names, he pointed to the object or person, rather than responding with the word8. By using yes or no responses and by following commands, he comprehended spoken and written language9. He was incapable of writing his name or the days of the week, but could point to the correct day from a list10. He quickly recognized family members and attending hospital staff as they entered the room. He could follow three-step commands accurately with his left arm and hand11.

Cranial Nerves

The optic disks were flat; visual fields were intact12. No hemorrhages or other embolic phenomena were present. He had a full range of extraocular motion. His pupils had a range of motion of 2.5mm to 1.5mm to both direct and consensual light reflexes. Both eyes could close tightly. Wrinkle lines were symmetric across the forehead, and eyebrows elevated symmetrically13. The lower right quadrant of his face showed some paresis13 when he was asked to grimace or smile. Corneal, jaw-jerk, and gag reflexes were intact. His uvula elevated symmetrically, his tongue protruded along the midline, and his shoulder shrug was symmetric.

Motor Systems

Motor examination revealed a right upper limb monoparesis with a right pronator drift14. Motor power was approximately 4/5 in the right upper extremity. Motor power in the right leg was 5/515. Deep tendon reflexes in his right upper extremity were 4/4 at the elbow and wrist, 3/4 at the knee, and 2/4 at the ankle. His right toe was up-going16.

Sensation

Sensation to touch, vibration, proprioception, and pain were decreased on the right arm and thigh17 but were intact on the left side of his body. This portion of the examination was somewhat equivocal because of the patient's poor communication skills.

Follow-Up

Examination at 3 months after discharge found his gross strength on the right much improved at 5/5 in both upper and lower extremities18. Deep tendon reflexes were 3/4 at the elbow and wrist, 2/4 at the knee and 2/4 at the ankle. He had regained normal sensation through his right side. His speech consisted of numerous words but was not flowing. Many words were broken in sound and he occasionally perseverated on sounds such as no-no-no or ta-ta-ta when he was unable to produce the correct word19.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough
From bedside findings to diagnosis
Localization

Left hemisphere, lateral cortex

Right-handedness predicts left-hemisphere language dominance, and this patient has aphasia — so the lesion is on the left. The combination of nonfluent speech, preserved comprehension, and a right face-and-arm-predominant weakness/sensory loss with clear upper motor neuron signs (hyperreflexia, Babinski, pronator drift) localizes the lesion to the left frontoparietal cortex along the lateral (Sylvian) convexity — the precentral/postcentral face-arm homunculus plus Broca's area — rather than to a subcortical, brainstem, or spinal level (no crossed cranial nerve findings, no bilateral or segmental signs).

Distribution

Focal

Every abnormal finding — the facial droop, arm weakness, arm/thigh sensory loss, and nonfluent aphasia — maps onto one contiguous region of cortex rather than several scattered areas. This is a single-territory (focal), not multifocal or diffuse, process.

Time course

Acute, then gradually improving

Onset was sudden and witnessed (a specific moment — dropping the pipe at 7 pm), followed by brief obtundation and then a persistent, maximal-at-onset deficit. Over the following weeks to months, strength and reflexes trended back toward normal. This "sudden onset, then recovery" shape is the signature of an acute vascular insult, not a progressive (tumor) or fluctuating (demyelinating, seizure) course.

Diagnostic workup

What to order, and why

1
Non-contrast head CT
First priority — excludes hemorrhage before any reperfusion therapy is considered; an acute ischemic infarct may still look normal in the first hours.
2
MRI brain with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)
Once hemorrhage is excluded, confirms the acute infarct and delineates the left MCA (upper division) territory.
3
Carotid duplex ultrasound / CTA of the neck vessels
Directly evaluates the bilateral bruits heard on exam for stenotic, embologenic plaque.
4
ECG and echocardiogram
Screens for atrial fibrillation or another cardioembolic source competing with the carotid lesion.
5
Basic labs — CBC, coagulation panel, fasting lipids, glucose
Assesses additional vascular risk factors and excludes a coagulopathy or hypoglycemia mimicking stroke.
6
Formal speech-language pathology evaluation
Characterizes the aphasia subtype precisely and sets a baseline for rehabilitation planning.
Prognosis

Motor recovers faster than language

By 3 months, right-sided strength had normalized and reflexes were settling toward baseline — consistent with the typical window in which most post-stroke motor recovery occurs. His expressive aphasia, however, remained abnormal, with broken, non-flowing speech and phonemic perseveration. Language recovery after a Broca's-type infarct is generally slower and more often incomplete than motor recovery, even when the causative lesion is relatively small.

Management

Acute care, then secondary prevention and rehab

  • Assess eligibility for acute reperfusion therapy (IV thrombolysis / thrombectomy) once hemorrhage is excluded and the time-of-onset window is established
  • Antiplatelet therapy (e.g., aspirin) if not a reperfusion candidate, plus a statin
  • Carotid endarterectomy or stenting if duplex/CTA confirms significant carotid stenosis
  • Anticoagulation instead of antiplatelet therapy if a cardioembolic source (e.g., atrial fibrillation) is found
  • Risk-factor control: blood pressure, glucose, lipids
  • Physical and occupational therapy for the residual arm weakness
  • Speech-language therapy for the persistent nonfluent aphasia
Concept Deep-Dive
The neuroanatomy behind the exam
Language Cortex

Broca's area and the expressive language network

Broca's area (posterior inferior frontal gyrus, areas 44/45) holds the motor programs for speech; Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal gyrus) supports comprehension. A left anterior (Broca's) lesion produces exactly this patient's pattern — sparse, effortful, monosyllabic output with preserved comprehension of spoken and written language.

This patient: nonfluent output, intact comprehension (follows commands, points to correct answers), agraphia — the textbook signature of Broca's (motor/expressive) aphasia rather than a Wernicke's (fluent, comprehension-impaired) picture.
Motor/Sensory Homunculus

Why the leg was spared

Face and arm sit on the lateral convexity, fed by the MCA; the leg's representation rolls over onto the medial surface, fed by the ACA. A lateral (MCA) lesion therefore weakens the face and arm while sparing the leg — exactly this patient's 4/5 arm vs. 5/5 leg split.

This patient: right face and arm weak, right leg normal strength — a lateral-surface, MCA-territory pattern.
Facial Nerve Pathway

Central vs. peripheral facial palsy

The upper-face motor neurons receive input from both hemispheres; the lower-face neurons receive it only from the contralateral cortex. A cortical (UMN) lesion therefore weakens only the opposite lower face and spares the forehead — unlike a peripheral (CN VII nucleus/nerve, LMN) lesion, which weakens the entire same-sided face, top to bottom.

This patient: symmetric forehead wrinkling and eyebrow elevation, weak lower-right face — forehead-sparing confirms a central (left cortical), not peripheral, facial lesion.
Vascular Territory

MCA vs. ACA vs. PCA

ACA: medial surface — leg, foot, urinary control, medial frontal behavior. MCA: lateral convexity — face, arm, primary language cortex. PCA: occipital/medial temporal — vision, memory structures. This patient's face+arm weakness, sensory loss, and language deficit — with normal vision and a normal leg — sit squarely in MCA territory.

This patient: intact visual fields (against PCA) and a normal leg (against ACA) — findings converge on the left MCA.

Upper Motor Neuron Signs

Reading the exam for UMN vs. LMN

This patient's exam — increased right-sided reflexes (4/4, 3/4), an up-going toe, and a pronator drift, with no atrophy or fasciculations — is a pure UMN pattern, consistent with a corticospinal-tract lesion at the cortical level rather than damage to a peripheral nerve, root, or anterior horn cell.
