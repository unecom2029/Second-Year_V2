Right Temporal Lobe Epilepsy, Post-Traumatic Mesial Temporal Focus

Chief Complaint

A 38-year-old trauma victim now experiencing loss of consciousness.

History of Chief Complaint

A 38-year-old, right-handed, male received a blow to the right side of his skull with the butt of a rifle1 6 years ago. At the time of the accident he suffered no syncope or seizures2. A subdural hematoma was removed from the right side3 of the patient. Nine months subsequent to the trauma, he developed seizures4. Recently, because of the increased severity of the seizures5, his wife brought him to the emergency room at the hospital for examination. They are now seeking consultation for surgery6.

Past Medical History

At the time of the examination he was a private with 10 years of service in the army. He had experienced head trauma to the head while on practice maneuvers in the Middle East. He had had several other accidents involving automobiles and at least one involving a motorcycle. He had no history of syncope or seizures prior to the traumatic event with the rifle7.

Subsequent to being struck on the head with a rifle butt, he developed seizures characterized by onset of dizziness8 and followed by an olfactory sensation of foul odor, such as that of vomitus9. He then would assume a blank stare10 and often demonstrate several automatisms, including lip-smacking and rubbing his hands on his shirt11. During these periods, he would not respond to questions or statements12. He has experienced periods characterized by a sense of light-headedness and mingled thoughts13. He occasionally hears voices that give him commands14, and he has had periods of automatisms during which he lacked conscious awareness of his acts15.

General Physical Examination

At the time of examination, he was an alert, well-hydrated, well-nourished male, oriented for time and place, and appeared his stated age. His body had numerous scars, presumably from previous traumatic injuries. Optic discs were sharp, with no hemorrhagic spots. Visual fields were full to confrontation. Chest was clear to auscultation and percussion. Abdomen was soft, with no masses or tenderness. Blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respirations were normal.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status

The patient was alert and oriented to time and place. Knowledge and memory seemed appropriate for his age and occupation. Speech, writing, and reading were intact. He complained of periods when he could not recall events; these times coincided with the seizure events16 as described by his wife.

Cranial Nerves

His visual fields were full to confrontation17, and a full range of eye movements was present. His hearing was normal in both ears. His face had a full range of expression; corneal and jaw-jerk reflexes were intact. His palate and uvula elevated midline; the tongue protruded midline. His shoulders shrugged bilaterally.

Motor Systems

His limb muscles were normotonic with bilaterally symmetric reflexes in all extremities. No weakness, tremor, or dysmetria were present18. He exhibited no past pointing or pronator drift.

Sensation

Pinprick, temperature, vibratory, and proprioceptive senses were intact throughout the body and face.

Tests

Interictal EEG studies were normal19. Those taken during seizure revealed a predominant right temporal focal involvement20.

  3. Are there any changes in cranial nerve function, if so are they signs of suprasegmental or segmental level defects?
  4. Are there any changes in motor function such as reflexes, muscle tone, movement, or coordination, if so are they signs of suprasegmental or segmental level defects?
  5. Are there any changes in sensory function, if so, what levels of the body have experienced this change?
  6. What is the clinical temporal profile of this patient's neurologic problem — acute or chronic, progressive or stable?
  7. Based on the presenting signs and symptoms, do you think the distribution of the neurologic pathology is focal, multifocal, or diffuse?
  8. Based on the answers to these questions, develop a differential diagnosis of the patient's neurological problem.
  9. If the origin of the pathology is vascular, what arterial supply is most likely involved with the lesion in this patient?
Below the Case

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

1 · Localization

Right mesial temporal lobe

The aura pattern (cacosmia, dreamy "mingled thoughts"), oral/manual automatisms, and impaired awareness all point to the mesial temporal structures — uncus, amygdala, hippocampus — on the right. This is a suprasegmental (cortical/limbic) disturbance: his cranial nerve, motor, and sensory exams are entirely normal between seizures, so there's no segmental (peripheral nerve or brainstem nucleus) lesion to explain.

2 · Distribution

Focal, unilateral (right)

Everything localizes to one side: the trauma and subdural hematoma were right-sided, and the ictal EEG focus is right temporal. There's no evidence of a multifocal or diffuse process — no bilateral findings, no generalized cognitive decline, no evidence pointing to structures beyond the right temporal lobe.

3 · Time Course

Chronic, progressive

The injury occurred 6 years ago; seizures began after a 9-month latency — the classic window in which a traumatic cortical scar matures into an epileptogenic focus (late post-traumatic epilepsy). The recent increase in seizure severity marks an evolving, not static, process, which is part of why a surgical work-up is now being pursued.

4 · Differential Diagnosis
DiagnosisFitsArgues Against
Post-traumatic right temporal lobe epilepsy (mesial temporal gliotic focus)
Most Likely
 Right-sided trauma + evacuated right subdural hematoma; classic ~9-month latency to late post-traumatic epilepsy; textbook temporal lobe semiology (cacosmia, dreamy state, oral/manual automatisms); ictal EEG confirms a right temporal focus. None of the findings argue against this — everything converges here.
Mesial temporal sclerosis, unrelated to the rifle injury Can produce an identical right temporal seizure semiology and EEG focus. No history of febrile seizures or childhood insult; the clear temporal link to a known structural injury is far more parsimonious.
Right temporal mass lesion (e.g., low-grade glioma, meningioma) Can present with progressive complex partial seizures and an olfactory aura. No other progressive focal deficits, no papilledema, normal interictal exam — but still needs MRI to formally exclude.
Primary psychotic disorder Command auditory hallucinations. Hallucinations occur only clustered within brief, stereotyped seizure episodes with automatisms and amnesia — mental status is normal between episodes, unlike a standalone psychotic illness.
Recurrent transient ischemic attacks Recurrent, stereotyped spells. Years of stereotyped episodes with automatisms plus an epileptiform right temporal ictal EEG are not a TIA pattern; TIAs don't produce lip-smacking or olfactory hallucinations.
5 · Diagnostic Workup
  1. Continued video-EEG monitoring
    Capture additional seizures to confirm the right temporal focus is consistent across events before any surgical planning proceeds.
  2. Brain MRI, epilepsy protocol
    Look for a structural correlate at the right mesial temporal lobe — encephalomalacia/gliosis from the old subdural hematoma, mesial temporal sclerosis, or an occult mass — and formally exclude a tumor.
  3. Neuropsychological testing
    Establish a baseline, particularly for verbal vs. nonverbal memory — with a right-sided (non-dominant, given right-handedness) focus, nonverbal/visuospatial memory is the more relevant one to protect.
  4. Wada test or fMRI language/memory mapping
    If resection is being considered, establish hemispheric dominance for language and memory to predict post-operative risk.
  5. Confirm drug-resistance
    Document an adequate trial of at least two appropriately dosed antiepileptic drugs — the threshold that justifies proceeding to surgical evaluation.
6 · Prognosis

A well-lateralized, unilateral right mesial temporal focus with no evidence of bilateral or diffuse disease makes this patient a good candidate for epilepsy surgery evaluation. If imaging confirms a discrete structural focus, resection (anterior temporal lobectomy or selective amygdalohippocampectomy) carries a meaningfully high chance of seizure freedom or major reduction, with comparatively modest memory risk given the non-dominant (right) side involved. Without intervention, the natural history of an unaddressed epileptogenic focus is for seizures to become both longer and more frequent, carrying ongoing risk of injury or impairment during unaware automatisms.

7 · Management
  • Seizure safety precautions and family/caregiver education (recognizing the aura, protecting during automatisms, driving restrictions per local law)
  • Optimize and document an adequate trial of antiepileptic drug therapy
  • Video-EEG monitoring to further localize the focus
  • MRI brain, epilepsy protocol, to identify a structural correlate
  • Neuropsychological and Wada/fMRI assessment if surgery is being pursued
  • Comprehensive epilepsy surgery evaluation (already underway) — consider anterior temporal lobectomy or amygdalohippocampectomy if a confirmed, drug-resistant unilateral focus
  • Vagal nerve stimulation or other neuromodulation as an alternative if resection isn't pursued or isn't feasible
Underlying Neuroanatomy

Concept Deep-Dive

Localization

The Uncus & Primary Olfactory Cortex

A thin veneer of piriform (primary olfactory) cortex covers the amygdala at the uncus, receiving axons directly from the olfactory bulb. This is the anatomic basis for this patient's cacosmia — his seizure focus sits right at this structure.

Pattern

Simple → Complex Partial Seizure Spread

The discharge starts focally (aura), spreads to impair consciousness (complex partial), recruits motor automatisms, and leaves the patient amnestic for the whole episode — matching his wife's description almost step for step.

Localization

The Papez Circuit

Hippocampus → fornix → mammillary body → anterior thalamus → cingulate → parahippocampal/entorhinal cortex → back to hippocampus. This loop underlies emotional tagging and memory consolidation — the same circuitry his right temporal focus is discharging into during each seizure, which is why he gets dreamy, dysmnesic auras rather than a purely sensory one.

Trajectory

Post-Traumatic Epilepsy Timeline

His seizures began 9 months after the injury — squarely in the "late" post-traumatic epilepsy window, when a maturing gliotic scar (not the acute injury itself) becomes the epileptogenic driver. As that scar matures — commonly cited at around three months, though the process can continue well beyond that — it releases pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, TNF-α, IL-6) that drive further gliosis and lower the local seizure threshold, which is the proposed mechanism behind this kind of delayed onset. That's also why his interictal EEG can be normal: the scar is a stable, low-level irritant most of the time.

Workup

Ictal vs. Interictal EEG

Deep mesial temporal discharges often don't reach scalp electrodes strongly enough to show up between seizures — exactly why this patient's interictal EEG was normal, while capturing an actual seizure revealed the right temporal focus. Ictal recording, not interictal, is what localizes a case like this.

Term

Automatisms & Lateralizing Value

SignLocalizing Implication
Oral automatisms (lip-smacking, chewing)Typically ipsilateral to the seizure focus

Dystonic posturing of a limbTypically contralateral to the seizure focus
Head/eye versionOften contralateral (version away from the focus)

Automatisms are one of the semiological features epileptologists use to help lateralize a focus before it's even confirmed on EEG. In this patient, that lateralizing framework independently supports the same right-sided focus that his ictal EEG ultimately confirmed.

Term

Focal vs. Generalized Seizures

Seizures are classified by how much cortex is involved: focal activity stays confined to one hemisphere, while generalized activity is diffuse and involves both — which, by definition, is why a generalized seizure impairs consciousness. A focal seizure can still cause loss of consciousness if it spreads to the other hemisphere across the corpus callosum (secondary generalization). This patient's seizures are already complex partial (impaired awareness within a focal, right-sided event); the concern behind his worsening severity is exactly this kind of progression toward bilateral spread.

Term

Seizure vs. Epilepsy

FeatureSeizureEpilepsy
DefinitionA single, temporary episode of abnormal electrical activityA chronic condition of recurrent, unprovoked seizures
CauseMay be provoked (fever, alcohol withdrawal, hypoglycemia) or unprovokedBy definition recurrent and unprovoked

Febrile seizures (in children, usually self-limited with a good prognosis) and alcohol-withdrawal seizures (from the sudden loss of alcohol's inhibitory tone, a classic board favorite) are the two provoked triggers most worth knowing. Neither applies to this patient — his seizures are recurrent and unprovoked, arising from a fixed structural focus, which is exactly what makes this epilepsy rather than an isolated symptomatic seizure.

Pattern

Why a Seizure Stops: Metabolic Exhaustion

A seizure is metabolically expensive: sustained neuronal firing rapidly burns through local glucose and ATP, and termination is largely driven by this energy debt rather than an active braking mechanism. That's why patients are so drained afterward — the post-ictal exhaustion is real energy depletion in the involved cortex, layered on top of the amnesia already caused by the disrupted Papez circuit.
