Case Study 8-4

A 78-year-old woman with confusion, an amnestic-confabulatory syndrome, and a sleep-wake system that collapses from insomnia to fatal coma over five weeks — the natural history of bilateral paramedian thalamic infarction.

Chief Complaint

A 78-year-old woman presents with confusion, memory dysfunction, and sleep disturbances1.

History of Chief Complaint

This 78-year-old woman was brought to the emergency room from a local nursing home after she became very agitated and disoriented, alarming the other residents2.

Medical History

At the time of admission she had been retired for 13 years from her position as an elementary schoolteacher. She had been married and her husband was deceased. She had no children. Five years previously she had moved from her house to a nursing home. Two years previously she had experienced a period of right facial weakness with language dysfunction, which had resolved over a 2-week period3. Until the day of admittance she had been a pleasant person, sociable with other residents of the nursing home, and with good memory4.

General Physical Examination

She was in an agitated state and uncooperative, making a detailed examination difficult5. She was well nourished and well hydrated6 and appeared her stated age. She had increased pulse, respiration, and blood pressure (190/100 mmHg)7. She appeared flushed and her skin was moist8.

Neurologic Examination

Mental Status. She was disoriented with respect to time and place9. She insisted she was going shopping and that the driver had let her out at the hospital by mistake10. She was aggravated and abusive with the attending personnel. She was a poor historian. At the time of admission she could not be tested for memory, since she refused to answer most questions11.

Cranial Nerves. Visual fields appeared full to confrontation, a full range of eye movements was possible, and no nystagmus was present13. Facial expression was full and hearing was normal. Jaw-jerk reflex was normal and corneal reflex was present12. Palate was elevated on the midline and tongue protruded midline. Shoulder shrug was bilaterally symmetric. Swallowing and voice were normal.

Motor Systems. Movements were normal; strength and deep tendon reflexes were physiologic throughout the patient's body14.

Sensation. Response to pinprick was normal; no detectable loss of proprioception was found15. Testing was complicated by her uncooperative nature16.

Follow-up

The patient experienced insomnia for 3 days while under observation17 in the hospital. On discharge she was calm but had marked memory dysfunction and continued to confabulate explanations to cover the memory loss18. One month after returning to the nursing home she developed marked hypersomnia and was difficult to rouse19. She returned to the hospital where she died one week later20.

Clinical Reasoning Walkthrough

From findings to localization

Built from the case findings plus the chapter's thalamic vascular-territory material (no small-group transcript was provided for this case).

01 · Localization

This is a diencephalic (thalamic), likely bilateral syndrome localizing to the paramedian / midline territory — the dorsomedial nucleus, mammillothalamic tract, and posterior internal medullary lamina — rather than the sensory relay (VPL/VPM) or motor pathways involved in Case 8-3. Disorientation, an amnestic-confabulatory syndrome, agitation, autonomic surge, and a sleep-wake system that collapses from insomnia into unrousable hypersomnolence are the signature deficits of this territory, while intact sensation and motor function argue against the posterolateral or lateral/internal-capsule territories.

02 · Distribution

Best described as focal but bilateral — restricted to one functional system (the paramedian thalamus) but very likely affecting both sides at once. Hypersomnolence is specifically flagged in the literature as a feature of bilateral paramedian lesions, and her prior, anatomically distinct anterolateral event two years ago shows this is not a single, isolated occurrence.

03 · Time Course

Acute onset, progressive course — unlike Case 8-3, this does not stabilize and improve. It moves through recognizable stages: agitation/confusion → insomnia (3 days) → an established amnestic-confabulatory state at discharge → hypersomnolence one month later → unrousable coma and death. That relentless progression, rather than the usual stroke pattern of stabilizing then improving, is itself a clue worth flagging.

04 · Differential Diagnosis

DiagnosisFitsArgues against
Bilateral paramedian thalamic infarct (artery of Percheron)Most likely Confusion/agitation, amnestic-confabulatory syndrome, insomnia → hypersomnolence progression (a bilateral-lesion sign), autonomic surge, sparing of sensory and motor pathways, and a known second cerebrovascular territory event two years prior. Formal vertical gaze testing — a classic paramedian sign — isn't specifically documented, likely due to her agitation.
Deep cerebral venous thrombosis (bilateral thalamic venous infarction) Can produce an essentially identical bilateral thalamic clinical picture. Typically more subacute, with headache and papilledema, none of which are documented (though not fully excluded by a limited exam).
Wernicke encephalopathy (thiamine deficiency) Confusion can occur in an elderly, institutionalized patient at nutritional risk. Documented as well nourished and well hydrated; no nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia, or ataxia described.
Rapidly progressive dementia (e.g. Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease) Progressive cognitive decline ending in death. Onset is far too abrupt on top of a fully intact baseline; CJD evolves over a more insidious course and typically features myoclonus.
Metabolic or infectious delirium (e.g. sepsis, electrolyte disturbance) Can cause acute agitation and confusion in an elderly patient. Doesn't explain the specific evolution into an amnestic-confabulatory syndrome and then hypersomnolence-to-coma; would need labs to formally exclude.

3
MR venography (MRV)To exclude deep cerebral venous thrombosis, the key mimic of bilateral thalamic infarction that changes management entirely.
4
Metabolic and infectious workup — electrolytes, glucose, ammonia, TSH, B12, blood cultures, urinalysisTo identify or exclude a contributing or mimicking delirium.
5
Thiamine level, with empiric thiamine replacementStandard practice in any confused elderly patient at nutritional risk, while Wernicke's is formally excluded.
6
Vascular risk-factor workup — echocardiogram, carotid/vertebral duplex, telemetry/EKGNow doubly important given this appears to be her second distinct cerebrovascular event.

06 · Prognosis

Unlike Case 8-3's favorable course, bilateral paramedian thalamic infarction carries a genuinely guarded prognosis, especially once hypersomnolence and impaired arousal appear. The progression documented here — insomnia, then an amnestic-confabulatory state, then hypersomnolence, then unrousable coma and death — matches the natural history described for bilateral (as opposed to unilateral) paramedian involvement.

07 · Management

  • Acute stroke pathway — urgent CT/MRI, though by the time bilateral paramedian infarcts are recognized, many are outside a reperfusion window
  • MR venography to exclude deep cerebral venous thrombosis, since anticoagulation rather than standard secondary prevention would be indicated if venous
  • Secondary stroke prevention — antiplatelet therapy, statin, blood pressure management — especially given a second, distinct vascular territory event
  • Empiric thiamine while nutritional workup is pending
  • Supportive care for agitation and sleep-wake disruption; caution with sedating agents given her risk of worsening arousal
  • Early goals-of-care discussion, given the declining level of consciousness and guarded trajectory
  • Ongoing neurologic monitoring for further decline or recurrent stroke, given her established cerebrovascular disease
Concept Deep-Dives

The neuroanatomy behind this case

Hand-built diagrams and reference tables — no lecture slides were provided for this case, so these are built to keep the format copyright-clean.

Localization
The Paramedian Territory & the Artery of Percheron

In most people, separate small paramedian branches supply each side of the thalamus. In a common anatomic variant, both sides share one perforating trunk off the basilar tip or proximal PCA — the artery of Percheron. A single occlusion there produces bilateral paramedian infarction from one event.

Tied to this patient

Her hypersomnolence — explicitly a bilateral-lesion sign in this territory — is hard to explain with a single unilateral infarct. A shared-trunk anatomic variant is exactly the kind of vascular lesion that produces bilateral findings from what is still, technically, one vessel going down.

Pattern clue
Diencephalic Amnesia & the Papez Circuit

The Papez circuit — hippocampus → fornix → mammillary body → mammillothalamic tract → dorsomedial/anterior thalamic nuclei → cingulate cortex → back to hippocampus — is the anatomic substrate for memory consolidation. The dorsomedial nucleus and mammillothalamic tract both sit within the paramedian territory.

Tied to this patient

Damage at the thalamic node of this circuit is exactly what produces her memory dysfunction and confabulation — the same mechanism underlying the amnesia of Korsakoff's psychosis, just from a vascular rather than nutritional cause.

Pattern clue
The Sleep-Wake Axis: Insomnia to Coma

Ascending arousal signals from the brainstem reticular formation relay through the intralaminar and paramedian thalamic nuclei on their way to diffusely activate the cortex. A graded lesion here produces a graded arousal deficit — not an all-or-nothing switch.

Tied to this patient

Her course traces that gradient exactly: early disruption first shows up as insomnia, then progresses to profound hypersomnolence, and finally to unrousable coma as the relay fails more completely.
