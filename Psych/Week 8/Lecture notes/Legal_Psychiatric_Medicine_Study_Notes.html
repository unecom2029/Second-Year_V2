Legal Psychiatric Medicine

Ryan Smith, DO, PhD — "Legal Psychiatric Medicine 2026"
TopicsConfidentiality, HIPAA, Tarasoff, capacity/competence, malpractice, ethics & forensics terms
Core TensionPatient confidentiality/autonomy vs. duty to protect identifiable third parties and society
Boards FactAlmost entirely vignette-based — "which legal/ethical principle applies here?"
High-YieldTarasoff I/II · malpractice's 4 D's · M'Naughton Rule · confidentiality exceptions
Yield check: Confidentiality is the default — it's what makes patients willing to disclose in the first place. Nearly everything in this lecture is really a catalog of the narrow, specific situations where the law or ethics code carves out an exception to that default (Tarasoff, mandatory reporting, emergencies, incompetence, supervision). On boards, expect a clinical vignette followed by "what should the physician do?" or "which principle is this?" — so the goal below is being able to instantly recognize which exception or doctrine a given scenario is testing, not memorizing statutes.
Section 01

Confidentiality: The Default Rule ⭐ HY

Confidentiality

DefinitionThe clinician's obligation not to release information obtained during treatment to third parties.
Ethical basisThe benefit of confidentiality outweighs the harm — patients would not seek treatment at all without the assurance that what they disclose stays protected. Confidentiality is considered implicit in the treatment setting; it doesn't need to be separately promised each visit.
Release ruleConfidential information may only be released with the patient's authorization or under proper legal compulsion (e.g., a valid court order).
👪
Collateral information from family: obtaining information from a patient's family requires the patient's consent — this is not a standard exception to confidentiality. The same is true when disclosing to, or obtaining information from, an attorney or a previous treater: patient permission is required either way.
🔄
The asymmetry rule — a classic exam trap: you (the clinician) cannot take information about the patient from an outside source without the patient's consent. But outside people are free to give you information about the patient without the patient's consent — nothing stops a worried spouse from calling and telling you something. You just can't confirm or discuss anything back without authorization. In practice: if a mother calls asking about "Johnny," the correct line is "I can neither confirm nor deny that Johnny is my patient" — you can let her keep talking, you just can't hand anything back.
🔪
The "shocker" every class gets wrong: if a patient discloses that they already murdered someone in the past, you cannot tell anyone — not unless there is a separately documented, imminent risk that they will harm someone else in the future. A past act, on its own, is not an exception to confidentiality, no matter how severe. You may be able to justify telling someone ethically, but you cannot do it legally without that future-risk piece — and legal is what protects your license.
Section 02

Exceptions to Confidentiality ⭐ HY

Six standard, boards-recognized situations where breaking confidentiality is permitted or required. Each is a "spot the exception" vignette waiting to happen.

1. Tarasoff Doctrine

TriggerProtecting an identifiable third party from a dangerous patient. Protecting the potential victim takes precedence over maintaining confidentiality.
More detailFull breakdown in Section 04 — this is the highest-yield exception by far.

2. Involuntary Hospitalization

TriggerThe doctor must break confidentiality to testify in court or to record the legal documentation explaining why involuntary hospitalization is necessary.

3. Supervision

TriggerA trainee disclosing patient information to a supervising attending. Allowed specifically for teaching and training purposes.

4. Emergency

TriggerPhysicians may break confidentiality in an emergency to obtain the information needed to treat the patient safely.

5. Incompetence

TriggerIf a patient is declared incompetent, the physician may break confidentiality to discuss the case with the court-appointed guardian.

6. Mandatory Reporting

TriggerState mandatory reporting laws require physicians to report specific circumstances to the appropriate authority — e.g., child abuse, elder abuse, an impaired physician, or certain communicable diseases.
Also coversMalicious wounding (a gunshot or stab wound) → inform law enforcement. Danger to the public (e.g., driving drunk, or a seizure disorder that impairs safe driving) → inform law enforcement and/or the DMV/RMV.
ProtectionMany states grant immunity from liability for an appropriate, good-faith breach of confidentiality made under a mandatory reporting statute.
⚖️
Physician-patient privilege: the right (and obligation) of the physician to refuse to testify in a trial or other legal proceeding about anything a patient told them, on the basis that the communication is confidential. This obligation to protect confidentiality continues after the death of the patient — it doesn't expire.
🩸
Confidentiality and HIV — "Common test question!" If a patient refuses to change behavior that places identifiable others at risk of HIV infection, it is ethically permissible for the psychiatrist to break confidentiality — either notifying the at-risk individual directly or arranging for public health authorities to do so. The nuance to remember: ethically permissible does not automatically mean legally permissible in every jurisdiction, and a lawyer could still litigate the breach. Good practice is to get legal input before breaking confidentiality in this scenario, not after.
Section 03

HIPAA — Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (1996)

What HIPAA Requires of Physicians

NoticePhysicians must give patients written notice of their privacy rights and of how their information is used, kept, and disclosed.
Records accessPatients have the right to copies of their medical records within a defined time period — this right does NOT extend to psychotherapy notes.
AuthorizationAuthorization is required to release health information, except for treatment, payment, or health care operations ("TPO") — these are considered routine uses that don't require separate authorization.
Patient rightsPatients may request an alternate means of communication (a different phone number, etc.) and may report privacy violations directly to HHS (the Department of Health and Human Services).
👨‍👩‍👧
Disclosing to friends/family under HIPAA is allowed only when: the patient gives explicit permission, or the patient does not object when given a reasonable opportunity to do so. Absent either, the better move is to support and encourage the patient to disclose themselves.
📓
Why psychotherapy notes are special: to qualify for this exclusion, they have to be kept as a separate paper file, apart from the rest of the electronic medical record — not typed into the EMR as just another note. Kept that way, they're one of the very few things in medicine you can lawfully withhold even when served a subpoena. Also remember: the patient owns the information in the record, not the physical/original record itself — they can request a copy and can request factual corrections, but they cannot demand you hand over or remove your original.
🎙️
Why your notes have to be legible — a cautionary tale: years ago, dictation software (long before it got good) turned "take the clozapine at bedtime" into "take the penis at bedtime" in a patient's chart. She read her own notes on the portal, came back for her next visit, and deadpanned, "I did everything you said — my schizophrenia isn't better, but my husband's a lot happier." Funny because she had a sense of humor about it; it could just as easily have been a serious problem. Anything you write can be subpoenaed and read in open court — reread what you dictate.

Privacy Rule vs. Security Rule

Privacy RuleSecurity Rule
ScopeNational standards protecting medical records & other personal health information (PHI)National standards protecting electronic PHI (ePHI) specifically
Applies toAll information — paper, electronic, and spokenElectronic information only
RequiresAppropriate safeguards; sets limits/conditions on uses & disclosures without patient authorization; gives patients the right to examine, copy, and request corrections to their recordsAppropriate safeguards to ensure confidentiality, integrity, and security of ePHI
🏛️
HIPAA sets the federal floor, not the ceiling — individual states may enact stricter privacy rules if they choose.
Section 04

Tarasoff Doctrine ⭐ HY

Tarasoff I
Establishes the "duty to warn" — a psychiatrist who determines a patient poses a serious danger to an identifiable victim has a duty to warn that intended victim. In practice, "warn" means contact the police and let them handle notifying/protecting the victim — you are not expected to call the intended victim yourself.
Tarasoff II
Expands the duty into the broader "duty to protect" — the obligation isn't satisfied by a warning alone; the psychiatrist must take whatever reasonable steps are needed to protect the potential victim. This does not mean physically inserting yourself between patient and victim — it still means notifying the police and documenting that you did.
📖
The actual case: in this 1976 California Supreme Court case, the court ruled that mental health professionals have a legal duty to protect identifiable individuals who are being threatened with serious harm by a patient. The case stemmed from the death of Tatiana Tarasoff, who was killed by a man who had told his therapist he intended to harm her.

When Tarasoff Applies

TriggerA specific, identifiable victim appears to be in imminent and probable danger from the threat of a mentally ill patient.
Professional standardPsychiatry holds that it is ethical to break confidentiality when, in the clinical judgment of the treating psychiatrist, the risk of danger is significant. The psychiatrist carries the duty of determining which threats are likely to be acted upon, based on the details of the case and their clinical judgement.
Fantasies ≠ reportableA patient's fantasies alone are not reportable. This assumes the clinician's judgment is that these are not feelings the patient actually intends to act upon.
Specific vs. fantasy — the actual test"I hate all men" or "I could kill my brother, I just hate him" is not reportable — it's global or clearly rhetorical, with no real target or plan. "I'm going to kill my brother with a handgun at 4 p.m. on Friday" is reportable — a named, identifiable victim plus a credible plan and timeframe. When it's genuinely unclear which side of that line a statement falls on, get a second clinician's opinion before deciding not to report.
📋
Before breaching confidentiality — try the least restrictive option first. The psychiatrist should exhaust reasonable in-house options to stabilize the patient and protect the potential victim before resorting to a warning: change medication → involve family → increase visit frequency → hospitalize. Warning/breaching confidentiality is not automatically the first move.

Principle of the Least Restrictive Alternative ⭐ HY

The patient has the right to receive the least restrictive means of treatment that still achieves the requisite clinical effect — this principle underlies Tarasoff management, medication-over-objection (Section 06), and seclusion/restraint (Section 10) alike.

Example order for an agitated patient, offered least-restrictive-first: PO → ODT ("Zydis") → IM → physical restraints

Section 05

Capacity and Competence ⭐ HY

CapacityCompetence
What it isA clinical determination, made by a psychiatrist, of whether a patient is able to make a specific decision, at a specific point in timeA legal term referring to a minimum ability to perform a specific act — decided by courts, not physicians
Who decidesPsychiatrist / treating clinicianCourts
ScopeTask- and time-specific — can fluctuateCan arise for health care decisions, refusing medication, consenting to research, or general competence over one's affairs

The 4 Standards Used to Assess Capacity

1. Communicate

Ability to communicate a choice.

2. Understand

Ability to understand the information presented.

3. Apply

Ability to understand how the information applies to one's own circumstances.

4. Reason

Ability to reason in a logical manner — even if most people would not agree with the final decision. Capacity is about the reasoning process, not agreement with the outcome.
📝
How a real capacity note is written: it always opens with a specific time and a specific decision — e.g., "At 4:55 p.m. on January 8th, regarding capacity for decision-making regarding surgery for brain cancer..." — never "the patient has capacity" as a blanket statement. If a patient uses their capacity to refuse a treatment with a strong chance of success (e.g., a 95% remission rate), and you've confirmed they meet all four standards, they still have the right to refuse — get a second clinician's capacity evaluation in these higher-stakes/contested cases so the finding is defensible if challenged.
🧠
Alternate mnemonic — "CARD": for a patient (or their surrogate) to demonstrate capacity, they must show Communication of a decision, Appreciation of the relevant facts, Reasoning (the thought process behind the decision), and Demonstrated understanding of the information and options presented — the same 4 standards above, in a more memorable order.
🏥
No capacity and no surrogate available (e.g., a patient in acute delirium with nobody to consult): the physician should proceed with treatment based on the patient's best interest.
💬
Intellectual disability ≠ no capacity. Patients with mild intellectual disability are still capable of having decision-making capacity. Useful tools for helping them make medical decisions include involving family members and using simple language or visual aids.
📜
Capacity to make a will: psychiatrists may be asked to evaluate this specifically. The patient must know the nature and extent of their property, the fact that they are making a will, and the identities of the people named as beneficiaries.
🧩
Mental illness ≠ automatic incompetence. A person may be competent in one area (e.g., consenting to a medical procedure) but not another (e.g., managing finances) — competence is domain-specific, and general incompetence (inability to manage most/all of one's affairs) is a separate, higher legal bar that only a court can determine. Simple example: having ADHD doesn't make someone incompetent — the same logic applies to most psychiatric diagnoses in isolation.
🏛️
How this plays out at a state hospital: committed patients typically see a judge within 72 hours in a small on-site courtroom. The psychiatrist presents the case with the patient and their guardian present, and the judge — who rarely overturns the doctor's clinical read — is mainly checking that due process was followed, not re-deciding the medicine. Due process violations are a real, current legal exposure: several states have faced major lawsuits over psychiatric patients boarding in ERs for days to weeks awaiting an inpatient bed without timely judicial review — a good illustration that "the patient's rights were violated" doesn't require anyone acting in bad faith, just a system failing to move fast enough.

Advance Directives

Living Will

Specifies the patient's end-of-life wishes — often including specific directives on intubation, CPR, enteral feeding, and other life-prolonging interventions.

Healthcare Proxy

Lets the patient designate a specific individual to make healthcare decisions if the patient becomes incapacitated — always in line with the wishes stated in the living will. Only in effect once the patient is deemed to have insufficient capacity.
👪
No advance directive on file? Family members typically serve as surrogate decision-makers in this order: spouse → adult children → parents → siblings — applying the substituted judgment standard (Section 09): deciding based on what the patient would have wanted.

Competence to Stand Trial

The 2-Part Test

1. UnderstandThe defendant must have a rational, factual understanding of the charges against them.
2. AssistThe defendant must be able to consult with, and aid, their own attorney in their defense with a reasonable degree of understanding.
Landmark caseDusky v. United States. Being mentally ill doesn't by itself make someone incompetent to stand trial — some individuals can be deemed competent despite mental illness; others cannot. The McGarry Instrument (Section 09) is the clinical guide used to formally assess this.
⚖️
Two legal bases for involuntary commitment: police power (protecting citizens from each other) and parens patriae (protecting citizens who can't care for themselves) — see Section 09 for the full definition of parens patriae.
Section 06

Medication Over Objection

The Baseline Right

Key factInvoluntary hospitalization does NOT negate the presumption that a patient is competent to refuse medication — being committed and being incompetent to refuse a specific treatment are two separate questions.
Legal basis for refusalThe common law right to control what is done to one's own body, the legal requirement for informed consent, and the patient's rights to due process and privacy.
⚖️
What the doctor must prove in court to medicate over objection: that the patient lacks the capacity to make the refusal, and that the proposed treatment is in the patient's best interest (this is the definition of paternalism — see Section 09).
🚨
Emergency override: the right to refuse medication can be overridden in emergencies (e.g., acute PSE / patient safety emergency) when the patient is acutely dangerous to self or others.
🗺️
State variation: in some states, doctors must go to court for medication over objection; in others, it can be decided through administrative review instead. Know that this varies — don't assume one process nationally.
💉
Why long-acting injectables matter here: once cognition clears on a forced medication order, most patients are relieved and grateful — but once the court order lapses, some will stop the medication and relapse, restarting the whole cycle. A long-acting injectable buys weeks to months of continued benefit even if the patient starts refusing again the moment they're able to, which is why it's often the preferred formulation in this population.
Section 07

Malpractice — The "4 D's" ⭐ HY

In a malpractice suit, a patient alleges they were damaged by a physician's negligence. To win, they must prove all four elements.

🚑
Good Samaritan laws and "Duty": if you help a stranger collapse on the sidewalk, there's technically no doctor-patient relationship, so technically no duty — and technically no malpractice suit. In practice, a determined lawyer can still find an angle and sue anyway, so "no duty" isn't an absolute shield, but it's still the correct first checkpoint.
🧽
Res ipsa loquitur ("the thing speaks for itself"): when the deviation is so obvious that the whole 4-D analysis is almost a formality. Classic example: a surgeon leaves a sponge inside a patient during surgery, and the patient later develops sepsis traced directly back to that sponge. Duty, deviation, damages, and direct causation are all self-evident from the single fact pattern.

The 4 D's: Duty → Deviation → Damages → Direct Causation

💊
Deviation alone isn't malpractice. Prescribing trazodone off-label for agitation is a deviation from its FDA-labeled indication, but it's a common, benefit-driven practice that causes no harm — so on its own it doesn't support a malpractice claim. Deviation only becomes a problem in combination with the other 3 D's.
🛡️
Your best defense against a suicide-related malpractice claim: regular, documented assessment showing a lack of suicidality — e.g., using a structured tool like the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale. You are not expected to predict the future — only to act on what was predictable/foreseeable. If the patient gave no indication of suicidality, failing to intervene is not a deviation from the standard of care. The proof of this is documentation of ongoing suicide risk assessment, not any single note.

Contracting for Safety ≠ Protection

A safety contract made in the absence of a careful suicide assessment offers no legal protection. It's the assessment — not the contract — that protects the physician.

Family Promises ≠ Your Responsibility Transferred

A family's promise to monitor a patient does not absolve the physician of responsibility if the patient is suicidal and no assessment/intervention occurred.

Section 08

Answers to Common Ethical Dilemmas

Quick-hit rules, phrased close to how boards tests them.

🚫
Sex with patients — the absolute rule: never have sex with a current patient — ever. If you are a psychiatrist specifically, never have sex with a current or former patient — ever (unlike some other specialties, there is no "cooling-off period" exception for psychiatry). Consequences: loss of a malpractice suit with no coverage of damages by your insurance company, potential conviction for rape or sexual assault/battery, loss of your state medical license, expulsion from national associations (AOA, etc.), and a permanent record kept by the NPDB (National Practitioner Data Bank). Before the NPDB existed, physicians disciplined in one state could simply move to another and start over, since state boards didn't share records — the NPDB closed that loophole nationally.
Exploitation
Using the therapeutic relationship for personal gain — e.g., hiring a patient or going into business with them. No business interactions with a patient beyond them paying you for treatment. (Gray-area exception: a small town with only one plumber, who happens to be your patient — some practical overlap is sometimes unavoidable, but keep it to genuine emergencies, not routine business.)
Bequests
Unethical for a patient to leave a psychiatrist part of their estate at death.
Recording for teaching
Allowed — but informed consent must be obtained, spelling out the purpose, scope, and use of the recording.
Retiring physicians
Should give patients sufficient notice and make every effort to find them reasonable follow-up care.
Insurance disclosure
Ethical to release patient information to an insurer, if limited to what's needed to process the claim.
Acceptable gifts
Small, non-monetary gifts are acceptable — motivation matters more than value. Cash is never acceptable (suggest a charity donation instead). Gifts from the medical industry (pens, notepads, a textbook) are fine only if they directly benefit patients and carry no substantial monetary value. A cat toy from a patient who knows you have a cat is a fine example of harmless — declining it would be its own kind of overcorrection.
Gifts from unstable patients
Gifts from a psychiatrically unstable patient (e.g., a manic patient) should not be accepted.
Missed visits
Ethical to charge a fee for a missed appointment.
Executions
Psychiatrists are forbidden from participating in executions, per the APA.
Fee splitting
Illegal — e.g., a psychiatrist paying an internist for referrals to their practice.
Language barrier
If an exam vignette shows a patient speaking a different language than the physician — get an interpreter. No exceptions.
🎙️
"The Goldwater Rule": psychiatrists asked for their opinion about a public figure may share their level of expertise on psychiatric issues in general — but may not comment specifically on that person unless they have personally examined them and obtained that person's consent to discuss their situation publicly.
Section 09

Ethics & Forensics Terms — Glossary ⭐ HY

Definition + a worked example for each — these terms are frequently tested by giving a short vignette and asking "which principle is this?"

Paternalism

DefinitionPerforming an action for another's benefit without that person's consent.
ExampleA patient has failed several PO medications; the doctor wants the patient on an IM injection for compliance. The patient refuses. The doctor says, "It's an IM injection or nothing."

Fiduciary Duty

DefinitionDuty to act in the patient's best interest.
ExampleA patient presents with depression; first-line is usually an SSRI like Prozac. You attended a drug-company dinner the night before for a brand-new drug you have reservations about — and you'd get a kickback for prescribing it. You still prescribe Prozac anyway.

Mens Rea & Actus Reus

DefinitionA socially harmful act alone is not enough to constitute a crime. Guilt requires both mens rea (evil intent) and actus reus (voluntary conduct).
ExampleMany mentally ill patients who commit socially harmful acts lack one of these two elements — which is the legal opening for an insanity-related defense (see M'Naughton Rule below).

Substituted Judgment

DefinitionA surrogate makes a decision based on what the patient would have wanted, accounting for the patient's own wishes and values.
ExampleA patient is mentally ill with no decision-making capacity (e.g., actively psychotic). The daughter is consulted because she should act taking into account the wants and needs of her mother.

Respondeat Superior

DefinitionThe person highest in the hierarchy is legally responsible for those in lower positions.
ExampleA medical assistant administers an IM injection but injects into a vein; the patient dies. The physician is sued — not the MA.

Respect for Autonomy

DefinitionThe patient's right to self-determination — they should not be coerced and must be given adequate information to make their own decisions.

Altruism

DefinitionPutting the needs of others before your own.
ExampleA stressed medical student still finds time to volunteer at a shelter to help others, receiving no money in return.

Beneficence

DefinitionThe obligation to help patients in order to relieve their suffering.
ExampleAn inpatient has a headache; you don't ignore it — you give them a Tylenol.

Non-Malfeasance

DefinitionThe duty to do no harm.

Justice

DefinitionEquitable distribution of social wealth and benefits.
ExampleMaking healthcare available to all keeps with justice; providing medical care to only a faction of a larger group would go against this principle.

Parens Patriae

DefinitionThe doctrine allowing the state to intervene and act as a surrogate parent for those unable to care for themselves.
ExampleA judge, acting on behalf of the state, makes treatment decisions for the benefit of a patient regarding mandatory medication administration.

M'Naughton Rule ⭐ HY

DefinitionNot guilty by reason of insanity if the person had a mental illness such that they were unaware of the nature, quality, and consequences of their actions, or were incapable of realizing their actions were wrong.

Durable Power of Attorney

DefinitionA document permitting the advance selection of a substitute decision-maker, who can act once the patient becomes incompetent through illness.

McGarry Instrument

DefinitionA clinical guide evaluating 13 areas of functioning to determine competence to stand trial.
StandardTo be competent to stand trial, the defendant must be able to consult with their lawyer with a reasonable degree of understanding, and have a rational, factual understanding of the charges against them — including relating to the attorney, understanding courtroom roles, appreciating the charges/penalties, disclosing pertinent information, and testifying relevantly.

Sexual Behavior & Forensic Treatment

DefinitionSexual behavior outside what is "healthy and not harming" can carry serious legal/societal consequences. The most prosecuted paraphilias are exhibitionism, pedophilia, and sexual sadism (particularly when leading to rape or murder).
Court-mandated treatmentSex-offender treatment can be mandated for those deemed "sexually dangerous" or "sexually violent." This breaks with the ordinary medical model of informed consent — patients can be involuntarily submitted to treatment, don't get to choose provider or treatment type, and completion is often a condition for community release or avoiding jail. In this specific realm, the court becomes the surrogate decision-maker, and the patient loses the right to refuse treatment.
Section 10

Seclusion and Restraint

Core Principle

PurposeSeclusion should be used only to protect the immediate physical safety of the patient and staff.
Least restrictive alternative applies here tooIf a patient can be treated as an outpatient, they should not be kept in the hospital. If they can be treated in an open setting, seclusion should not be used.
🚫
Seclusion cannot be used as: coercion, discipline, or for staff convenience or retaliation. It is a safety measure only, never a punitive one.
Order required
A written order from an MD, DO, or (in some states) an NP is needed.
Ongoing review
The patient's condition must be regularly reviewed and documented.
Time-limited
Orders are time-limited; extension requires periodic reauthorization by the MD/DO, and the order should be stopped at the earliest possible time.
Chemical restraint
When medications are used specifically to subdue a patient, they constitute a form of chemical restraint.
🚑
Next step for an acutely psychotic, agitated patient who is an immediate danger to self or others: physical AND pharmacological restraints (antipsychotic ± benzodiazepine). If there is no immediate danger, attempt de-escalation techniques first. Your own safety comes first.
🆘
Just attempted suicide, risk of further self-harm remains high? Next step: involuntarily hospitalize the patient.
Section 11

Informed Consent & Termination of Care

Informed Consent

Required Elements

Patients must consent to treatment in most circumstances, typically via a consent form covering: explanation of the procedure and its purpose, identification of alternative procedures available (even if the patient can't afford them), experimental-procedure status, reasonably expected risks and discomforts, description of benefits, an offer to answer questions, and notice that consent can be withdrawn at any time.
🧠
The 4 exceptions to informed consent — "WIPE it away": Waiver (explicitly done by the patient, e.g. for surgery/research) · Legally Incompetent (<18 years old, or lacking capacity) · Therapeutic Privilege (withholding information that may harm the patient) · Emergency (a life-threatening condition).

Minors ⭐ HY

The ruleIf a patient is under 18, their own consent is not sufficient — the consent of their parent/guardian is required.
The exceptions — "Sex, Drugs, Rock and Roll"Parental consent is usually NOT required for: Sex (contraception, STIs, pregnancy*), Drugs (substance abuse treatment), and Rock and Roll (emergency/trauma care) — plus two more situations outside the mnemonic: the minor is legally emancipated (self-supporting, married, or in the military), or a psychiatric emergency (though involuntary commitment may still be necessary if parents are unavailable or refuse consent).
*The exception to the exceptionAbortion is treated differently — most states require some form of parental involvement in a minor's decision to have an abortion.

🧫
Reportable STIs: virtually all STIs are reportable diseases to the health department — the one notable exception is genital herpes (HSV-2), which is not on the CDC's National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS) list (though some states may still require it be reported to the state health department).

Termination of Care

Improper termination can itself be a cause of malpractice. Done properly, it requires:

Discuss the termination thoroughly with the patient
Send a letter of termination including: reason for termination, date of termination, availability for emergencies only until that date, willingness to recommend other doctors, willingness to provide records to the new doctor, and a note on the need for continued treatment
Allow the patient adequate time to find another provider
Give the patient a list of appropriate providers
Provide records to the new doctor upon request
Send the termination letter by certified mail with return receipt requested
⚠️
A psychiatrist can be accused of abandonment through improper termination if they: fail to stay abreast of the patient's condition; fail to admit the patient to the hospital when needed; discharge a patient from the hospital prematurely; fail to provide the patient a way to contact them between visits; fail to maintain reasonable contact with a hospitalized patient; fail to provide clinical coverage while away from the office; fail to maintain boundaries; or terminate a patient based solely on a managed care organization's denial of benefits.
Section 12

Practice Questions

Vignette-style, matching how this topic is actually tested. Tap an option to check your answer.

Question 1
A psychiatrist's patient describes a detailed, specific plan to harm a former coworker whose home address he knows. The psychiatrist judges the threat to be credible and imminent. What is the psychiatrist's most appropriate first step?
A. Immediately call the police and the intended victim, with no further clinical intervention
B. Attempt the least restrictive stabilizing options first (e.g., adjust medication, increase visit frequency, involve family, or hospitalize) as part of a plan to protect the potential victim
C. Do nothing, since patient fantasies are never reportable
D. Wait for the patient to act before intervening
Answer: B. Tarasoff (Section 04) requires protecting the identified victim, but the psychiatrist should still try the least restrictive stabilizing measures first, escalating to warning/hospitalization if the danger persists. This isn't a "fantasy" (C) — the plan is specific, credible, and imminent.
Question 2
A patient's spouse calls the psychiatrist, unprompted, to report that the patient has stopped taking medication and has become increasingly paranoid at home. The psychiatrist listens to the spouse's concerns. Has the psychiatrist violated confidentiality?
A. Yes — any contact with family without the patient's consent is a violation
B. No — outside parties may give information to the physician without the patient's consent; the physician just cannot confirm or disclose information back without authorization
C. Yes, unless it is a documented emergency
D. No, because family members are always exempt from confidentiality rules
Answer: B. The asymmetry rule (Section 01): information can flow in from outside parties without patient consent, but the physician cannot proactively obtain or disclose patient information without authorization.
Question 3
A hospital's electronic health record system is upgraded, and administrators want to know which HIPAA rule governs the new safeguards required for that system specifically.
A. The Privacy Rule
B. The Security Rule
C. The Tarasoff Doctrine
D. The McGarry Instrument
Answer: B. The Security Rule (Section 03) applies specifically to electronic PHI; the Privacy Rule (A) is broader, covering paper, electronic, and spoken information.
Question 4
A patient with schizophrenia, involuntarily hospitalized, refuses all antipsychotic medication. The treatment team believes medication is clinically necessary. What must the team demonstrate in order to medicate this patient over objection (assuming no acute emergency)?
A. Nothing further — involuntary hospitalization alone authorizes involuntary medication
B. That the patient lacks capacity to refuse, and that the treatment is in the patient's best interest
C. That the patient's family agrees with the treatment plan
D. That the medication has no side effects
Answer: B. Involuntary hospitalization does not negate the presumption of competence to refuse medication (Section 06) — the team must separately show incapacity to refuse plus that treatment serves the patient's best interest (paternalism).
Question 5
A patient dies by suicide two weeks after a routine outpatient visit at which no suicidal ideation was elicited or documented as absent. The family sues for malpractice. What is most likely to determine the outcome?
A. Whether the psychiatrist could have predicted the suicide with certainty
B. Whether there is documentation of an ongoing, adequate suicide risk assessment showing no indication of suicidality
C. Whether the patient had signed a safety contract
D. Whether the patient's family had agreed to monitor them
Answer: B. Documentation of ongoing assessment is the actual legal defense (Section 07) — physicians aren't required to predict the future, only to act on the foreseeable. A safety contract (C) or family monitoring (D) alone offers no legal protection without a real underlying assessment.
Question 6
Which combination of elements must a plaintiff prove to win a psychiatric malpractice claim?
A. Damages and Direct Causation only
B. Duty, Deviation, Damages, and Direct Causation
C. Mens rea and Actus reus
D. Beneficence and Non-malfeasance
Answer: B. The "4 D's" (Section 07) are all required together — missing any one element defeats the claim.
Question 7
A defendant with a documented history of severe psychotic illness committed a crime while acutely delusional, genuinely believing he was defending himself from an imminent physical attack that did not actually exist, and did not understand that his action was wrong. Which legal standard is most relevant to his defense?
A. The McGarry Instrument
B. Respondeat superior
C. The M'Naughton Rule
D. Parens patriae
Answer: C. The M'Naughton Rule (Section 09) governs not-guilty-by-reason-of-insanity determinations — inability to understand the nature/quality/wrongness of one's actions due to mental illness. The McGarry Instrument (A) instead assesses competence to stand trial, a different question entirely.
Question 8
A medical assistant administers an intramuscular injection incorrectly, injecting into a vein, and the patient dies as a result. The supervising physician — who did not perform the injection — is the one named in the resulting lawsuit. What legal doctrine explains this?
A. Substituted judgment
B. Parens patriae
C. Respondeat superior
D. Fiduciary duty
Answer: C. Respondeat superior (Section 09) holds the person highest in the hierarchy legally responsible for those below them.
Question 9
A 16-year-old presents alone requesting treatment for a sexually transmitted infection and asks that her parents not be told. Under most state laws, is parental consent required?
A. Yes — parental consent is always required for minors regardless of the condition
B. No — venereal disease is a commonly cited exception allowing a minor to consent to their own treatment
C. No, but only if she is legally emancipated
D. Yes, unless a judge grants an exception in advance
Answer: B. Venereal disease, pregnancy, substance abuse, and contagious disease are commonly cited state-law exceptions to the general rule that parents/guardians consent for a minor's treatment (Section 11).
Question 10
A psychiatrist wants to stop treating a patient who has become verbally abusive during sessions. What is the most legally appropriate way to proceed?
A. Simply stop scheduling further appointments without further communication
B. Send a termination letter by certified mail with return receipt, stating the reason and date of termination, providing emergency-only coverage until then, and offering a referral list and records transfer
C. Terminate immediately with no notice, since the patient's behavior justifies it
D. Ask the patient's family to inform the patient
Answer: B. Proper termination (Section 11) requires adequate notice, a documented reason, continued emergency coverage until the effective date, a referral list, and records availability — skipping these steps risks an abandonment claim.
Question 11
A patient with no advance directive on file becomes incapacitated. She is unmarried, has no children, and her parents are both alive. Who is the appropriate surrogate decision-maker?
A. Her closest sibling
B. Her parents
C. The treating physician, using paternalism
D. No one — treatment must be withheld until a court appoints a guardian
Answer: B. Absent an advance directive, the default surrogate order (Section 05) is spouse → adult children → parents → siblings, under the substituted judgment standard. With no spouse or children, her parents are next in line.
Question 12
A surgeon needs to withhold a specific piece of information from a patient before a procedure, judging that disclosing it would itself cause the patient significant psychological harm and undermine the treatment. Which exception to informed consent does this describe?
A. Waiver
B. Legal incompetence
C. Therapeutic privilege
D. Emergency
Answer: C. Therapeutic privilege — the "P" in WIPE (Section 11) — is withholding information specifically because disclosing it would harm the patient. It's a narrow exception, not a general license to withhold unwelcome information.
Question 13
A defendant with schizophrenia is being evaluated for competence to stand trial. Which finding is most relevant to that determination?
A. Whether he understood the nature and wrongness of his actions at the time of the crime
B. Whether he has a rational, factual understanding of the charges and can assist his attorney in his defense
C. Whether he has a documented history of conduct disorder before age 15
D. Whether his family agrees with the proposed defense strategy
Answer: B. Competence to stand trial (Dusky v. United States, Section 05) asks whether the defendant can understand the charges and assist counsel now — a distinct question from the M'Naughton Rule (A), which asks about his mental state at the time of the crime for an insanity defense.

Rapid Review Grid

Confidentiality
Default rule — implicit in treatment; release only with patient authorization or legal compulsion.
Asymmetry rule
Can't take info from outsiders without consent; outsiders can give info without patient consent.
6 exceptions
Tarasoff, involuntary hospitalization, supervision, emergency, incompetence (guardian), mandatory reporting.
Physician-patient privilege
Right to refuse to testify about patient communications; survives the patient's death.
HIV exception
Ethically permissible to warn at-risk contacts; may still need legal input before breaching.
HIPAA Privacy Rule
All PHI — paper, electronic, spoken. Right to records (not psychotherapy notes). No auth needed for TPO.
HIPAA Security Rule
Electronic PHI only.
Tarasoff I / II
I = duty to warn. II = duty to protect (broader than just warning).
Tarasoff trigger
Imminent, probable danger to a specific identifiable victim; try least-restrictive stabilization first.
Least restrictive alternative
PO → ODT → IM → restraints; de-escalate ASAP. Applies to Tarasoff, med-over-objection, and seclusion/restraint alike.
Capacity vs. Competence
Capacity = clinical, task/time-specific, decided by psychiatrist. Competence = legal, decided by courts.
4 capacity standards
Communicate a choice, understand information, apply it to self, reason logically (agreement not required).
CARD mnemonic
Capacity requires Communication of a decision, Appreciation of facts, Reasoning, and Demonstrated understanding.
Advance directives
Living will (end-of-life wishes) + healthcare proxy (in effect only once capacity is lost). No directive → spouse → adult children → parents → siblings.
Competence to stand trial
Dusky v. United States: understand the charges + able to assist your attorney. Distinct from M'Naughton (mental state at time of crime).
Involuntary commitment bases
Police power (protects citizens from each other) + parens patriae (protects those who can't care for themselves).
Med over objection
Involuntary hospitalization ≠ incompetent to refuse meds. Must prove incapacity + treatment is in best interest.
Malpractice — 4 D's
Duty → Deviation → Damages → Direct Causation. All four required.
Suicide malpractice defense
Documented ongoing risk assessment — not a safety contract, not a family promise.
Sex with patients
Never with current patients. Psychiatrists specifically: never with current OR former patients.
Goldwater Rule
Can discuss psychiatric expertise generally about a public figure; cannot diagnose them without exam + consent.
Mens rea / actus reus
Both required for criminal guilt; mentally ill patients often lack one — opens the door to an insanity defense.
M'Naughton Rule
NGRI if mental illness prevented understanding the nature/quality/wrongness of the act.
McGarry Instrument
13-area clinical guide for competence to stand trial (understand charges, work with attorney).
Respondeat superior
Highest person in the hierarchy is legally responsible for those below.
Parens patriae
State acts as surrogate parent for those unable to care for themselves.
Seclusion/restraint
Only for immediate physical safety; never coercion/discipline/convenience; written MD/DO(/NP) order, regularly reviewed, time-limited.
WIPE mnemonic
Exceptions to informed consent: Waiver, legally Incompetent, Therapeutic Privilege, Emergency.
Informed consent minors
"Sex, Drugs, Rock and Roll" + legal emancipation + psychiatric emergencies. Abortion is an exception to the exceptions in most states.
Reportable STIs
All are reportable except genital herpes (HSV-2).
Acute agitation, immediate danger
Physical + pharmacological restraints (antipsychotic ± benzo). No immediate danger → try de-escalation first.
Gifts
Small/non-monetary OK, cash never OK. Never accept gifts from psychiatrically unstable (e.g., manic) patients.
Proper termination
Discuss + certified letter w/ reason, date, emergency coverage, referrals, records + adequate transition time.
Abandonment
Improper termination, no coverage while away, premature discharge, terminating solely for a benefits denial.

Legal Psychiatric Medicine — condensed pre-exam summary

Confidentiality & Exceptions

  • Default rule, implicit, release only w/ patient authorization or legal compulsion
  • Asymmetry: can't take info from outsiders w/o consent; outsiders can give it freely
  • 6 exceptions: Tarasoff, involuntary hospitalization, supervision, emergency, incompetence/guardian, mandatory reporting
  • Privilege survives the patient's death; HIV warning is ethically OK, get legal input first

HIPAA (1996)

  • Privacy Rule = all PHI (paper/electronic/spoken); Security Rule = electronic PHI only
  • No authorization needed for Treatment/Payment/healthcare Operations (TPO)
  • Right to records EXCLUDES psychotherapy notes
  • States may enact stricter rules than the federal floor

Tarasoff Doctrine

  • I = duty to warn; II = duty to protect (broader)
  • Trigger: imminent/probable danger to a specific identifiable victim
  • Try least restrictive first: meds → family → visit frequency → hospitalize
  • Fantasies alone aren't reportable — must be judged as likely to be acted on

Capacity, Competence & Med Objection

  • Capacity = clinical/task-specific (psychiatrist decides); Competence = legal (courts decide)
  • 4 standards / "CARD": communicate, appreciate facts, reason, demonstrate understanding
  • No capacity + no surrogate → treat per patient's best interest
  • Advance directives: living will + healthcare proxy (active only once capacity lost); no directive → spouse → adult children → parents → siblings
  • Competence to stand trial (Dusky v. US): understand charges + assist attorney. Involuntary commitment bases: police power + parens patriae
  • Involuntary hospitalization ≠ incompetent to refuse meds — must prove incapacity + best interest; emergency overrides right to refuse

Malpractice — 4 D's

  • Duty → Deviation → Damages → Direct Causation — all 4 required
  • Best suicide-malpractice defense = documented ongoing risk assessment, not a safety contract
  • Family monitoring promises don't transfer physician responsibility

Ethical Dilemmas — Quick Hits

  • Never sex w/ current patients; psychiatrists: never with current OR former
  • Bequests to psychiatrists = unethical; recording needs informed consent; fee splitting illegal
  • Charging for missed visits = OK; APA bars psychiatrists from executions
  • Goldwater Rule: general expertise OK, no diagnosing public figures without exam + consent

Seclusion & Restraint

  • Only for immediate physical safety — never coercion, discipline, or convenience
  • Written MD/DO(/NP) order, regularly reviewed, time-limited, stopped ASAP
  • Medication used to subdue = chemical restraint

Consent & Termination

  • Informed consent: procedure, alternatives (even unaffordable ones), risks, benefits, right to withdraw
  • WIPE exceptions: Waiver, legally Incompetent, Therapeutic Privilege, Emergency
  • Minors: "Sex, Drugs, Rock and Roll" + legal emancipation + psychiatric emergencies; abortion is the exception to the exceptions
  • All STIs reportable except genital herpes (HSV-2)
  • Proper termination: certified letter, reason/date, emergency coverage, referrals, records, adequate time

Ethics & Forensics Terms — Quick Reference

  • Paternalism: acting for someone's benefit without their consent
  • Fiduciary duty: duty to act in the patient's best interest
  • Mens rea / actus reus: evil intent + voluntary conduct, both required for guilt
  • Substituted judgment: surrogate decides based on what the patient would have wanted
  • Respondeat superior: highest person in hierarchy is legally responsible for those below
  • Respect for autonomy: patient's right to self-determination, free of coercion
  • Altruism: putting others' needs before your own
  • Beneficence: obligation to relieve suffering
  • Non-malfeasance: duty to do no harm
  • Justice: equitable distribution of social wealth/benefits
  • Parens patriae: state acts as surrogate parent for those who can't care for themselves
  • M'Naughton Rule: NGRI if illness prevented understanding nature/quality/wrongness of the act
  • Durable power of attorney: advance-selected substitute decision-maker for incompetence
  • McGarry Instrument: 13-area guide for competence to stand trial
  • Sex-offender treatment: can be court-mandated; court becomes surrogate decision-maker in this domain
