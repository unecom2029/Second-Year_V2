Foundations

Mood Episodes 101

🧸 ELI5

Episodes are the Lego bricks; disorders are the Lego sets you build from them. There are three bricks — manic, hypomanic, and depressive episodes. Different combinations of bricks (and how long they've been happening) build different disorders. Learn the bricks first and the disorders basically name themselves.

🧠 Lecture Framework — The DSM Is Your Bible

Dr. Wolfrum opened both lectures with this mental model, and it applies to every diagnosis in this system, not just mood disorders: almost every psychiatric diagnosis is an exclusionary diagnosis. Before you diagnose anything, run through this checklist:

  1. Is it significant enough to cause impairment? Waking up sad one morning isn't MDD. Feeling amped and ready to tackle the day isn't mania. Everyone lives somewhere on the mood spectrum day to day — a diagnosis requires impairment, which breaks down into three types: (1) can't function in daily life — school, work, ADLs; (2) poses a risk — to self, to others, or to their finances/safety; (3) disconnect from reality — can't follow normal thought processes (psychosis).
  2. Rule out a medical cause. Medical causes are often more reversible than psychiatric ones, and exam writers love "gotcha" questions where the real answer is a thyroid panel, not an SSRI.
  3. Rule out a substance cause. Remember substances cut two ways — intoxication and withdrawal both have psychiatric effects. A heavy drinker who is depressed isn't MDD — that's the depressant effect of alcohol. Someone in alcohol withdrawal isn't psychotic from a primary psychotic disorder — their GABA system is in freefall and glutamate is unopposed, which is also why withdrawal carries seizure risk.

Only after clearing all three do you land on a primary psychiatric diagnosis.

🧸 ELI5 — Why Depression Peaks Twice

MDD has a bimodal peak — around age 18 and again around age 50 — and the lecture's explanation is a nice mini-lesson in the biopsychosocial model: biologic (neurotransmitter/brain-region factors), psychologic (developmental stage — identity formation at 18 vs. Eriksonian midlife reflection at 50), and social (transition stressors — leaving home/starting college/career at 18; retirement, midlife reassessment at 50 — plus universal social stressors like isolation or financial strain). Any time a question seems to be asking "why does this happen," try running it through biologic → psychologic → social.

🧸 ELI5 — Bipolar I vs. II

Bipolar I is the "I mean it" version: one manic episode is all it takes to earn the diagnosis — depression is optional. Bipolar II needs a two-ingredient recipe: at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode, but if a manic episode ever shows up, the diagnosis is immediately promoted to Bipolar I. Hypomania is Bipolar II's ceiling — it can never break through into full mania and stay Bipolar II.

⚠️ Don't Forget

Always rule out substance-induced and due-to-a-general-medical-condition causes before diagnosing a primary mood disorder. Symptoms must cause significant functional impairment and not be better explained by another diagnosis. Depression and bipolar disorder both carry increased suicide risk — this comes up constantly on both classroom and boards.

Q: A patient has 4 days of elevated mood, grandiosity, and decreased need for sleep, with no psychosis and no major impact on her job. What is this episode, and what disorder(s) could it belong to?

Answer

This is a hypomanic episode (≥4 days, no psychosis, no marked impairment). Paired with a history of major depressive episodes, this points to Bipolar II. If it were part of a ≥2-year fluctuating pattern of mild hypomanic + mild depressive symptoms that never met full episode criteria, it would instead suggest cyclothymic disorder.

Depressive Disorders

MDD, PDD & PMDD

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

5+ of 9 SIG E CAPS symptoms for ≥2 weeks, including depressed mood or anhedonia, causing clinically significant distress or impairment. Lifetime prevalence up to 15% (up to 25% in women); annual prevalence ~10%; female:male ratio 2:1. Formally, diagnosis technically requires ruling out that symptoms represent a single vs. recurrent presentation (2+ episodes separated by ≥2 months of normal mood) — but in practice, most people are diagnosed off a single qualifying episode without getting hung up on that technicality.

🩺 Why PHQ-9 Matters More Than It Looks

~70–80% of people who get psychiatric care get it from their primary care provider, not a psychiatrist — and a PCP visit is often 15 minutes, while a full psychiatric intake for MDD can take closer to an hour. The PHQ-9 is a fast, validated screener that lets a time-strapped PCP get most of the way to a diagnosis without conducting a full intake. If it shows up on an exam, it's pointing you toward MDD.

Persistent Depressive Disorder (PDD / "Dysthymia")

Chronic depressed mood for most of the day, more days than not, for ≥2 years (1 year in children/adolescents), plus 2+ of: poor appetite/overeating, insomnia/hypersomnia, low energy, low self-esteem, poor concentration, hopelessness. Symptoms never absent for more than 2 months at a time. No MDE during the first 2 years (if present → consider MDD instead). Per lecture: think of it as generally a somewhat milder symptom picture than MDD, just stretched across years instead of weeks.

🧸 ELI5 — MDD vs. PDD

MDD is a hard storm — intense, but it passes. PDD is a low, grey overcast sky that just doesn't lift for years. Storms (MDD) can actually happen on top of the overcast sky too — that's called "double depression," and if a full MDE shows up it should make you reconsider whether it's really MDD instead of (or in addition to) PDD.

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) — brief mention

Symptoms occur in the final week before menses, improve within a few days after onset, and become minimal/absent the week post-menses. Requires 1+ of: mood swings/rejection sensitivity, anger/irritability, hopelessness/self-critical thoughts, anxiety/tension — plus enough additional symptoms (concentration, appetite, interest, fatigue, feeling overwhelmed, physical symptoms like bloating/breast tenderness, sleep changes) to reach 5 total. Must be confirmed with prospective daily ratings over ≥2 cycles and interfere with work/school/relationships.

🩺 Clinical Pearl

"Atypical" depression is a bit of a misnomer — it's actually the most common subtype. Mnemonic hook: atypical depression reacts (mood reactivity) and gains (weight/sleep/heaviness) — basically the opposite polarity of melancholic depression, which loses (appetite, pleasure, energy) and doesn't react to good news.

🧸 ELI5 — Typical vs. Atypical, In Plain English

Think of "typical" SIG E CAPS depression as the low-and-slow presentation: sleeping more, eating more or less but generally shutting down. Atypical features flip a couple of those dials the opposite direction — instead of sleeping too much, some people can't sleep at all (insomnia) even though everything else says depression. That reversal is exactly why it's called "atypical," even though statistically it's the most common flavor.

Medical Causes of Depression — What to Screen For

Dr. Wolfrum's framing: as second-year med students you'd out-diagnose most practicing psychiatrists on this specific point, because ruling out medical causes is exactly the kind of thing that's fresh in your head right now. Know these cold:

Thyroid disease

The single most-tested answer

If a question describes someone meeting MDD criteria and asks what lab to screen, TSH is the answer almost every time.

Anemia

Neck-and-neck with thyroid

Watch for an associated clue: pica (compulsively eating non-food items) can accompany anemia — classically ice, clay, or carrots are the exam's favorite examples.

Cardiac disease

Frequently under-recognized

Post-MI and other cardiac events carry a well-documented depression association.

Peripartum hormonal change

Ties to the peripartum-onset specifier

Dramatic hormone shifts around delivery are a recognized medical contributor, not just a "specifier" to memorize in isolation.

⚠️ Substances Cut Both Ways

Always think intoxication and withdrawal separately. Medications matter here too, not just illicit substances: anticholinergics and steroids are the classic exam culprits — steroids in particular are notorious for wrecking sleep and precipitating a manic-like picture, but they can just as easily cause depression, so if an exam gives you the option to blame steroids, it's usually right. (NSAIDs show up on some reference charts too, via a theorized inflammation-depression link — low yield, don't over-invest.)

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder — Treatment Trick Question

⚠️ Common Misconception

Most people assume PMDD gets treated like depression — start an SSRI. Roughly 70% of real-world patients do end up on an antidepressant, but that's not actually first-line. PMDD is thought to arise from the hormonal shifts of the menstrual cycle, so hormonal treatment (combined oral contraceptives) — which regulates/equalizes hormone levels across the cycle — is generally the preferred first-line treatment, and many patients do very well on it. If there's no appropriate estrogen-containing option available, an SSRI is the fallback. This same hormonal logic means introducing or withdrawing estrogen in a person not biologically female (e.g., in the context of gender-affirming hormone therapy) can produce a similar mood presentation — the hormones, not the biological sex assigned at birth, are the operative variable.

Q: A patient reports her depressive episodes only happen every winter and remit by spring, for the past 3 years. Which specifier applies, and how does this differ from "atypical features"?

Answer

With seasonal pattern — episodes and remissions have a regular seasonal relationship, no off-cycle episodes, over ≥2 years. This is about timing. "Atypical features" is about the symptom profile (mood reactivity, hyperphagia, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis) and can co-occur with a seasonal pattern, but they are separate specifiers.

Bipolar Spectrum

Mania, Hypomania & the Bipolar Disorders

Mood Spectrum — Dr. Wolfrum's lecture

🧸 ELI5 — Reading the Spectrum

Think of mood as a number line from −10 (severe depression) to +10 (mania), with 0 in the middle as "normal." Bipolar II is the territory from about −6 to +6 (dysthymia through hypomania) — big swings, but it never touches the extremes. Bipolar I is the whole line, −10 to +10 — it's allowed to hit full mania.

Mania vs. Hypomania — the exact line

🌤️

If a manic episode ever occurs, the diagnosis is Bipolar I regardless of prior hypomanic/depressive history.

🧸 ELI5 — Hurricane vs. Tropical Storm

Mania is a hurricane: it can flatten your house (impairment), force an evacuation (hospitalization), or come with a storm surge that distorts reality (psychosis) — and it sticks around for a full week. Hypomania is a tropical storm: strong winds people definitely notice, but it doesn't damage the house, doesn't require an evacuation, and moves through in as little as 4 days.

Epidemiology & Genetics

  • Lifetime prevalence worldwide: 1–3%
  • Mean age of onset: 18 years (Bipolar I), 20 years (Bipolar II)
  • Male:female ratio ≈ 1:1
  • 18th leading cause of disability in the US; during manic/hypomanic episodes ~70% have severely impaired functioning, during major depression ~90%

  • Monozygotic co-twin lifetime risk: 40–70% — one of the most heritable psychiatric conditions
🩺 Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ)

The bipolar-specific counterpart to the PHQ-9 — a quick screener for whether a patient's symptom picture and severity are consistent with a mood disorder on the bipolar spectrum. Same purpose as any screener in this system: get a fast, validated read in a time-limited visit rather than requiring a full diagnostic interview up front.

⚠️ Rule Out First

Before diagnosing a primary manic/hypomanic episode, screen for organic causes: medications (steroids, amphetamines, levodopa, corticosteroids), substances (cocaine, hallucinogens, caffeine), and medical conditions (Cushing disease, hyperthyroidism, MS, stroke, CNS infection). This is the same "rule out substance/medical cause first" logic that applies to every mood disorder.

Mixed Episodes

A mixed episode means manic and depressive symptoms are met concurrently — it's rare clinically, but shows up on exams as a straightforward "meets criteria for both at the same time" scenario. It's less contradictory than it sounds: think irritability plus mania's racing energy, or dark/hopeless thoughts occurring alongside classic manic hyperactivity — mania and depression can share a body at the same moment.

Hypomania Isn't Always a Problem

🧸 ELI5 — The Upside of Hypomania

Unlike full mania, hypomania doesn't cause significant impairment — and it can even look like a benefit. Think of the stereotypical high-achieving CEO or the physician who's constantly producing research: less need for sleep, more energy, bigger ideas. This is exactly why hypomania and even mania can be hard to treat clinically — patients often don't want the feeling to go away, because subjectively it feels good, even though objectively it's often unsustainable or a warning sign of worse to come.

Distinguishing Bipolar Disorder from Borderline Personality Disorder

🔑 High-Yield Differentiator — Timing, Not Symptoms

Bipolar disorder and borderline personality disorder (BPD) both involve dramatic mood swings, and mixing them up is a classic exam trap and a real clinical pitfall (a lot of BPD gets misdiagnosed as bipolar over a patient's lifetime). The fastest way to split them:

Unipolar vs. Bipolar Depression

"Unipolar depression" is an older term that just means MDD (as opposed to the depressive episodes that occur within bipolar disorder). Most patients present with a depressive episode first, often years before any manic episode ever appears — fewer than 5% of bipolar patients present with mania as their first episode. This is exactly why a well-meaning antidepressant prescription can "unmask" a previously undiagnosed bipolar disorder (see case below).

Episodic vs. Continuous — Placing Bipolar Among Related Diagnoses

A conceptual framework from lecture for quickly sorting mood-plus-psychosis question stems: is the psychiatric symptom picture episodic (comes and goes) or continuous (always present)?

MDD and Bipolar: mood episodes are the whole picture, no continuous psychosis. Schizoaffective: continuous psychosis plus episodic mood. Schizophrenia: continuous psychosis, no mood component.

  • MDD / Bipolar disorder: fully episodic — psychosis, when present, only occurs layered on top of a mood episode (usually late in a depressive episode; usually during the manic phase of bipolar disorder). Psychosis present in both the depressive and manic phases of the same illness suggests a more severe course.
  • Schizoaffective disorder: psychosis is continuous (present essentially all the time, absent treatment), with a mood component (depressive-type or bipolar-type) layered on top episodically — and often less frequent than mood episodes in primary mood disorders, because ongoing treatment tends to suppress them.
  • Schizophrenia: continuous psychosis with no mood-episode component at all.

"Unmasking" a Bipolar Disorder with an Antidepressant

Presentation: A patient presents with a depressive episode and is started on an antidepressant, as is standard practice. Several weeks later (roughly 3–6 weeks), they return looking manic.

What happened? This is a classic "unmasking" scenario — the patient likely had an underlying, not-yet-diagnosed bipolar disorder the whole time. Since <5% of bipolar patients present with mania as their very first episode, a first depressive episode can't rule bipolar disorder out, and an antidepressant can push a susceptible patient into their first manic (or hypomanic) episode.

Bipolar I or Bipolar II? Judge by extremity, not just presence of symptoms. Example given in lecture: if the patient is shopping more than usual and can comfortably afford it, that impulsivity looks more like hypomania. If the patient buys three cars and a Lamborghini and bankrupts themselves, that degree of impaired judgment and consequence is mania — pushing the diagnosis to Bipolar I.

How do you know it's "real" bipolar and not just a substance-induced mood disorder? There's no perfect test — it's a clinical judgment call. The strongest supporting clue is the typical pattern: most people with bipolar disorder present with a depressive episode first, and the age of first manic-episode onset for that small subset clusters in the 18–20 range, consistent with the overall epidemiology of the disorder.

Q: A patient with a well-documented history of Bipolar II disorder has a manic episode requiring hospitalization. What is the new diagnosis?

Answer

Bipolar I disorder. A single manic episode is sufficient to diagnose Bipolar I, and Bipolar II is defined by the absence of any lifetime manic episode. The occurrence of mania supersedes the prior Bipolar II diagnosis.

Risk Assessment

Suicide Risk — SAD PERSONS

Depression and bipolar disorder both carry increased suicide risk — this was emphasized on the very first slide of the lecture for a reason. Risk assessment and mitigation is described as one of the central, defining activities of psychiatric practice — not just a topic, but the actual day-to-day work. Risk mitigation breaks down into two categories: risk of an untimely death (suicide), and risk of dangerous behavior more broadly (violence toward others, reckless spending, or other consequential impulsive acts — relevant in mania as well as depression).

🩺 Four Treatments Specifically Linked to Reduced Suicide Risk

Most psychiatric treatments target symptoms broadly. A small, specific list is called out in the literature as having direct evidence for reducing death by suicide — worth memorizing as its own group because exams like to test exactly this kind of "unique fact":

  • ECT
  • Esketamine
  • Lithium
  • Clozapine (Clozaril)
🔑 First Aid Mnemonic — SAD PERSONS are more likely to die from suicide
  • Sex (male)
  • Age (young adult or older adult)
  • Depression
  • Previous attempt — highest risk factor
  • Ethanol or drug use
  • Rational thinking loss (psychosis)
  • Sickness (medical illness)
  • Organized plan
  • No spouse or social support
  • Stated future intent
⚠️ Know the Asymmetry

Women attempt suicide more often; men die by suicide more often (more lethal means — firearms are the most common method in the US, and access to guns increases risk of death). Protective factors: effective treatment of comorbidities, strong social/family/community connectedness, cultural or religious beliefs favoring self-preservation, and good problem-solving skills.

🔑 Gold Standards to Memorize Verbatim

Gold standard for depression: therapy + medication combined. Gold standard for mania: lithium. Severe depression sometimes warrants brief hospitalization even without acute safety risk — not because outpatient treatment can't work, but because an inpatient setting allows faster titration and augmentation of medications than a normal 4–6 week outpatient follow-up cadence would allow.

Choosing an SSRI vs. an SNRI

A classic exam setup gives you one SSRI, one SNRI, one TCA, and one MAOI as options — don't panic. SSRIs and SNRIs are tied as first-line, so the tie-breaker is almost always a comorbidity or side-effect clue hidden in the stem:

🎯

Favor an SSRI when...

  • "Garden variety" depression with no other clues
  • Comorbid anxiety-spectrum disorder — PTSD, OCD, panic, social anxiety
⚡

Favor an SNRI when...

  • Comorbid pain condition (fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain) — the norepinephrine component helps pain
  • Comorbid ADHD-like presentation or need for a mildly stimulating effect
🩺 The "Extra Clue" Pattern

Once you've picked SSRI vs. SNRI, exams love adding one more clue to point at a specific drug. Classic example: everything above is true and the patient smokes → answer is bupropion (FDA-approved for both depression and smoking cessation, plus an ADHD indication). Get comfortable scanning the stem for a single extra detail like this — it's almost always the intended differentiator.

🩺 Augmentation Options (Treatment-Resistant Depression)

Treatment-resistant = no benefit from multiple adequate medication trials. Augmentation strategies: 2 antidepressants combined, antidepressant + second-generation antipsychotic, antidepressant + mood stabilizer, or antidepressant + thyroid augmentation.

This drives how long you continue treatment: 6 months–1 year after a first episode, 3 years after a second episode, and lifetime treatment after a third episode. The STAR*D trial is the classic reference guiding real-world antidepressant sequencing — it found that SSRIs and SNRIs both had reasonable tolerability and effectiveness compared with other classes, which is exactly why they're tied as first-line.

🧸 ELI5 — Why Duration Escalates

Each depressive episode is like a pothole the brain's "mood road" falls into more easily the next time. One pothole, you can probably fix the road and move on (6mo–1yr). A second pothole in the same spot means the road is genuinely weaker there (3 years of maintenance). A third pothole means you just keep paving that spot forever (lifetime treatment).

Interventional & Device-Based Options

ECT

First used 1939 · Gold standard for affective disorders

Indicated for severe/refractory depression, peripartum depression, psychotic features, catatonia, acute suicidality. Screening: basic labs, EKG, head imaging. Induces a seizure, changing neurotransmitter state and possibly improving plasticity. Adverse effects: disorientation, headache, partial antero-/retrograde amnesia (usually resolves in 6 months). No absolute contraindications — safe in pregnancy and older adults.

Esketamine / Ketamine

NMDA receptor antagonist; also opioid receptor & AMPA (glutamate)

Indicated for treatment-resistant, severe unipolar MDD without psychotic features, after failure/declined other indicated treatments. Adverse effects: dissociation, psychotomimetic effects, ↑ systolic/diastolic BP and pulse (usually resolve quickly). Liable to abuse/addiction/diversion; associated with neurotoxicity and bladder dysfunction with chronic misuse.

TMS

Transcranial magnetic stimulation

Magnetic frequency applied to specific brain regions to induce change. Non-invasive, but requires frequent treatments.

DBS

Deep brain stimulation

Implanted electrode targeting a specific brain region. Invasive, implanted device.

VNS

Vagal nerve stimulation

Pulse generator, most commonly on the left vagal nerve. Invasive; unclear efficacy for depression. Lecture flagged TMS/DBS/VNS as "not high yield" overall — know they exist and roughly what they are, don't over-invest.

Q: A patient has failed three adequate antidepressant trials. Her psychiatrist adds low-dose second-generation antipsychotic to her current SSRI. What is this strategy called, and name two alternative augmentation strategies.

Answer

This is augmentation for treatment-resistant depression. Alternatives: combining two antidepressants, antidepressant + mood stabilizer, antidepressant + thyroid hormone, or moving to an interventional approach like ECT or ketamine/esketamine.

Walking Through a New Depression Presentation

Presentation: A 25-year-old presents with 2 weeks of insomnia, suicidal ideation, social withdrawal, poor self-care, and decreased energy — a first episode, no major recent life stressor.

What else do you want to know? Family history of depression, current medications/substances (to rule out a substance-induced cause), and medical history. In this vignette the patient was recently diagnosed with a thyroid disorder — on an exam, the SIG E CAPS picture is usually still enough to call this MDD even with that medical comorbidity in the background, though clinically you'd want to investigate further before settling on a primary psychiatric diagnosis. Always ask about safety planning — is there a plan to self-harm, and is anything currently keeping the patient safe? This is one of the most consistently tested (and clinically vital) questions in the whole system.

Treatment ladder, step by step:

  1. SSRI or SNRI first. Decide between them using comorbidities: a pain syndrome or ADHD-like presentation nudges toward an SNRI; a "garden variety" presentation stays with an SSRI. Mechanism for both: block the transporter, flood the synapse with the relevant neurotransmitter(s) — takes several weeks to show full effect.
  2. If the SSRI/SNRI doesn't help → move to a TCA. Mechanism: multiple rings binding many receptors, more side effects, and true overdose danger.
  3. If the TCA causes an intolerable side effect (say, excessive sedation) → the "Others" category — bupropion, mirtazapine, trazodone — chosen based on which side-effect profile fits the patient and any specific indication (e.g. smoking cessation for bupropion).
  4. If that still doesn't work → an MAOI, reserved this late in the sequence because of the dietary restrictions and interaction burden.
  5. If pharmacotherapy across all classes fails → ECT first, then TMS and/or ketamine/esketamine.

SSRIs — first-line for most depression/anxiety

MOA: Bind the serotonin transporter (SERT), blocking reuptake of serotonin from the synapse. At the cell body, autoreceptor desensitization over time boosts serotonin release too — a dual effect.

🧸 ELI5 — SSRI Mechanism

Serotonin is a text message between two neurons. Normally, right after it's "read," the sender scoops the message back up (reuptake) so the conversation stops. An SSRI jams the scoop — the message just sits in the gap longer, getting read over and over. That's a Selective Serotonin Reputake Inhibitor.

🩺 Why Side Effects Show Up Before the Antidepressant Effect

The moment an SSRI is started, the transporter is blocked and serotonin floods the synapse immediately — but the postsynaptic receptors need time (roughly 4–6 weeks) to adapt/upregulate before the person actually feels less depressed. Side effects, though, don't need that adaptation: the flood of serotonin hits GI receptors, sexual-function pathways, and other targets right away, which is why side effects typically appear in the first 1–2 weeks, well before the therapeutic benefit does. This is also why severely depressed patients are sometimes hospitalized — not because outpatient treatment can't work, but because it allows faster dose titration and augmentation than waiting out a 4–6 week outpatient trial. And it's why "I started the antidepressant two weeks ago and don't feel different" is expected, not a treatment failure.

Side effects to know: GI distress, sexual dysfunction, SIADH, serotonin syndrome (see below).

☐ Antidepressant Discontinuation Syndrome

A relatively recently-recognized (last ~10 years) but testable phenomenon: a patient who abruptly stops or misses doses of an antidepressant — SSRIs included, not exclusive to any one class — presents with flu-like symptoms, dizziness, "brain zaps," irritability, and sensory disturbances. Management: restart the antidepressant and taper slowly. If restarting isn't appropriate (e.g. concern for serotonin syndrome), manage supportively (hydration, rest) instead. Higher risk with short half-life agents like paroxetine; lower risk with long half-life agents like fluoxetine.

🧸 ELI5 — Picking a Class

SSRIs/SNRIs are the "default order" — safe, effective, broad-spectrum. TCAs are the powerful-but-risky specialist you call in for pain syndromes or refractory OCD, knowing an overdose can kill. Q: A patient on venlafaxine develops a headache, palpitations, and diastolic BP of 128 after eating aged cheese and salami at a family dinner. What happened, and what's the mechanism?

Answer

Careful — this scenario is actually the classic MAOI + tyramine hypertensive crisis presentation, not venlafaxine's typical picture. Tyramine displaces NE at the synapse, causing excess sympathetic stimulation. Treatment is phentolamine. (Venlafaxine itself can raise BP at higher doses via NE reuptake inhibition, but the tyramine-food trigger specifically implicates MAOI use.)

🩺 Treatment

Benzodiazepines and supportive care first; cyproheptadine (a 5-HT2 receptor antagonist) if there's no improvement. Prevention: avoid combining serotonergic drugs and observe a proper washout period between them (2 weeks after stopping an MAOI before starting another serotonergic drug).

🔑 The Real Risk Factor Is Crossing Mechanisms, Not Counting Drugs

It's tempting to think "more serotonergic drugs = more risk," but the more precise clinical teaching point is: risk climbs sharply when you combine different mechanisms of raising serotonin, not just more drugs from the same mechanism. Two SSRIs together (both block reuptake) carry less added risk than one SSRI plus one MAOI (reuptake block + degradation block) — because once the reuptake pumps are saturated, adding a second reuptake blocker doesn't meaningfully raise synaptic serotonin further, but blocking a completely different step in the pathway does. Practically: don't stress as much about stacking two SSRIs or two SNRIs, but always pause before combining across classes — especially anything touching an MAOI.

Q: How do you distinguish serotonin syndrome from neuroleptic malignant syndrome at the bedside?

Answer

Serotonin syndrome develops rapidly (hours) with hyperreflexia and clonus, and follows a serotonergic drug/combination. NMS develops over days with "lead-pipe" rigidity and hyporeflexia, and follows antipsychotic (dopamine-blocking) exposure. Both can cause hyperthermia and autonomic instability, but the reflex findings and time course are the key differentiators.

Notice the pattern: lithium, valproate, and carbamazepine are the go-to's for mania but are only backup options for bipolar depression. Lamotrigine flips that completely — it's weak for mania but is actually a first-line choice for bipolar depression. Different phases genuinely need different drugs — memorizing "one mood stabilizer treats bipolar" will get you the wrong answer on a phase-specific question.

⚠️ Antidepressant Caution in Bipolar

Fluoxetine's own bipolar-context side effect profile flags worsening of depression and manic episodes in bipolar patients, plus increased suicidality in children — antidepressants in bipolar disorder are generally used cautiously and typically alongside a mood stabilizer, never as unopposed monotherapy. The one FDA-approved exception is a fluoxetine + olanzapine combination — but that's a maintenance-phase indication, not a treatment for acute mania. You would not start an acutely manic patient on fluoxetine.

Which Drug, for Which Phase — the Underlying Logic

  • Acute mania: lithium, valproate, and antipsychotics all break a manic episode effectively.
  • Acute depression: lithium, carbamazepine, and lamotrigine all help prevent/treat depressive episodes. Valproate does not — it's the most sedating of the mood stabilizers and doesn't protect against depressive episodes, so relying on it alone for the depressive phase is a mismatch. Antipsychotics are inconsistent here — augmentation (e.g. SSRI + second-generation antipsychotic) is the more common approach for a resistant depressive phase.
  • Maintenance: lithium, carbamazepine, and lamotrigine all have a role.
🔑 Why Lithium Is "The" Gold Standard

Lithium is the only agent effective across all three domains — mania, depression, and maintenance — which is exactly why it's considered the gold standard for bipolar disorder as a whole, not just for breaking an acute manic episode.

🩺 Real-World Inpatient vs. Outpatient Patterns

Worth knowing even though it's more clinical color than hard criteria: valproate is frequently used inpatient because it's excellent at breaking acute mania quickly, but clinicians often work to transition patients off it once they reach outpatient care, given its side-effect burden. Lamotrigine is rarely started inpatient — its titration is too slow and it's not good at breaking acute mania. Carbamazepine is also rarely started inpatient, mainly because of how many other medications it interacts with.

Bipolar Disorder in Pregnancy

Pregnancy category letters are a simplified stand-in for risk-benefit data: think of them like school grades — A/B is reassuring, C means "we don't have enough information either way," and D means known harm has been documented. Most psychiatric medications land in category C simply because pregnant patients are rarely included in trials, not necessarily because they're dangerous. Because untreated severe depression or mania in pregnancy is itself harmful to the fetus, the goal is an informed risk-benefit conversation, not automatic avoidance of all medication.

  • Lithium: Ebstein anomaly risk is trimester-specific — cardiac formation happens early, so risk is highest in the first trimester. Many clinicians minimize or hold lithium during the first trimester, then resume or increase the dose later in pregnancy once heart formation is complete and risk drops substantially.
  • Valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine: neural tube defect risk; folic acid supplementation (typically more than a standard prenatal vitamin provides) can help mitigate this risk.
  • Antipsychotics: most have little to no pregnancy safety data — the exception is clozapine, which actually has accumulated outcome data. Because of this data gap (not necessarily proven danger), antipsychotics are often the preferred bipolar treatment in pregnancy when a mood stabilizer's teratogenic risk is a bigger concern.
  • Antidepressants: no strong teratogenic signal either way. The main concern is neonatal discontinuation/serotonin syndrome in the newborn after birth, from placental transfer — but most patients are kept on their antidepressant throughout pregnancy regardless, because untreated depression carries its own fetal risk.

Working Through a New Manic Presentation

Presentation: A 25-year-old presents with increased activity, grandiosity ("I run the world"), no sleep, and distractibility.

Is this enough to diagnose a manic episode? If the stem tells you the patient has been hospitalized, the answer is unambiguously yes — hospitalization alone satisfies the mania threshold. Without that detail, the extremity of the grandiosity is the clue: "I run the world" is a much bigger claim than "I'm going to solve this one big problem," and pushes the picture toward full mania rather than hypomania.

What do you ask about before treatment? Family history (bipolar disorder is one of the more strongly heritable psychiatric conditions — think of the monozygotic twin concordance data), and the duration/frequency pattern (to rule out borderline personality disorder mimicking rapid mood swings). Then, before choosing a medication: kidney and liver function (affects lithium and valproate/carbamazepine dosing), pregnancy status (teratogenicity), and what other medications the patient takes (interactions — particularly relevant if carbamazepine is being considered, since it's an auto-inducer and interacts with hormonal contraception).

Which medication? Depends on severity and phase. A hospitalized, severely manic patient points toward lithium. A milder, hypomanic-level presentation might instead start with a more tolerable option like lamotrigine. And it depends on where the patient is in the illness course — acutely manic, in maintenance, or in a depressive phase — since (as above) the right drug changes by phase.

One safety note carried over from the mood-stabilizer teaching: a strongly anti-manic drug like valproate can push a patient into a depressive episode if used too aggressively — whenever a treatment strongly suppresses one pole, watch for an overcorrection toward the other.

Lithium hitches a ride on the same sodium channels the kidney uses to reabsorb salt at the PCT. 🩺 Clinical Pearl — Check an Ammonia Level

Valproate can cause a hyperammonemic delirium — a confused, altered patient on valproate should prompt an ammonia level, regardless of whether the dose "seems low." This isn't strictly dose-dependent, and it can happen at any point in treatment. Catching this is a small detail that reads as genuine clinical fluency. Carbamazepine

Key feature: CYP autoinduction — induces its own metabolism (and that of oral contraceptives). Generally well tolerated: nausea, dizziness, drowsiness. Can cause SIADH (hyponatremia via increased ADH sensitivity). Rash is the second most common cause among mood stabilizers. Teratogenic (neural tube defects). Can help treat certain pain syndromes.

⚠️ Autoinduction — a Classic Trap

Autoinduction means the drug ramps up its own breakdown over time via the CYP system. Clinical trap: start carbamazepine, check a level a week later — it looks great, therapeutic, everything's going well. Come back two weeks later and the patient looks more manic again, even though nothing about the prescription changed. Check the level and it's dropped significantly — carbamazepine induced its own metabolism faster than expected, and the same dose no longer produces the same blood level. The fix isn't "the drug stopped working" — it's recognizing that carbamazepine levels need to be rechecked periodically even on a stable dose, especially early in treatment.

Because it's such a "dirty drug" that interacts with many other medications, be especially cautious prescribing it to patients who are young, otherwise healthy, and — critically — anyone capable of becoming pregnant: carbamazepine's induction of hepatic metabolism makes standard oral contraceptives unreliable, so a non-hormonally-interacting method (e.g. an IUD) is usually recommended instead.

🎯 Mood Disorders — High-Yield One-Pager

Condensed by learning objective — LO1 Symptoms & Timeline · LO2 Treatment Options · LO3 Differentiation by Indication/Side Effects/Choice

LO 1 · SYMPTOMS & TIMELINE

Episode Durations

  • Manic: ≥7d, OR hospitalization/psychosis — AND impairment
  • Hypomanic: ≥4d, NO psychosis/marked impairment
  • MDE: ≥2wk, 5+/9 SIG E CAPS (mood or anhedonia required)
  • PDD: ≥2yr (1yr peds), 3+ milder symptoms
  • Cyclothymic: ≥2yr hypomania-lite + depression-lite, never full episode

Mnemonics

  • DIGFAST (mania): Distractibility, Impulsivity, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity↑, Sleep need↓, Talkativeness
  • SIG E CAPS (depression): Sleep, Interest↓, Guilt, Energy↓, Concentration↓, Appetite, Psychomotor, Suicidality
  • SAD PERSONS: Sex(M), Age, Depression, Prior attempt (#1 risk factor), EtOH/drug, Rational thought loss, Sickness, Organized plan, No support, Stated intent

Disorders at a Glance

  • MDD = 1+ MDE
  • PDD = chronic mild depression ≥2yr
  • Bipolar I = 1+ manic episode (depression not required)
  • Bipolar II = hypomania + MDE, never a manic episode
  • Cyclothymic = hypomania-lite + depression-lite ≥2yr

Bipolar vs. Borderline PD

  • Bipolar: episodes last weeks–months, recur ~every couple years, ↓sleep need is core
  • BPD: swings hours–days, multiple times per day/week, sleep not disrupted the same way
LO 2 · TREATMENT OPTIONS

Depression Treatment Ladder

  • Mild: lifestyle changes ± app-based therapy
  • Moderate: therapy or antidepressant
  • Severe: therapy and antidepressant
  • Resistant: augment (2 antidepressants / +SGA / +mood stabilizer / +thyroid) → ECT → ketamine/esketamine, TMS

Antidepressant Line of Therapy

  • 1st: SSRI = SNRI (tied)
  • 2nd: TCA
  • 3rd: "Others" — bupropion, mirtazapine, trazodone
  • 4th: MAOI

Gold Standards & Key Facts

  • Gold standard for depression = therapy + medication
  • Gold standard for mania = lithium
  • 4 treatments proven to ↓ suicide: ECT, esketamine, lithium, clozapine
  • Recurrence risk climbs every episode → duration: 1st ep. ≥1yr, 2nd ep. 2–3yr, 3rd ep. lifetime

Bipolar Treatment by Phase

  • Mania: lithium, valproate, carbamazepine, 2nd-gen antipsychotic — all 1st-line
  • Depression: lamotrigine 1st-line; lithium/carbamazepine also help; valproate does not
  • Maintenance: lithium (best evidence), carbamazepine, lamotrigine
  • Fluoxetine+olanzapine = only FDA-approved antidepressant combo, maintenance only (not acute mania)
  • 2nd-gen antipsychotics used: aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, ziprasidone — haloperidol is 1st-gen despite appearing in med tables; don't pick it if a question specifically asks for "2nd-generation"

Interventional / Procedural Options

  • ECT: gold standard, safe in pregnancy/elderly, no absolute contraindications
  • Ketamine (IV) / Esketamine (intranasal): treatment-resistant depression w/o psychotic features
  • TMS: non-invasive, no anesthesia, ~5x/week — lower yield than ECT/ketamine
LO 3 · DIFFERENTIATION

Antidepressants — Named Drugs

  • SSRI: Sertraline (most Rx'd, well tolerated) · Fluoxetine (longest t½, 2D6 inhibitor, also FDA for bulimia) · Escitalopram (no QTc effect) · Citalopram (QTc risk — older) · Fluvoxamine (OCD only) · Paroxetine (shortest t½, "dirty" — many interactions)
  • SNRI: Venlafaxine (dose-dependent, low=SSRI-like/high=NE, ↑BP) · Desvenlafaxine (NE-specific metabolite) · Duloxetine (+ neuropathic pain/fibromyalgia)
  • TCA — tertiary (more SE): Amitriptyline (pain) · Imipramine (pain/IBS/enuresis) · Clomipramine (OCD/panic) · Doxepin (bipolar depression/insomnia)
  • TCA — secondary (fewer SE): Nortriptyline (cyclic vomiting/IBS/pain/migraine/smoking cessation) · Desipramine (ADHD)
  • MAOI: Tranylcypromine · Phenelzine · Isocarboxazid · Selegiline (patch, MAO-B selective, less strict diet)
  • Others: Bupropion — NDRI, smoking cessation, ↓seizure threshold (black box) · Mirtazapine — alpha-2 antagonist, wt gain/sedation · Trazodone — mainly insomnia, priapism · Vilazodone/Vortioxetine — newer, low yield beyond MOA

Serotonin Syndrome vs. NMS

  • 5-HT syndrome: hours, hyperreflexia/clonus, autonomic instability, cross-mechanism combos = highest risk
  • NMS: days, "lead-pipe" rigidity, hyporeflexia, high fever, follows antipsychotics

Quick Pick by Scenario

  • Depression + pain → SNRI or TCA
  • Depression + smoker → Bupropion
  • Depression + insomnia/↓appetite → Mirtazapine
  • OCD → Fluvoxamine (SSRI) or Clomipramine (TCA)
  • Acute mania, hospitalized → Lithium
  • Bipolar depression → Lamotrigine
