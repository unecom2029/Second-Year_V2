Section 01 · Part 1

History of Psychiatry

Guiding question: Articulate a brief history of the field of psychiatry.

Psychiatry is structurally different from the rest of medicine: there is no blood test or brain scan that makes a definitive diagnosis. Psychological tests (Myers-Briggs, projective testing) can hint at a diagnosis, but nothing proves one the way labs and imaging do elsewhere. That pushes psychiatric diagnosis into a subjective gray area — the DSM-5-TR tries to make it objective with explicit criteria, but real patients don't always fit the boxes cleanly.

🩺
This is why psychiatry rewards imagination, comfort with uncertainty, and strong generalist skills — a huge number of psychiatric referrals are medical conditions that were missed in primary care and masquerade as psychiatric illness. A running theme of this whole course: always rule out a medical or substance cause before calling something psychiatric.

The First Psychiatric Medication: Lithium

⚗️
Lithium — a monovalent alkali metal, #3 on the periodic table — has been used for mania for thousands of years. Soranus of Ephesus (2nd century A.D.) recommended alkaline mineral waters (naturally containing lithium) for various ailments. Lithium was even added to 7-Up soda as a mood-calming ingredient until it was pulled roughly 100 years ago, once its kidney-toxicity risk in mass consumption became clear.

Before Medications: 1940s "Treatments"

Frame this in context: in 1940, lithium was essentially the only drug available, and asylums were overflowing with patients who weren't improving on anything. That desperation drove some dramatic, out-of-the-box interventions — a few historical examples below (not an exhaustive list).

Prefrontal Lobotomy

Popularized by Walter Freeman in the 1940s as a last-resort intervention for severely institutionalized patients.

Trephining (Burr Holes)

Drilling holes into the skull — one of the oldest interventions in the history of psychiatric/neurologic "treatment," predating modern medicine entirely.

Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

Still used today with great efficacy — don't believe the media hype. Modern ECT is far removed from these early images; more on this later in the course.

Insulin Shock Therapy

Induced hypoglycemic coma as a psychiatric intervention — largely abandoned once its risk profile became clear.

Metrazol ("Convulsive Therapy")

A circulatory/respiratory stimulant used to intentionally induce convulsions before ECT became standardized.

Hydrotherapy

Prolonged immersion/wrapping treatments intended to calm agitated patients.

Restraints and chains were also standard practice; physical restraints are still used today and remain a necessity at times for patient and staff safety — the difference now is heavy regulation, documentation, and the "code grey" framework covered in Part 2.

Timeline: From Opium to Modern Psychopharmacology

Ancient – 2nd c. A.D.
Lithium-containing alkaline waters used for mania; likely the oldest continuously-used psychiatric treatment.
Early 1800s
Morphine isolated from crude opium. Laudanum (tincture of opium) used for mental illness.
1832
Codeine isolated.
Early–mid 1800s
Cocaine sees widespread stimulant use, adapted from South American indigenous practice.
1887
First amphetamine developed — Benzedrine, originally used as a bronchodilator.
1898
Heroin created via acetylation of morphine, marketed as a cough suppressant.
1930s
Amphetamines recognized for CNS activity — found to produce euphoria, relieve fatigue, and (yes) produce anorexia. A 1936 case series described benefit in depression, manic-depression, and "dementia praecox" (early term for schizophrenia) — but effects were temporary. Benzodiazepine compounds are also discovered this decade, though not used for anxiety until the 1960s.
1950s
Birth of psychopharmacology. Discovery of phenothiazines; discovery of iproniazid's (and other MAOIs') antidepressant effects in tuberculosis patients; synthesis of other MAOIs (tranylcypromine); synthesis and early use of TCAs like imipramine for depression.
1960s
Broad clinical use of TCAs (e.g. amitriptyline) begins, now preferred over MAOIs. The catecholamine hypothesis of depression is widely discussed. Major advances in nervous-system study techniques.
1990s
Development and clinical use of SSRIs (fluoxetine, citalopram, sertraline); antidepressant use broadens to anxiety disorders and pain conditions; primary care physicians begin prescribing antidepressants far more commonly.
2000s
Dual-acting (NE + 5-HT) antidepressants with fewer TCA-like side effects — SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine), mirtazapine, bupropion, nefazodone. More rigorous study of antidepressants' analgesic properties.
🏘️
The net effect of the psychopharmacology era: deinstitutionalization — the shift toward community-based treatment and programs aimed at reintegrating the mentally ill into society and the workforce, rather than warehousing them in asylums.
Practice Question
A patient asks why lithium — a drug still used for bipolar disorder today — was once added to a popular soft drink. What is the historical basis for this?
A. Lithium was believed to cure infectious disease outbreaks in the early 1900s.
B. Lithium had long been used for mania/mood calming, and was added to 7-Up as a general "calming" ingredient before its toxicity profile was appreciated.
C. Lithium was added purely as a flavor stabilizer with no therapeutic intent.
D. Lithium was mandated by the FDA as a mood stabilizer in all consumer beverages in the 1950s.
Answer: B. Lithium's mood-calming reputation predates modern psychiatry by centuries; it was pulled from 7-Up about 100 years ago once the risk of kidney injury from casual, high-volume consumption became a concern.

Practice of Psychiatry

Guiding questions: The mental health system in your state · What happens in a psychiatric hospital · Why insurance matters in treatment planning.

Mental Health Systems Vary Enormously by State

Maine

Structure2 state hospitals (Augusta & Bangor); several private hospitals with psych wards; no state-run community mental health system — all community mental health is privately owned/operated.

New Hampshire

StructureVery different model — each of the 10 counties has its own state-run mental health clinic.

Georgia

Structure5 state hospitals, dozens of privately-run psych hospitals/psych wards, and a hybrid of state-run and private outpatient clinics.

Takeaway: funding, structure, and access differ vastly state to state — there's no single national model to memorize, but you should expect to ask "how does this work here?" wherever you rotate.

📊
~95% of psychiatric patients are managed as outpatients — the majority (less severely ill) by their PCP, and a minority (more severely ill) by psychiatrists. Only the most severely ill, or those directed by other means (e.g. legal/court), are hospitalized.

Commitment: Voluntary vs. Involuntary ⭐ HY

When illness escalates to the point psychiatric admission is needed, that's termed commitment. If a patient needs hospitalization but doesn't meet involuntary criteria, they can agree to a voluntary ("sign-in") admission on their own volition, provided they have capacity to make that decision.

Involuntary — Inpatient Criteria*Involuntary — Outpatient Criteria*
Danger to self or othersMentally ill, and capable of surviving safely in the community with supports
Gravely disabledBased on psychiatric history, respondent is in need of treatment to prevent further deterioration
In need of protection or treatment for health/safety because of a mental disorderCurrent mental status negates/limits the ability to voluntarily comply with treatment

*Exact statutory criteria vary by jurisdiction — this is the general shape, not a fixed national standard.

⚖️
If a patient is held involuntarily, all patients must be seen by a judge within 72 hours of admission. (There is ongoing litigation in NH testing aspects of this system — capacity/competence will be covered in more depth in the legal psychiatry lecture.)

What Happens Inside a Psychiatric Hospital — Great Variability

  • State hospital vs. community hospital
  • Locked vs. unlocked unit
  • Restraints/seclusion used vs. not; chemical vs. physical restraint
  • If state-run: is it under DHHS or the Department of Corrections?
  • A psychiatrist can take away a patient's "rights" in some involuntary situations
  • "Code grey" / personal-safety-emergency protocols allow taking away rights in the moment when a patient cannot make reasonable choices for their own safety
  • Partial hospitalization programs (PHP) sit between inpatient and outpatient intensity
Why insurance matters: the case of "LH" Real case

Dr. Smith described one of his first patients out of residency, practicing in Calais, Maine — a small town where he was the only psychiatrist for roughly 200 miles. Police dropped a ~50-year-old woman, "LH," directly at his outpatient office (unusual, but word travels fast in a small town).

She'd been brought in because a boy riding past her house on a dead-end street noticed her windows were coated in something brown. His mother investigated and called police. Officers found LH had been alone in her house for several weeks, acutely psychotic — smearing feces on the windows, her toilet and bathtub filled with feces and urine, her water shut off for nonpayment, her cats dead, and a small shrine set up on her living room floor where she was praying to Satan for the strength to survive.

On exam she could state her name but not her date of birth, didn't know where she was despite living in that town 30 years, and didn't know her own phone number. Dr. Smith transferred her to the psychiatric hospital — an outpatient clinic cannot manage an acutely psychotic patient — and followed her care.

The root cause, once records came in: she'd been discharged from another psychiatric hospital ~2 weeks earlier with a prescription for ziprasidone and only a 3-day supply. She tried to fill it, couldn't afford it, and relapsed into psychosis as a direct result.

The fix: in the hospital, Dr. Smith switched her to a long-acting injectable (LAI) antipsychotic — paliperidone (Invega) — a once-monthly injection that time-releases the medication, functioning like birth control in that sense. No daily pill to forget or afford piecemeal → 100% compliance by design. She improved, moved in with her daughter, and — while still living with schizophrenia — was able to work and interact with her grandchildren.

💡
Teaching point: a single phone call to the pharmacy to check affordability before writing the discharge script could have prevented the entire relapse, the loss of her cats, and real risk to her life. Always consider the whole biopsychosocial and financial picture, not just the correct drug on paper.

Defense Mechanisms

Guiding question: What is a defense mechanism? What are some examples?

🔑
Definition: A defense mechanism is a psychological tendency the ego uses to prevent a person from being overwhelmed by conflict (and the resulting anxiety) among the id, ego, and superego. Freud's original framework. Defense mechanisms operate at an unconscious level — you're not aware of them while using them, though you may recognize their use afterward, often through psychotherapy. They're also the reason our own automatic habits sometimes baffle (or drive crazy) the people close to us.
🎯
There are technically ~26 named defense mechanisms (more granular lists run to 40–45). Dr. Smith does not require memorizing all of them — some psych courses do, this one doesn't. Expect roughly one question on the exam. Dr. Wolfrum's psychotherapy lecture goes into more depth; wait for the study guide to know exactly which ones you're responsible for. What's worth knowing at this stage: the handful of mature defenses, and roughly what each term means when you see it on boards.

All 26 — Ordered Roughly Least → Most Mature

DefenseMaturityDefinitionExample
RepressionLess mature Unconsciously blocking a disturbing wish, thought, or memory from awareness entirely (distinct from suppression, which is conscious). A patient has no memory at all of a traumatic childhood event.
FantasyLess mature Substituting reality with a less disturbing, imagined version of the world. A terminally ill patient states she's sure she'll survive because her long-deceased mother assured her so in a dream.
RegressionLess mature Reverting to an earlier, less mature pattern of behavior under stress. An adult throws a tantrum when frustrated at work.
SplittingLess mature Viewing people/situations as all-good or all-bad, unable to integrate positive and negative qualities together. Classic in BPD. A patient idealizes a new nurse, then suddenly devalues her entirely after one minor disagreement.
DenialLess mature Failure to acknowledge a disturbing aspect of external reality. A patient with a grim prognosis insists he will get better because he "just knows it."
ResistanceLess mature Unconscious opposition to bringing repressed material into conscious awareness — often surfaces in therapy as avoidance of a topic. A patient repeatedly changes the subject whenever therapy approaches a specific childhood memory.
ConversionLess mature Emotional conflict transformed into a physical symptom. A patient who is obviously angry about a subtle insult instead develops sudden gastric distress and denies any feelings beyond concern.
DissociationLess mature Sealing off disturbing thoughts/emotions from consciousness. Hours after a terrible accident, a patient says she has no memory of the event.
Isolation (of affect)Less mature Separating a thought from its attached emotional tone, making it tolerable. Common in extremely stressful moments. Hours after a fatal accident, a patient mechanically discusses the financial/legal paperwork with no visible emotion.
ProjectionLess mature Attributing one's own uncomfortable feelings (often anger/guilt) to other people instead. A patient seeking large financial compensation accuses other patients of "manipulating the system."
UndoingLess mature An act performed to symbolically negate or atone for an unacceptable thought, feeling, or act. A person who had an angry thought about their spouse buys them an elaborate gift that same day.
SymbolizationLess mature One object/idea unconsciously comes to represent another, usually via some similarity. A patient with unresolved anger toward a parent has recurring nightmares about a menacing authority figure.
DisplacementLess mature Emotions tied to an unacceptable object/idea are transferred onto a different, often symbolically-related, object. A patient badly injured in an accident becomes angry at his surgeon over a small scar from the reconstructive procedure.
DistortionLess mature Reshaping one's perception of a disturbing reality to make it more palatable. A patient permanently disabled by his own careless driving views himself as a victim of "bad traffic laws."
Reaction formationLess mature Unacceptable feelings/impulses are transformed into their exact opposite. A person with an unwanted attraction acts with exaggerated hostility toward that person instead.
IntrojectionLess mature Absorbing aspects of another person into one's own self-image, including their negative views. A victim of spousal abuse absorbs her partner's negative opinions of her worth and defends his right to batter her.
Passive-aggressive behaviorLess mature Expressing unconscious anger/aggression through passive failure to meet another person's needs. A resident consistently arrives late to rounds with his attending because he "has so many important tasks."
CompensationLess mature Using skill/competence in one area to counterbalance a self-perceived deficiency in another. A patient with a gait disturbance becomes a self-taught expert on the history of dance.
IdealizationLess mature Unrealistically attributing only excellent qualities to a person or situation. An elderly man fondly but unrealistically describes his hometown as the most wonderful place that ever existed.
IdentificationLess mature Modeling oneself after someone perceived as admirable or powerful. A hospitalized child decides she wants to be a doctor and asks for her own toy stethoscope.
RationalizationLess mature Distorting reality to make an undesirable act or outcome seem more acceptable — often used when a person can't tolerate the real reason. A student who didn't study for an exam blames a "bad test-writer" for the failing grade.
IntellectualizationLess mature Transforming an emotionally disturbing event into a purely cognitive/academic problem. A physician diagnosed with a serious illness becomes consumed with reading every available clinical research report about it.
SublimationMature Channeling an unacceptable impulse into a socially acceptable, often productive, activity. A person with aggressive impulses becomes a competitive athlete in a contact sport.
SuppressionMature The conscious, deliberate postponement of attention to a disturbing thought or feeling — the mature, conscious cousin of repression. A surgeon consciously sets aside worry about a personal problem in order to focus during an operation.
HumorMature Using amusing thoughts or observations to decrease anxiety about a difficult situation. A patient with a severe motor disability tells droll stories about his struggles with airport security.
AltruismMature Decreasing one's own internal fear/anxiety by caring for others facing a similar struggle. A patient with a rare but serious illness devotes himself to leading a support group for others with the same disease.
🧠
Memory Hook — "SASH" for the mature four: Suppression, Altruism, Sublimation, Humor. If you only lock in four terms for this topic, make it these — they're the ones most likely to be the "correct" answer choice rather than a distractor.

Mental Status Examination & the Psychiatric SOAP

Guiding question: Comment on the psychiatric diagnostic interview — what is the MSE and what are its parts?

Psychiatry documents in a SOAP-note structure, just like every other specialty — but with one substitution: the Mental Status Exam (MSE) is done either alongside or in place of the physical exam. It's a written description detailed enough that another clinician reading only your note can understand the patient's mental state at the time you saw them.

Parts of the Initial Psychiatric Interview

  1. I–IV. Identifying data, source/reliability, chief complaint, present illnessSame as any H&P.
  2. V–IX. Psychiatric, substance use, medical, family, developmental/social historyStandard background — but substance use history carries extra weight here.
  3. X. Review of systems
  4. XI. Mental Status ExaminationThe psychiatry-specific substitution for (or complement to) the physical exam.
  5. XII. Physical examination
  6. XIII–XV. Formulation, DSM-5 diagnoses, treatment planFormulation is where the biopsychosocial synthesis happens (below).

🧩
MSE vs. MMSE vs. MoCA — a very common source of confusion:
  • MSE (Mental Status Exam): the whole written narrative described above — a full description of the patient's mental functioning.
  • MMSE (Mini-Mental State Exam): just one piece of the MSE — a scored (0–30) cognitive screening test.
  • MoCA (Montreal Cognitive Assessment): a specific type of MMSE-style instrument, testing visuospatial/executive function, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation — also scored out of 30.

The Biopsychosocial Formulation

Psychiatry's version of the "assessment" section leans on this framework explicitly: consider biological, psychological, and social contributors together when explaining why you're arriving at a given diagnosis.

Biological

Genetic predisposition, neurochemistry, medication effects, immune response, HPA axis / fight-flight, other physiologic responses.
Ex: a patient with schizophrenia and a positive family history — that family history is the biological contributor.

Psychological

Learning, emotions, thinking, attitudes, memory, perceptions, beliefs, stress-management strategies.
Ex: the patient's mental state at the time of the visit, and how it feeds into the diagnosis you're making.

Social

Social support, family background, interpersonal relationships, cultural traditions, socioeconomic status, poverty, access to medical care.
Ex: homelessness, lack of family support, and financial limitations — "social determinants of health" (SDOH), a much more mainstream concept now than 20 years ago, when it was largely a psychiatry-only consideration.
Practice Question
A colleague says a patient "scored 24 on her MSE." What's wrong with this statement?
A. Nothing — the MSE is scored out of 30, and 24 is a valid score.
B. The MSE itself isn't a scored instrument — a numeric score out of 30 describes the MMSE (or a MoCA), which is only one component of the full MSE.
C. The MSE is scored out of 100, so 24 would indicate severe impairment.
D. MSE scores only apply to inpatient settings.
Answer: B. The full MSE is a written narrative, not a single number. The 0–30 scored instruments (MMSE, MoCA) are cognitive-screening subcomponents that sometimes get colloquially (and incorrectly) referred to as "the MSE."
Section 05 · Part 5

DSM-5-TR

Guiding questions: What is the DSM-5(-TR) and how is it organized? How do you diagnose via decision trees/"axes"?

📖
The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders was first published in 1952 with 106 disorders. By DSM-5(-TR), that number has roughly tripled to 365 — one for every day of the year, a genuinely useful (if coincidental) memory hook.

Why Did the Number of Diagnoses Triple?

  • Diagnostic precision improved — e.g. "manic depression" split into Bipolar I, Bipolar II, and Cyclothymic Disorder as distinct entities rather than one catch-all.
  • Insurance & billing — more granular codes support more granular billing. Unfortunate but real, and a running theme you'll see across all of medicine, not just psychiatry.
🎯
You do not need to memorize criteria for all 365 diagnoses — nor even most of them. Dr. Smith wants deep criteria-level memorization for only a handful of "ideal" high-yield diagnoses (major depressive disorder, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder). For everything else: know it well enough to recognize it and answer board-style questions, not recite DSM criteria from memory.

Diagnostic Logic: Rule Out Before You Call It Psychiatric ⭐ HY

🚨
The single most important idea in this whole section: always rule out a drug-induced or general medical cause before diagnosing a primary psychiatric illness. Medical/drug causes are treatable with medical therapy; a missed medical cause treated as "just depression" with antidepressant after antidepressant is a classic, avoidable failure mode.
  1. 1. Rule out Malingering & Factitious DisorderIs the person intentionally producing/feigning symptoms (for external gain, or for the sick role itself)?
  2. 2. Rule out a substance etiologyIntoxication, withdrawal, or a medication side effect mimicking psychiatric illness.
  3. 3. Rule out an etiological medical conditionE.g. thyroid disease, a CNS lesion, a metabolic derangement.
  4. — Only now can you diagnose a primary mental illness —
  5. 4. Determine the specific primary psychiatric disorder(s)Pick from the DSM-5-TR's 365 diagnoses.
  6. 5. Differentiate Adjustment Disorder from residual Other Specified/Unspecified categories
  7. 6. Establish the boundary with no mental disorderDistress that doesn't rise to a diagnosable condition.

The DSM-5 itself also publishes formal decision-tree flowcharts for specific presentations (e.g. delusions) for clinicians who think algorithmically — worth knowing they exist, not worth memorizing branch-by-branch at this stage.

Primary vs. Secondary Psychiatric Disorder

🔀
Primary psychiatric disorder: one of the 365 DSM-5-TR diagnoses in its own right. Secondary psychiatric disorder: psychiatric symptoms caused by something else entirely — e.g. hypothyroidism-induced depressive disorder. The distinction matters clinically: if it's secondary, treating the underlying medical problem can resolve the "psychiatric" symptoms too.

The (Retired) DSM-IV Axis System — Still Worth Knowing

The axis system was dropped when DSM-5 was published in 2013 — but most practicing attendings trained before that change, and many still document this way, or you'll hear "what's your Axis I?" on rotations. Recognizing it puts you ahead of peers from other schools.

AxisCovers
Axis IClinical disorders (e.g. schizophrenia, bipolar I, OCD, ADHD) + other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention
Axis IIPersonality disorders (e.g. borderline, OCPD) + intellectual disability (formerly "mental retardation")
Axis IIIGeneral medical conditions (e.g. hypothyroidism, diabetes)
Axis IVPsychosocial & environmental problems — maps directly onto the "Social" arm of the biopsychosocial formulation (poverty, homelessness, lack of family support)
Axis VGlobal Assessment of Functioning (GAF) — a single 0–100 functional score
🧠
GAF in practice: nobody realistically scores near 100. Most high-functioning people land ~90–95; most patients being actively managed for a psychiatric condition fall roughly in the 50–80 range; more severe impairment runs into the 35–45 range. It's not an exact science — it's a fast way to communicate "this person is a 90" vs. "this person is a 40."

CYP450: The Three Terms That Matter Clinically ⭐ HY

Structure of the enzyme system isn't the point here — the clinical logic is. You will never be asked "which P450 isoform metabolizes bupropion." You will be given a scenario with two drugs and asked to reason through the interaction.

Substrate + Inhibitor

A drug that reduces enzyme activity → increases plasma level of the substrate → increased side effects & toxicity risk.

Substrate + Inducer

A drug that increases enzyme activity → decreases plasma level of the substrate → loss of efficacy / relapse risk (the handout frames this as "decreased side effects and toxicity," which is true, but the clinically dangerous side of induction is usually treatment failure, not safety).
TermDefinition
SubstrateThe drug (or other substance) being metabolized by the P450 enzyme system — i.e., the drug you're actually giving the patient.
InhibitorA drug/substance that reduces enzyme activity → substrate is metabolized more slowly → plasma level of substrate rises.
InducerA drug/substance that increases enzyme activity → substrate is metabolized faster → plasma level of substrate falls.

Two Real Cases from Dr. Smith's Practice

Eastport, ME — smoking cessation script triggers anticholinergic delirium Inhibitor

A stable patient on imipramine (a TCA — a 2D6 substrate) was doing well in Dr. Smith's practice. Her PCP started her on bupropion (a 2D6 inhibitor) for smoking cessation, without knowing she was already on imipramine.

Bupropion inhibited 2D6 → imipramine could no longer be metabolized normally → imipramine levels rose to ~10× therapeutic levels. She became confused and delirious with florid anticholinergic symptoms, and developed a cardiac arrhythmia — TCAs carry real cardiotoxicity at supratherapeutic levels. She was admitted to telemetry; bupropion was stopped immediately, and she improved.

💡
This is exactly why medication reconciliation and reading existing notes before prescribing matters — a single uncoordinated addition caused a genuinely dangerous interaction.
NH State Hospital — cigarette smoking as a 1A2 inducer Inducer

~95% of patients with schizophrenia smoke cigarettes — still the current statistic, and worth remembering because hydrocarbons in cigarette smoke are a 1A2 inducer. Both clozapine and olanzapine (antipsychotics) are 1A2 substrates.

A patient was hospitalized, titrated onto clozapine, and did well — psychosis cleared and he was discharged. He went home and resumed smoking ~2 packs/day. His psychosis re-emerged (he was found naked in the street, screaming, while smoking) and he was re-hospitalized — despite being fully medication-compliant. The smoking induced faster clozapine metabolism, plasma levels plummeted, and psychosis returned.

💡
A patient can do everything "right" (take every dose) and still relapse if a co-occurring exposure is silently inducing the enzyme that clears their drug. Always ask about smoking status when managing 1A2-substrate antipsychotics.

Practice Question
A patient stable on clozapine is admitted with re-emergent psychosis despite confirmed medication adherence. Records show he recently resumed heavy cigarette smoking. What's the mechanism?
A. Nicotine directly blocks dopamine receptors, counteracting clozapine's effect.
B. Hydrocarbons in cigarette smoke induce CYP1A2, increasing clozapine's metabolism and lowering its plasma level below the therapeutic threshold.
C. Smoking inhibits CYP1A2, causing clozapine toxicity.
D. Smoking has no pharmacokinetic effect on clozapine; the relapse is unrelated.
Answer: B. Cigarette-smoke hydrocarbons (not nicotine itself) are a CYP1A2 inducer; clozapine and olanzapine are 1A2 substrates, so resumed smoking drops their plasma levels and can precipitate relapse even with perfect adherence.

Section 08 · Part 6c — Closing

The Mental Health Crisis: Barriers to Care

Guiding question: Why is there such a mental health crisis in this country? What are the barriers on both the patient's and the doctor's side?

👤 Patient-side barriers

  • May present with a somatic complaint instead of a psychiatric one
  • Concurrent medical illness can obscure psychiatric symptoms
  • Denial
  • Stigma & shame
  • Belief that psychiatric illness is untreatable
  • Belief that psychiatric drugs are mind-altering / addictive / will "change my personality" (largely unfounded, but a real barrier)

🩺 Doctor-side barriers

  • Lack of time (often insurance-driven visit-length pressure)
  • Fear of embarrassing the patient by bringing it up
  • Uncertainty — psychiatry's inherent "gray area" is uncomfortable for more black-and-white-oriented specialties
  • Fear the patient's illness will be unresponsive to treatment
  • The doctor's own prior negative experience with mental illness
  • Lack of knowledge

Transference vs. Countertransference

🔄
Transference: the patient's unconscious projection of their own beliefs/feelings onto the doctor.
Countertransference: the doctor's unconscious projection of their own beliefs/biases onto the patient.

Worked example: a doctor whose mother was an abusive alcoholic may unconsciously be harsher (or, in overcorrection, unusually over-nurturing) toward patients who struggle with alcohol — that's countertransference. If the direction runs from patient onto doctor instead, it's transference. Both are unconscious by definition; covered in more depth later in the course.
📚
Required/recommended reading going forward: the Key Drug List (primary pharm resource, more thorough than First Aid for this course), the Medical Letter (non-profit, pharma-independent, ~15–20 min per issue, includes a self-quiz — the only mandatory outside reading), and Sadock & Sadock's Kaplan & Sadock's textbook as an optional (if long-winded) reference. Upcoming lectures will also use recorded standardized-patient videos from Symptom Media.

🎯 Intro & History of Psychiatry — High-Yield One-Pager

Condensed by topic cluster — for pre-exam review, not first-pass learning.

History Quick Facts

  • First psych drug: Lithium (element #3); once in 7-Up.
  • 1887 amphetamine (Benzedrine, bronchodilator origin); 1898 heroin (cough suppressant origin).
  • Benzos discovered 1930s, not used for anxiety until 1960s.
  • 1950s = birth of psychopharmacology (phenothiazines, MAOIs, TCAs).
  • 1990s SSRIs broaden use; 2000s SNRIs/dual-acting agents.
  • Net effect: deinstitutionalization.

Practice & Commitment

  • ME: no state-run community MH system. NH: 10 county-run clinics. GA: hybrid.
  • ~95% managed outpatient (mostly by PCPs).
  • Involuntary hold → judge review within 72 hrs.
  • LH case: always confirm medication affordability before discharge — LAIs solve compliance.

Defense Mechanisms

  • Ego's unconscious protection from id/superego conflict.
  • ~26 total; only ~1 exam question — don't over-study.
  • Mature 4 (SASH): Suppression, Altruism, Sublimation, Humor.
  • Suppression = conscious; Repression = unconscious (common mix-up).

MSE / MMSE / MoCA

  • MSE = full written narrative, replaces/supplements physical exam.
  • MMSE = scored (0–30) cognitive subtest, part of the MSE.
  • MoCA = a specific MMSE-type instrument (visuospatial, executive, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, recall, orientation).
  • Biopsychosocial = Bio + Psych + Social all drive the formulation.

DSM-5-TR Essentials

  • 1952: 106 dx → now 365 dx (precision + billing).
  • Rule-out order: malingering/factitious → substance → medical → then primary psychiatric → adjustment vs. residual → no disorder.
  • Primary = one of the 365. Secondary = caused by something else (e.g. hypothyroid-induced depression).
  • Deep-memorize only: MDD, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder criteria.

DSM-IV Axis System (retired, still used clinically)

AxisCovers
IClinical disorders
IIPersonality d/o + intellectual disability
IIIGeneral medical conditions
IVPsychosocial/environmental problems
VGAF (0–100; most patients 50–80)

CYP450 Logic

  • Substrate = drug being metabolized.
  • Inhibitor → ↓ metabolism → ↑ substrate level → toxicity risk.
  • Inducer → ↑ metabolism → ↓ substrate level → efficacy loss/relapse risk.
  • Bupropion (2D6 inhibitor) + imipramine → anticholinergic delirium + arrhythmia.
  • Smoking (1A2 inducer) + clozapine/olanzapine → psychosis relapse despite adherence.

Drug Classes & Boards

  • Antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, anti-anxiety, anti-ADHD stimulants — class names are "largely historical," heavy cross-over use.
  • Benzos also stop active seizures (neuro crossover).
  • Know all 6 SSRIs and their differences — explicitly boards-testable.

Psychotherapy Modalities

  • Humanistic — Rogers — unconditional positive regard, reflection.
  • Psychodynamic — Freud — free association, dream analysis, transference.
  • Cognitive — Beck & Ellis — CBT, RET.
  • Behavioral — Skinner — systematic desensitization, exposure, flooding.
  • Biological — meds, ECT, psychosurgery. Other — group/family, Gestalt.

Barriers & Transference

  • Patient: somatic presentation, denial, stigma, fear meds change personality.
  • Doctor: time pressure, uncertainty, fear of treatment failure, own history.
  • Transference = patient → doctor. Countertransference = doctor → patient.
